Five major fields of science and math all arrived at the same shocking conclusion on their own: no system can save itself. In logic, Kurt Gödel proved in 1931 that any system powerful enough to do basic math cannot prove its own consistency—it can't show it's free of contradictions using only its own rules. In language theory, Alfred Tarski showed in 1936 that a system can't even define what "true" means for itself without creating a paradox like "this sentence is false." In computer science, Alan Turing proved the same year that no computer can fully predict whether any given program will run forever or stop. In physics, the Second Law of Thermodynamics says every closed system moves toward disorder—coffee cools, stars burn out. And Rolf Landauer showed in 1961 that even thinking has a physical cost: erasing information always releases heat into the environment.

These five proofs share one structure: every sufficiently complex closed system hits a wall. It cannot validate itself, define its own truth, predict its own behavior, maintain its own order, or process information without paying an irreversible price. None of these discoveries are controversial—every physicist and mathematician accepts them. But together they raise an unavoidable question: how has the universe maintained extraordinary order, truth, and consistent information for 13.8 billion years? The universe works. Mathematics holds. You're reading this. Yet five proven theorems say closed systems can't do any of that on their own.

Only three explanations exist. First, an infinite chain where each layer depends on another forever—but that means nothing is ever truly grounded, which contradicts what we observe. Second, a "brute fact" that the universe just happens to work for no reason—but that's just giving up on asking why. Third, something that grounds itself: a being that is its own consistency, truth, and order. Something necessary, eternal, and self-existent. These properties match exactly what classical philosophy calls God. The argument didn't start with religion—it started with five mathematical proofs, and the theology emerged from the math.
