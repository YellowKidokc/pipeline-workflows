When multiple different AI systems, working completely independently, reach the same conclusion about a document, that agreement counts as real evidence—not because someone forced them to agree, but because they arrived there on their own. This is the central argument of the paper. Most current research tries to make AI systems agree by having them debate each other or vote, but that approach is circular: if you design a system to produce agreement, you can't then point to that agreement as proof of anything. The paper calls this "engineered consensus" and argues it's fundamentally different from "unforced consensus," where agreement emerges naturally from independent analysis.

The proof-of-concept test involved four different AI systems—ChatGPT, two versions of Google's Gemini, and Perplexity—analyzing the U.S. Constitution separately, at different times, with different instructions. Three of the four systems independently concluded that the Constitution's core liberty protections have been heavily eroded, scoring between 1.0 and 3.4 out of 10. The fourth system scored much higher at 6.6, but the investigation revealed it was using a different standard: it measured whether the government's actions were legally permitted by courts, not whether they matched what the Constitution's authors intended. This disagreement turned out to be the most valuable finding, because it revealed the gap between what the law currently allows and what the original contract actually says.

The paper concludes that the real power of this method isn't just finding agreement—it's finding where and why systems disagree. Those divergences often reveal deeper truths that engineered consensus would have averaged away. This matters because it offers a way to use AI as a genuinely independent analytical tool rather than just a faster version of groupthink. The method is designed to be testable and falsifiable: anyone with a different AI system can run the same Constitution audit and either confirm or weaken the findings. That openness to being proven wrong is exactly what makes the approach scientific rather than just persuasive.
