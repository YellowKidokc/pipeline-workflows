Physicists have discovered that certain things in the universe can never change—energy, momentum, and electric charge are always conserved, meaning they can't be created or destroyed. These are called conservation laws, and they seem like the final, unbreakable floor beneath all of physics. But this article argues that physicists have stopped too soon. They never ask what holds that floor up.

The key idea comes from mathematician Emmy Noether, who proved in 1918 that every conservation law is actually the result of a deeper symmetry. Energy is conserved because the laws of physics don't change over time. Momentum is conserved because those laws don't change from place to place. But Noether's theorem only connects conservation to symmetry—it doesn't explain why the symmetries exist in the first place. Physics simply assumes them without explanation, like a floor that somehow floats in midair.

The article proposes that God is not a being who obeys conservation laws, but the reason conservation is possible at all. This isn't just a religious label slapped onto physics. It leads to three testable consequences that match what we observe. First, if God is pure order, then sin (disorder) can't directly touch God any more than matter can touch antimatter—they annihilate on contact. Second, this creates a problem: how does grace reach a messy, entropic world? The answer requires a mediator, just like quantum forces require particles like photons to carry interactions between charges. Third, this forces a Trinity: a source (the Father), a mediator (the Son), and a field that distributes the interaction everywhere (the Holy Spirit).

The article concludes that physics has been digging downward and finally hit something solid. The conservation laws aren't the bottom—they're held up by something deeper that is external, infinite, holy, and accessible only through a mediator. This matters because it claims that the structure of physics itself points toward a specific theological answer, not as a decoration but as the only way to explain why the floor holds.
