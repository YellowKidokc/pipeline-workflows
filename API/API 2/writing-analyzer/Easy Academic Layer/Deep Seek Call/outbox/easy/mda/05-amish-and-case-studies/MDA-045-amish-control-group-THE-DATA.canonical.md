# The Amish Experiment: What Happens When a Community Holds Together

## Abstract

Between 1960 and 2025, every measurable sign of American social health got worse. Families fell apart. Trust in institutions disappeared. Mental health got worse. Drug and alcohol abuse exploded. People stopped voting and joining clubs.

Every single indicator — in every group of people — in every part of the country.

Except one.

The Old Order Amish live inside the same country, follow the same federal laws, breathe the same air, and face the same cultural pressures. But they did not get worse. They got better. By every measure that matters — family stability, mental health, economic strength, community togetherness, population growth — they went the opposite direction from the country around them.

This paper treats the Amish not as a weird curiosity but as a **controlled experiment**. America is the test group. The Amish are the control group. The thing that's different between them is the binding force — call it authority, call it Ordnung (the unwritten Amish rules), call it χ (the Greek letter chi, used here as a math symbol for this binding force). America set _h_ → 0 (meaning they removed the external binding force) in 1968. The Amish never did.

The data that follows is not sentimental. The Amish have real, documented problems — genetic diseases from a small gene pool, not enough systems to report abuse, and education limits that make it hard for individuals to leave. Those problems are mentioned here because they make the argument _stronger_ , not weaker. A system does not need to be perfect to be structurally sound. It just needs to keep its binding force working.

## I. The Population Curve — Growing When Everyone Else Isn't

While the American birth rate dropped from 3.65 children per woman in 1960 to 1.62 in 2023 — below the level needed to replace the population for the first time in history — the Amish population went the opposite way.

The Amish population **doubled** about every 20–22 years. Since 2000 they have grown **131%** — from 177,910 to 410,955. In that same time, the U.S. birth rate fell below replacement level, the "deaths of despair" epidemic (deaths from suicide, drug overdose, and alcohol) killed over a million people, and the Census Bureau started predicting the U.S. population will shrink by mid-century.

This growth is not from immigration. Almost no one converts to become Amish. This is entirely from inside the community: large families (6–7 children on average) combined with very high rates of children staying in the community as adults.

About 40–45% of _new_ settlements eventually fail within 20 years (Luthy, 2009). This is not hidden. It is part of the system — the Amish let experimental communities form, test whether they work, and dissolve without treating failure as a disaster. The overall direction is still sharply upward.

## II. Retention — What Really Happens During Rumspringa

The popular story — that Amish teenagers taste freedom during Rumspringa (a period of less strict rules) and then run away — is factually wrong.

Period| Retention (Percent Who Stay)| Source
---|---|---
Pre-1950s| ~50–70% (varied a lot)| Meyers (1994)
1960s–1970s| ~70–75%| Hostetler (1993)
1980s–1990s| ~80–85%| Kraybill (2001)
2000s–2010s| ~85–95%| Kraybill, Johnson-Weiner, Nolt (2013)

Sources: Kraybill et al., _The Amish_ (2013); Stevick, _Growing Up Amish_ (2014).

Lancaster County runs 90–95% retention. The more conservative Swartzentruber Amish hold ~90%. Andy Weaver Amish report ~97%. Even the more progressive New Order Amish retain 60–70%.

**The trend is upward.** Retention has _increased_ over the same decades that every American institution — churches, civic groups, marriages, political parties — lost members. The Amish kept more of their young people as the surrounding culture lost more of theirs.

The most surprising finding: retention _improved_ after Amish parochial schools (private religious schools) replaced public schools (after the Supreme Court case _Wisconsin v. Yoder_ in 1972). Taking Amish children out of the public school system did not produce ignorant people who left. It produced committed members who chose to stay at rates their grandparents never reached.

Of the 10–15% who leave permanently, most leave _before_ baptism, which means they face no shunning (being cut off from the community). The system provides a way out without maximum punishment for those who choose it before being baptized.

In 1983, Janice Egeland and Andrea Hostetter published a major three-part study in the _American Journal of Psychiatry_ looking at mood disorders among 12,500 Old Order Amish in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania.

### Key Findings (Egeland & Hostetter, 1983)

  * Total active mental illness cases identified112 / 12,500
  * Overall rate of any mental illness~0.9%
  * Major depression rate (Amish)~0.33%

Source: _American Journal of Psychiatry_ 140 (1983): 56–61, 62–66, 67–71.

An important note about the method from Egeland: because alcohol abuse, drug abuse, and sociopathy (antisocial behavior) were "essentially absent" in the Amish community, diagnosis was _cleaner_ than in general population studies. These confusing factors — which make psychiatric research messy everywhere else — simply didn't exist.

### Suicide

  * Amish rate (Lancaster County, 1980)5.5 per 100,000 people
  * U.S. national average (same period)~13 per 100,000
  * U.S. national average (2022)~14.5 per 100,000


Source: Egeland data; CDC WISQARS.

The Amish suicide rate was **less than half** the national rate. The national rate has since _risen_. The Amish rate has not gone up.

### The Youth Mental Health Contrast

The most damning comparison is between generations. Between 2013 and 2023, the rate of depression among American teenagers rose from **8.2% to 13.1%** (CDC). Sixteen percent of U.S. teenagers aged 12–17 now have diagnosed anxiety. The Surgeon General declared loneliness a public health epidemic.

Amish teenagers — who have no smartphones, no social media, no Instagram, no TikTok, no algorithms designed to capture their attention — do not appear in these statistics. No careful study of Amish teenage mental health exists (a real gap in research), but **the fact that the technological causes of the American youth mental health crisis are simply absent is itself data**.

**Zero percent** of Amish men were obese. The U.S. male obesity rate has since climbed to over 40%. Amish men averaged over 18,000 steps per day — nearly **four times** the American average. This is not exercise. This is work. Farming, construction, carpentry. The physical demands of Amish life are the demands that human bodies evolved to handle.

### Asthma and Immune Function (Stein et al., 2016)

The _New England Journal of Medicine_ published a study comparing Amish farm children to Hutterite farm children — two genetically similar Anabaptist groups (Christian groups that baptize adults) with different farming practices.

  * Amish children asthma rate4 times lower
  * Allergic sensitization (allergies)6 times lower
  * Endotoxin (a substance from bacteria) in house dust6.8 times higher


Source: _NEJM_ 375 (2016): 411–421; Stein et al.

The Amish immune advantage comes from _dirt_. Their traditional farming practices expose children to many different microbes that modern cleanliness has eliminated. The immune system, which evolved for a world of dust and animals, malfunctions in sterile environments.

### Cardiovascular Protection (Pollin et al., 2008)

About 5% of Lancaster County Amish carry the R19X null mutation in the APOC3 gene (a specific genetic change). Carriers show significantly lower triglycerides (a type of fat in the blood), higher HDL cholesterol (the "good" cholesterol), and **65% lower odds (OR = 0.35) of coronary artery calcification (hardening of the arteries)** . No bad effects were observed.

Source: _Science_ 322 (2008): 1702–1705; Pollin et al., University of Maryland.

This is a genetic lottery win specific to the Amish founder population — a reminder that the same small gene pool that causes their higher rates of rare diseases also gave them protective genes. Biology is not a simple story about good and bad. It is a trade-off matrix.

The reasons are structural, not just cultural sayings:

Elders and experienced businesspeople evaluate ideas before they launch. Bad plans are redirected before money is spent.

2

### Zero Debt Startup Culture

Most Amish businesses start with personal savings and family loans, not bank debt. No borrowed money means no interest payments causing early failure.

3

### Built-In Customer Base

The Amish community itself provides the first customers, reducing the problem of starting from nothing.

4

### Low Overhead

No commercial health insurance premiums. No Social Security taxes (they've been exempt since 1965). Often no rent — workshops are built on family land.

5

### Failure Is Not Catastrophic

Mutual aid (community help) catches failed entrepreneurs. They don't face homelessness or bankruptcy.

### The Mutual Aid Economy

The Amish run a parallel economic system inside the United States:

The trade-off is real: 39% of Amish people surveyed reported "sometimes" avoiding recommended medical care because of cost. This is not a system without problems. But it is a system without medical bankruptcy — which destroys roughly 530,000 American families per year.

## VI. Criminal Justice — The Missing Data Point

**There are virtually no Amish people in American prisons.**

No formal per-person study exists comparing Amish incarceration to surrounding counties. This itself is significant — the population is large enough (410,955 people, roughly the size of Cleveland) that if they were incarcerated at the national rate (~0.7% of adults), there would be approximately **2,000 Amish inmates**. There are not.

Amish criminal violations tend to be regulatory: building code infractions, buggy safety equipment disputes, puppy mill violations in some settlements. Violent crime within Amish communities is extremely rare in the reported data. The church discipline system — confession before the congregation, temporary or permanent shunning (Meidung, being cut off from the community) — handles most interpersonal offenses internally. This is both a strength and a vulnerability, as discussed below.

Source: Young Center — "Deviance"; _Plain Anabaptist Journal_ — "The Crime Experiences of the Amish."

## VII. The Honest Problems

A case study that hides the flaws is propaganda, not analysis. The Amish system has documented structural failures.

### Genetic Disease

The founder effect — everyone descends from about 200 immigrants who came in the 1700s — concentrates recessive genetic disorders (diseases you get only if both parents carry the gene):

  * Maple syrup urine disease1 in 358 births vs 1 in 185,000
  * Glutaric aciduria type Ihigher than normal
  * Ellis-van Creveld syndromehigher than normal
  * Cohen syndrome / Crigler-Najjarhigher than normal


The Clinic for Special Children in Strasburg, PA (founded by Dr. Holmes Morton, a MacArthur Fellow) treats these conditions. It is funded largely by Amish community fundraisers — mutual aid applied to genetic inevitability.

### Sexual Abuse and Domestic Violence

This is the system's darkest failure. Investigative journalism (Reuters, 2020; Cosmopolitan, 2020) documented cases across multiple settlements where sexual abuse was handled through church confession rather than law enforcement. Structural factors that enable this:

  * Patriarchal authority structures (men hold the power)
  * Theology of forgiveness that pressures victims to reconcile
  * Cultural reluctance to involve "English" (non-Amish) law enforcement
  * Limited access to outside support services
  * No reliable data on how common it is because reporting rates are extremely low


**No system built on human authority is immune to the abuse of that authority.** The Amish binding force (external authority/Ordnung) constrains entropy (disorder) — but when the authority itself becomes the source of harm, the system's own design makes it hard to fix. This is the cost of strong boundaries: they keep the bad in as effectively as they keep the bad out. Some settlements have begun forming Amish Safety Committees. Progress is slow.

### Educational Ceiling

_Wisconsin v. Yoder_ (1972) gave the Amish legal permission to end formal education at 8th grade. The consequences:

  * Basic reading and writing skills are very high (~97%+)
  * Job skills are strong (construction, farming, small engine repair, furniture making)
  * Higher-level thinking skills, scientific literacy, and critical reasoning are **not formally developed**
  * People who leave the community face a big educational disadvantage in the broader economy


This is by design. The Amish are not failing to educate; they are choosing what to educate _for_. The trade-off is explicit: depth of community connection vs. breadth of individual ability. Justice Douglas's partial dissent in _Yoder_ — arguing that the children's own wishes should have been considered, not just the parents' — remains unresolved. The tension between community togetherness and individual freedom is real, and the Amish system resolves it in favor of the community.

## IX. The Structural Interpretation

The Amish are not better people. They are not smarter, kinder, or more virtuous than other Americans. They are a population embedded in a **structural system that maintains its binding force**.

In the language of the Theophysics framework:

### America after 1968

The external field (_h_) was set to zero. Authority was dismantled — in law (no-fault divorce), in money (paper money not backed by gold), in truth (post-truth media), in identity (expressive individualism, the idea that your feelings define who you are). The system went through a phase transition from ordered to disordered. Entropy (disorder) maximized. This is thermodynamically inevitable when you remove constraints from a complex system. Think of it like heat flowing from hot to cold — it only moves one direction, and you can't make it go backwards without spending energy from outside the system. That's thermodynamics, and it's the same principle as judgment: some things only go one way.

### The Amish (Always)

The external field (_h_) was maintained. The Ordnung is the field. It is not a law imposed from outside — it is an unwritten code maintained by agreement within each church district of 25–35 families. It is evaluated, updated, and enforced at the local level. Technologies are assessed by whether they strengthen or weaken the field. Those that weaken it are rejected. Those that strengthen or are neutral are adopted.

**The critical insight is not that the Amish reject modernity. They do not.** They use diesel generators, pneumatic tools, LED lighting, solar panels, GPS for farming, and increasingly, cell phones. What they reject is **any technology that shifts the focus of attention from the community to the individual**.

The car is banned not because it is complex, but because it removes the need to live near each other. The television is banned not because it is entertaining, but because it replaces active participation with passive watching. The smartphone is banned from the home not because it is useful (it is — they use them), but because it brings the infinite outside world into the small domestic space without any filter.

The Amish are engineering their own [coherence field](/glossary/?term=coherence-field "See glossary: coherence field") — a system where everything works together smoothly. They are doing it with the same physics that governs every other system: strong binding forces (mutual aid, living close together, shared language), well-defined boundaries (Ordnung, plain dress, choosing which technologies to use), and active rejection of entropy sources (media saturation, financial leverage, individual autonomy as the highest value). Think of it like a tuning fork — when you strike it near other objects, they start vibrating at the same frequency. One source of perfect pitch that brings everything else into harmony. That's coherence, and it's the same principle as Christ.

The data says this works. Not perfectly. Not without cost. But it works.

## X. The Experiment Is Still Running

America and the Amish started in the same place. They diverged at the same moment — the late 1960s. One set _h_ = 0 (removed the binding force). The other held _h_ constant (kept the binding force). Sixty years later, the results are not ambiguous.

The system that maintained its binding force — its external authority, its shared definitions, its constraints on individual entropy (disorder) — grew **1,367%** (from 28,000 to 410,955). Its members are less depressed, less obese, less isolated, less in debt, less likely to be in prison, and more likely to remain in their community than at any point in their history.

The system that removed its binding force — in law, in money, in truth, in family, in identity — faces population decline, a mental health crisis, institutional collapse, $35 trillion in debt, and a loneliness epidemic affecting a majority of its citizens.

The Amish did not need a government program. They did not need a platform. They did not need an algorithm. They needed the Ordnung — the field — and 25 families willing to enforce it on themselves.
