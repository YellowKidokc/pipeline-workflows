# The Numbers Behind the Amish Boom

The earlier articles in this series showed *why* the Amish are the control group: the Coherence Factory in Holmes County (09a), the head-to-head measurement against America (09b), and the Technological Filter that does the actual selection (09c). This article is the numbers underneath all of it.

In North America, the Amish are a strange and stubborn exception to every rule. While the rest of the U.S. and Canada have fewer babies, older workers, and less religion, the Amish are growing fast, keeping their young people, and piling up wealth. They are **shutting out modern life while becoming a bigger and bigger economic force within it**.

The data comes from the Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies, demographer Joseph Donnermeyer's long-term research, and quarterly reports from the Bank of Bird-in-Hand. This covers Old Order Amish and similar horse-and-buggy groups. It leaves out car-driving Beachy Amish and Amish Mennonites, whose numbers and money habits are very different.

A population that will likely pass one million people by mid-century, changing the basic makeup of the states where they live.

* * *

## 2.1 The Big Count

Counting the Amish is hard because the U.S. Census doesn't track religion, and the Amish don't fill out surveys like everyone else. So researchers use a bottom-up method: count church districts (congregations) and multiply by the average size, which is about 130 to 170 people (adults and kids together).

As of summer 2024, the Young Center put the total North American Amish population at **400,910** — a big milestone past 400,000. Projections for 2025 land between 404,575 and 410,955.

To see how fast this is, look at the past. In 1900 there were about 5,000–6,000 Amish in North America. By 2000 it was 177,910. In just the first 25 years of the 2000s, the population grew by about 233,000 — a **131%** increase. The growth isn't steady. It's compounding off a bigger and bigger base.

The Compound Annual Growth Rate (the average yearly growth over time) is about **3.5%**. By math, that doubles the population every generation.

## 2.2 Doubling Time

Throughout the 1900s and into the 2000s, the Amish doubling time has been about **20 to 22 years**. The U.S. general population — with fewer babies and relying on immigration — has a doubling time that's stretching toward a hundred years.

Joseph Donnermeyer, the top rural sociologist studying Amish numbers, found a 30-year doubling time in his long-term Holmes County study from 1965 to 2010. Older settlements grow slower. But for the whole country — including fast-growing new settlements — the 20-year mark is still the best way to predict the future.

**2025:** ~410,000
**2044 (projected):** ~747,000
**2050 (projected):** 1,000,000+

Apply a careful 22-year doubling assumption to the 2022 number: **~747,000 by 2044, crossing one million in the early 2050s**. Unless something terrible happens to retention or birth rates, this isn't a guess anymore. It's arithmetic.

## 2.3 Fertility — The Baby Engine

The Amish strictly forbid artificial birth control. They see children as a "heritage of the Lord." The Total Fertility Rate (TFR — the average number of babies per woman) runs **6.0 to 7.0**. The U.S. average has fallen below 2.1, which is the minimum needed to keep the population steady.

**Total Fertility Rate**
- U.S. average: < 2.1
- Amish (mainstream): 6.0 – 7.0
- Ultra-conservative affiliations: 9.0 – 10.0+

The hidden meaning in those numbers is huge. New Order Amish have smaller families. Ultra-conservative groups — Swartzentruber, Andy Weaver — have TFRs above 9.0. **Different birth rates mean the Amish population is becoming statistically more conservative over time**, because the strictest subgroups have the most babies. This isn't a stable balance. It's a slow drift toward greater strictness.

The result is a population pyramid (a graph showing ages) with an extremely wide base. A huge chunk of the Amish population is under 18. That momentum guarantees continued growth for decades, even if birth rates drop a little, because the number of women having babies keeps expanding.

## 3. How They Keep Their Kids

High birth rates supply potential growth. **Retention** — the share of Amish-born kids who choose to join the church as adults — supplies actual growth. Without high retention, no birth rate can keep up with people leaving.

The popular story of Amish teenagers running away from rules to enjoy the "English" world is statistically false. The overall retention rate is **85% or higher**. Nearly nine out of every ten kids born Amish end up choosing baptism, the Ordnung (the set of rules), and the community. Smartphones and the internet have not caused a mass exodus. *Rumspringa* — the teenage period of relative freedom — seems to work like a vaccine: young people see the world enough to make an informed choice, and they usually come back.

| Affiliation | Characteristics | Retention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Andy Weaver (Dan Church) | Ultra-conservative; strict shunning; minimal tech | ~97% |
| Old Order (mainstream) | Traditional; standard buggy; standard Ordnung | 85% – 90% |
| New Order Amish | Evangelical focus; higher tech; some outreach | 50% – 65% |

Strictness is a survival trait. The groups that demand the most sacrifice get the highest loyalty.

This is the "strict church" idea from sociology, backed up by the numbers. It also predicts something uncomfortable for the wider culture: the part of the Amish population growing fastest is also the part with the strictest separation, the most rigorous shunning, and the lowest tolerance for compromise.

## 4. Where They Live

The Amish are not spread out evenly. Two-thirds live in three states — Pennsylvania, Ohio, Indiana — but the population is moving, with explosive percentage growth in second-tier states and the western frontier.

In **Holmes County, Ohio** — the birthplace of the Coherence Factory from article 09a — the Amish are over 40% of the population. The "minority" is actually the main culture. New York's Amish population has more than quadrupled since 2000. Between 2020 and 2024, Nebraska saw a **64.8% increase** and Colorado a **56.9% increase**. These western settlements are usually small and conservative — pioneers escaping eastern crowding and high land prices.

The reasons for moving are predictable and worth naming because they show up in every settlement directory: good farmland at fair prices, nearby non-farm jobs, rural isolation, laws friendly to the Amish, being close to family, and solving church conflicts by physically moving away.

## 5. From Plows to Profits

The picture of an Amish man behind a plow is increasingly wrong. In Lancaster and Holmes Counties, there are so many people that owning enough land for everyone is mathematically impossible — there just isn't enough acreage. So the Amish switched to small businesses and home-based industries. The switch has been amazing.

**Amish Small Business:** ~95% five-year survival rate
**U.S. National Average:** ~50% five-year survival rate

The structural advantages pile up:

**1. Low Overhead**
Operations run from barns and sheds. No electric bills from the grid. Family labor. No expensive insurance or benefits packages.

**2. Amplified Work Ethic**
The culture hates laziness. That translates directly into how much work gets done per hour.

**3. Extreme Networking**
In Holmes County, economists see a strange thing: competitors actively help each other. A booked roofer hands the job to his cousin. A furniture maker borrows lumber from his neighbor. Cooperation lowers the risk of bankruptcy for the whole network.

**4. Niche Brand Pricing**
High-quality, labor-heavy goods (heirloom furniture, quilts, timber framing) where the "Amish" brand gets a higher price.

The result is the rise of the **"Amish Millionaire"** — that's no longer a contradiction. In Holmes County and Elkhart-LaGrange, manufacturing operations making RV parts, cabinets, or portable storage sheds bring in more than **$10 million a year**. Between 2005 and 2019, the average household income for 27-year-olds raised in lower-income Holmes County homes rose 24% — upward mobility beating nearly every other rural county in the United States.

## 6. The Bank of Bird-in-Hand

The clearest proof of Amish wealth is one regulated bank: the **Bank of Bird-in-Hand (BBIH)** , started in 2013 in the heart of Lancaster County. It was the first U.S. bank created specifically to serve the Plain community. Its quarterly reports give a rare, audited look at the money in an otherwise hidden economy.

| Metric | Dec 2022 | Dec 2023 | Sep 2024 | Dec 2024 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Total Assets | ~$1.10B | $1.363B | $1.578B | $1.592B |
| Total Deposits | ~$893M | $1.089B | $1.307B | $1.328B |
| Net Loans | — | $1.182B | $1.333B | $1.379B |

Asset growth of nearly **$500 million in two years** (2022–2024). Shareholders' equity (the value owned by shareholders) was $147 million in late 2024 — and the bank is mostly owned by the community it serves. To help customers who travel by horse and buggy, BBIH runs a fleet of mobile branches called **GELT Buses** (a play on the Yiddish word for money) that drive to rural crossroads to handle deposits and withdrawals.

The shift from informal mutual-aid lending to formal balance-sheet credit means modern Amish business has outgrown what a traditional church aid fund can finance. The amount of money involved has reached the level where formal banking is not optional.

## 7. The New Inequality

The idea of the Amish as a flat, equal society is being challenged by the data. The shift from farming (where land limits how much you can have) to business (where size can be unlimited) has created serious economic gaps.

A major early study by Moledina and McConnell on wealth distribution in Holmes County found clearly: **economic differences are growing**. Wealth is no longer spread out evenly. A class of wealthy business owners and church leaders — often the established landowners — controls a huge share of community assets, especially land. Amish farmers in the study owned an average of 56 acres, and church leadership was linked to higher land values, suggesting a mix of spiritual authority and economic standing.

Strangely, the same study found that a large number of Amish people fall below the national poverty line by reported cash income. Some of that statistical poverty is misleading — big families spread income thin, and Amish families grow much of their own food, so they need less cash. But it also points to a real group: the **"Lunch Pail" Amish**, men with no land and no business who must work hourly wages in factories. They are vulnerable to economic downturns in a way that landed farmers are not — a new working class within the Plain community.

## 8. Three Regional Economies

**Lancaster County, PA — The Agritourism Engine**
In 2024, tourism driven by the Amish brand brought in **$2.7 billion** in direct visitor spending and supported over 26,000 jobs. The county gets over 10 million visitors a year. The Amish have mixed feelings about tourism but make money from it through roadside stands, quilt shops, and furniture showrooms. High land prices from tourism force Lancaster Amish to be super-productive on small farms — tobacco, dairy, intensive produce.

**Holmes County, OH — The Manufacturing Powerhouse**
Less tourism, more production. Between **450 and 500 wood shops** are in the county. Lumber and furniture contribute hundreds of millions to a Gross Regional Product (the total value of goods and services) over $2.3 billion. The extreme-networking culture let Holmes recover faster than nearby regions from both the 2008 crash and COVID-19, quickly switching to making masks and desks during the lockdowns.

**LaGrange / Elkhart, IN — The RV Belt**
This settlement is tied directly to the Recreational Vehicle industry. A huge percentage of Amish men work in RV factories. Wages are high, but the work is secular and goes up and down with the national economy. During the 2008 crash, unemployment skyrocketed; during the COVID RV boom, wages soared. This reliance on "English" corporations sets Indiana Amish apart from the more independent entrepreneurs of Ohio and Pennsylvania.

## 9. Future Outlook

The trends carry forward mechanically. By 2050 the Amish are expected to cross the one-million mark. They will change from a fringe group into a major minority in states like Ohio and Pennsylvania, with the power to alter the political landscape of rural districts.

The main limit on this growth is physical: **land**. Relying on horses limits how far any settlement can spread. As core settlements like Lancaster and Holmes fill up, three pressures will get stronger:

- **Accelerated Westward Migration** — More settlements in Colorado, Wyoming, Nebraska.
- **Subdivision of Farms** — Continued shrinking of farms pushes more men into non-farm work.
- **Increased Friction** — More people means more conflicts with local zoning boards over sanitation, schooling, and road maintenance.

The 2024–2025 statistics confirm what the rest of this series has been arguing. The Amish are not a relic. They are a dynamic, growing, and increasingly wealthy sub-society — doubling every generation, surviving in business at rates that embarrass the corporate sector, and piling up billions in capital. The success is creating new problems: the gap between the Amish Millionaire and the Lunch Pail laborer is widening, and the pressure on the land is speeding up. But the headline number is clear.

For now — and that "for now" should be heard against the whole arc of this series — the numbers tell a story of extraordinary vitality.
