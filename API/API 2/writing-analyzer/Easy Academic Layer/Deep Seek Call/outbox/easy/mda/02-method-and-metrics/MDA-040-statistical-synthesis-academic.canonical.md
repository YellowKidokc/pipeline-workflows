# The Arc of Decline: 125 Years in Charts

This paper tracks how American society has changed across five generations. We use the Lowe family as a real-life example: Samuel (born 1900), Henry (1926), William (1950), Thomas (1974), and Jacob (1998 and still going in 2025). Each generation inherited a world shaped by the choices of the generation before them. Each generation had more freedom—from staying in one place, from marriage, from church, from debt limits—but the costs of that freedom weren't obvious at the time.

The evidence is organized into three big categories: **Family Structure** (how families stay together or fall apart), **Social Coherence** (how much people trust institutions, go to church, and join groups), and **Economic Stability** (the money and jobs that make long-term planning possible).

## 1900: Samuel Lowe — The Baseline, Before Everything Changed

In 1900, America was a world of small, tight-knit communities. The population was 76 million people spread across 3.5 million square miles. Most people lived and died within 50 miles of where they were born. There was no radio, no rural phones, no movies. The only voices you heard were the voices of people standing right next to you. The only faces that mattered were faces you knew in person.

This wasn't experienced as a problem. It was just normal life before technology collapsed distance. The social world was small but deeply connected—what sociologist Émile Durkheim called "mechanical solidarity." You couldn't compare your life to strangers' lives because you didn't know what strangers' lives looked like. That meant you couldn't feel dissatisfied by what you didn't have.

Time moved slowly too. Farming, animal care, and mail delivery all followed natural rhythms you couldn't speed up. When you're forced to wait, learning patience isn't a virtue—it's survival. Marriage lasted because leaving was socially impossible and there was nowhere else to go. Almost everyone went to church because churches were the main way communities formed, helped each other, and passed down values.

### Family Structure

- **Divorce Rate:** 0.7 per 1,000 people (baseline)
- **Marriage Age:** 26 for men, 22 for women (baseline)
- **Cohabitation:** ~0% (baseline)

### Society & Faith

- **Church Attendance:** ~95% (near universal)

### Technology & Connection

- **Households with Phone:** <1% (baseline)
- **Households with Electricity:** <3% (baseline)

## 1926: Henry Lowe — The Builder, First Cracks Appear

By 1926, the U.S. population had grown to 117 million. Cities were exploding: New York had over 6 million people, Chicago over 3 million. Henry Ford's Model T put about 20 million cars on American roads. Travel times shrank dramatically. The old limits on how far you could go were starting to dissolve.

But the bigger change wasn't physical—it was informational. Broadcast radio arrived. When Henry bought a Radiola in 1924, for the first time in history, voices of strangers from far away entered the family home. The information bubble that Samuel lived in was now cracked. You could hear New York voices, Chicago orchestras. You could compare your life to other lives.

At first, radio was communal—the family gathered for about an hour each evening. The old constraints were still mostly working. But the mechanism that would eventually destroy them had been introduced. The silence that forced families to talk to each other was starting to shrink.

### Family Structure

- **Divorce Rate:** 1.6 per 1,000 (+129% from 1900)
- **Marriage Age:** 24 for men, 21 for women (stable)
- **Cohabitation:** ~0% (still rare)

### Society & Faith

- **Church Attendance (Rural):** ~90% (still universal)

### Technology & Connection

- **Population:** 117 million (+54% from 1900)
- **Automobiles:** 20 million (from 8,000 in 1900)
- **Media Hours/Day:** ~1 (first intrusion)

## 1950: William Lowe — Peak Coherence, The Golden Moment

America in 1950 was the most powerful country in world history. World War II was over, and the U.S. made about 50% of the world's manufactured goods. The GI Bill sent about 8 million veterans to college. Suburbs exploded with identical houses and young families. This was the peak—not of technology or wealth, but of what this paper calls the **Coherence Factor**: the combined measure of family stability, trust in institutions, shared values, and community involvement.

Weekly church attendance hit 55%—the highest it's ever been in America (Gallup polls). The divorce rate had gone up from 1900 but then stabilized. People married younger because they were optimistic about the future. The birth rate hit 3.7 children per woman in 1957—the peak of the Baby Boom. The middle class grew faster than ever before, thanks to the GI Bill, union wages, and factory jobs.

Television went from 9% of households in 1950 to 87% in 1960—the fastest technology adoption in American history (Putnam, 2000). But in 1950, TV was still communal: one screen per household, watched together. The scarcity of content—only one channel, shared attention—kept families together. The mechanism that would later fragment everything was already working, but nobody could see it yet.

### Family Structure

- **Divorce Rate:** 2.6 per 1,000 (+271% from 1900)
- **Marriage Age:** 23 for men, 20 for women (younger—optimism)

### Society & Faith

- **Church Attendance:** 55% (PEAK—historical high)
- **"No Religion":** 2% (near zero)
- **Median Income (2024$):** $42K (30% above 1940)

### Technology & Connection

- **TV Hours/Day (if owned):** 4.5 (new environment)

## 1974: Thomas Lowe — The Breaking, Everything Comes Apart

The 1950s consensus shattered across multiple fronts at once. Change happened so fast that society couldn't keep up. The system had crossed a critical threshold. The old stability was gone forever.

The Vietnam War ended in humiliating defeat, with 58,220 American soldiers dead. President Nixon resigned in August 1974—the first president ever to quit. Watergate proved that the government had lied to the public at the highest level. Trust in the federal government collapsed from 77% (1964) to 36% (1974)—a 53-point drop in one decade. That's the steepest trust collapse ever recorded in American surveys.

The birth control pill changed everything. By the late 1960s, about 10 million women were using oral contraceptives. For the first time in human history, sex and pregnancy were no longer automatically linked. The biological rule that had governed human sexuality across every civilization—sex usually leads to babies—was removed at scale. The natural enforcement mechanism for marriage was gone.

Then the legal enforcement mechanism went too. California's Family Law Act (1969) introduced no-fault divorce: you no longer needed to prove your spouse did something wrong to end the marriage. Most states followed within five years. Marriage was now terminable by either spouse, for any reason, at any time.

### Family Structure

- **Divorce Rate:** 4.6 per 1,000 (+557% from 1900)
- **Marriage Age:** 24 for men, 22 for women (+117% from 1950)

### Society & Faith

- **Church Attendance:** 40% (-27% from peak)
- **Trust in Government:** 36% (-53% from peak)
- **Violent Crime (per 100k):** 461 (+187% from 1960)
- **Murder Rate (per 100k):** 9.8 (+93% from 1960)

### Economic Reality

- **Consumer Credit:** $197 billion (debt rising)

### Technology Saturation

- **TV Hours/Day:** 6+ (always on)
- **Homes with TV:** 98% (universal)
- **Cable:** emerging (fragmentation begins)

## 1998: Jacob Lowe — The Aftermath, Rich, Comfortable, Untethered

America in 1998 had more money than ever but was falling apart in ways that mattered more. The Cold War was over—the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991. Unemployment was 4.5%. The federal budget had its first surplus since 1969. The stock market was in its longest bull run ever. President Clinton was impeached for lying about an affair, but his approval ratings barely budged. The public no longer cared if a leader's personal life was a mess—they judged institutional performance separately from personal character.

The internet had reached 26% of U.S. households. AOL, Yahoo, and early e-commerce were the face of it. The path to near-universal adoption was clear but not yet obvious. The structural implication: information scarcity was dead. Any question could be answered instantly. Any desire could find resources to fulfill it. The friction that had once disciplined human behavior was being systematically removed.

At the same time, geographic limits on friendship dissolved. You could talk to anyone, anywhere, instantly and for free. But removing the scarcity of connection didn't create deeper relationships. It created breadth without depth—what sociologist Robert Putnam calls "thin" versus "thick" social capital. The old anchors of identity—"where you're from"—were gone, replaced by the screen as the only consistent feature of daily life.

Jacob's parents divorced when he was five. That meant moving around, unstable housing, and the social disruption that defined his generation.

### Family Structure

- **Divorce Rate:** 4.0 per 1,000 (+471% from 1900)
- **Marriage Age:** 27 for men, 25 for women (delayed or skipped)
- **Cohabiting Couples:** 5.5 million (10x from 1970)
- **Adults Living Alone:** 25 million (from 4 million in 1950)

### Society & Faith

- **Church Attendance:** 35% (-36% from peak)
- **"No Religion":** 14% (from 2% in 1950)
- **Violent Crime (per 100k):** 566 (+252% from 1960)
- **Median Income (2024$):** $70K (prosperity)

### Economic Reality

- **Trust in Government:** 38% (-51% from 1974)
- **Consumer Credit:** $1.37 trillion (7x from 1974)
- **Federal Debt:** $5.6 trillion (12x from 1974)

### Technology Saturation

- **Cable Channels:** 50+ (infinite choice)

## 2025: Jacob Lowe — The Choice, Connected, Isolated, Searching

The loneliness epidemic is now officially a public health crisis. The U.S. Surgeon General's report (2023) says social isolation is as deadly as smoking 15 cigarettes a day. That finding comes from a massive study by Holt-Lunstad et al. (2015; N = 3.4 million people). Social isolation raises your risk of early death by 26% (HR = 1.26, 95% CI [1.23, 1.29]).

The American Perspectives Survey (2021) found that 12% of Americans have zero close friends—up from 3% in 1990. Marriage rates are at historic lows. Fertility has dropped below the replacement level. Membership in clubs and community groups has kept falling, just as Putnam predicted. Average daily screen time exceeds 7.5 hours (Nielsen, 2023)—more than sleep and more than face-to-face interaction combined.

The information environment has flipped completely. In 1900, you heard zero outside voices per day. In 2025, you encounter about 10,000 algorithmically curated pieces of content per day. We went from silence to supersaturation.

Despite—or because of—ubiquitous digital connection, isolation has gotten worse. This paradox makes sense within the coherence framework: connection quantity does not substitute for connection depth; presence without commitment does not generate the trust and shared meaning that constitute social coherence.

Artificial intelligence has entered intimate relationships. AI-generated dating messages are common. AI companion apps are a billion-dollar market. Dating app usage is declining—not because people are pairing up again, but because the odds of success are so low that sustained effort no longer feels rational.

Total fertility has fallen to 1.6 children per woman—below the replacement threshold of 2.1, and the lowest in American history. South Korea's fertility rate is 0.72 (2023). That's a warning sign: it shows where post-coherence societies are heading. Population shrinks unless immigration makes up the difference.

The psychological picture matches what the coherence framework predicts. Populations operating below the critical threshold show elevated rates of meaninglessness, isolation, and despair. That's exactly what you'd expect when the structural conditions for human flourishing have been systematically removed.

- **Marriage Rate:** 6.0 per 1,000 (-54% from 1900 | HISTORIC LOW)
- **Adults Living Alone:** 37 million (from 4 million in 1950)
- **Couples Met Online:** 65%+ (from 0% in 1990)

### Society & Faith

- **Church Attendance:** 30% (-45% from peak)
- **"No Religion":** 28% (from 2% in 1950)
- **Close Friends (zero):** 12% (from 3% in 1990)
- **Trust in Government:** <20% (-74% from peak)

### Technology Saturation

- **AI Companion Users:** 30 million+ (replacing humans)
- **Screen Time:** 7.5+ hours/day (no escape)
