# The John Templeton Foundation has spent fifty years asking how science and religion can talk to each other. The papers are good. The conversation is thin. The gap has not closed. **Here is why: every attempt starts from a broken starting point.**

## The 80/20 Split Nobody Voted For

Science owns eighty percent of the public room. Logic. Mathematics. Prediction. Technology. Medicine. Policy. The definition of what counts as knowledge. Christianity owns the other twenty. Sunday morning. Personal devotion. Private morality. The polite acknowledgment that faith is meaningful to those who hold it — as long as it stays in its own lane.

This arrangement feels earned. It feels like the verdict of four centuries of scientific progress beating religious dogma. It is not. It is a verdict given by one side after it quietly took the other side's foundation and did not say so.

The 80% science owns only works because the 20% is true. That is the structural contradiction nobody names.

Before science takes its first measurement, it must borrow ten things it cannot prove with its own method. That the external world exists. That it is orderly and knowable. That logic works. That human thinking reliably tracks reality. That mathematics — an abstract invention — actually describes physical structure. That nature behaves consistently across time. That truth exists. That honesty matters. That simpler explanations are better. That the universe is understandable to minds at all.

Ten assumptions. Zero provable by experiment. Every one required before the experiment can be designed. These assumptions do not describe a neutral universe. They describe a universe that is rational, ordered, knowable, and accessible to minds. They describe a universe that theology has been describing for three thousand years. Science did not build that foundation. It borrowed it. Then it used the borrowed foundation to declare the lender out of bounds.

That is not a settlement. That is a structural contradiction. And it is the reason fifty years of Templeton funding has produced conversation without resolution. You cannot have genuine conversation between two parties when one party is operating on premises it took from the other without acknowledgment.

## Hold Both Sides to the Same Standard

The standard move in science-religion discussion is to ask whether religious claims survive scientific testing. That is one direction. It is half an audit. The two-way audit runs the other half too.

Hold God accountable to science's own standards — falsifiability (can the claim be proven wrong?), predictive power (does it predict things we can test?), explanatory scope (how much does it explain?), internal consistency (does it contradict itself?), reproducibility (can we get the same result again?). Run the full pipeline. Do not protect theological claims from pressure.

Then hold science accountable to the same standard. Can it ground its own starting assumptions? Can it explain consciousness — not just map which brain parts light up, but explain why there is something it *feels like* to be a brain? Can it explain why abstract mathematics describes physical reality with precision that borders on the absurd? Can it account for the fine-tuning of cosmological constants (the exact settings of the universe's basic forces)? Can it explain where information comes from, not just how it is processed once it exists?
