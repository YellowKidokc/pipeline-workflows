# The Death of Sacred Words: How Our Language About Virtue Has Changed

## Introduction: A Measurable Drift in the Language of Virtue

The "Fruits of the Spirit" are nine virtues from the Bible (Galatians 5:22–23): **Love, Joy, Peace, Patience, Kindness, Goodness, Faithfulness, Gentleness,** and **Self-Control**. For a long time, these were seen as the building blocks of good character. This study looks at how the use and meaning of these words have changed over the last 100 years. We track two things: (1) **How often these words appear in books (Google Ngrams, 1900–2025)** , and (2) **How dictionaries defined them in 1900 vs. 2025**. We want to see if there's a real, measurable drop in how much we use this moral and spiritual language, and a rise in self-focused words like *trauma, validation, empowerment, self-care,* and *authenticity*. We also check if the definitions have changed — a kind of "semantic entropy" (meaning decay) where old virtues lose their sharpness and cultural power. Finally, we ask what these language trends say about bigger changes in our culture and how we stick together as a society.

*A quick note:* All Google Ngram findings come from the English Google Books collection. Word frequency is measured as a share of all words each year, which automatically adjusts for the fact that more books are published now. But we're careful about biases — like more science books being published, which can make moral words seem rarer than they really are.

## Section One: Google Ngram Frequency Analysis (1900–2025)

### 1.1 How We Did the Study and Controlled for Bias

We used Google's Ngram Viewer to track how often each virtue word appeared each year from 1900 to about 2019. To avoid problems from digitization errors, we looked at **relative frequencies** (how many times a word appears per million words) instead of raw counts, and we smoothed the data over 3-year periods to reduce random noise. We also kept in mind that the Google Books collection after 1900 includes more and more scientific and technical writing, which naturally uses fewer moral or religious words. This means that **a general downward trend** for many common words in the 20th century is partly just because the types of books changed.

One study found that about 80% of a random sample of English words appeared less often in the late 1900s than in the early 1900s. So we focus more on **comparisons** — like virtue words vs. self-help words — than on the absolute drop of any single word. We also checked the **English Fiction** subset to make sure the trends weren't just from academic texts.

### 1.2 The Decline of Virtue Words

With only a few exceptions, the nine "Fruit of the Spirit" words show **flat or falling usage** over the century, especially from the mid-1900s onward. This suggests that our culture puts less emphasis on explicitly spiritual or moral vocabulary.

**Virtue Words — Declining**

↓Kindness — down 56%
↓Modesty — down 52%
↓Gentleness — steady decline
↓Faithfulness — rarely used outside religious contexts
↓Patience — declining
↓Love — subtle decline after 1950
↓Peace — spikes in 1918, 1945, 1960s; otherwise declining

**Therapeutic Words — Rising**

↑Trauma — skyrocketed after 1970
↑Empowerment — exploded in the 1990s–2000s
↑Self-care — steep climb in the 2010s
↑Authenticity — growing since 1970s
↑Validation — uptick from the 1980s
↑"Me" (pronoun) — up 87% from 1975 to 2007
↑Self-control — rose (a secular version of *temperance*)

**Overall Decline:** A 2012 study of 50 morality-related words found that 74% of them declined in frequency over the 20th century. Common Christian virtue words like *grace, mercy, wisdom,* and *faith* all showed clear downturns. Words for the "fruit of the Spirit" virtues — *love, patience, gentleness, faithfulness* — **became much rarer in printed books**. For example, "compassion words, like **kindness**, dropped by 56%," and "humility words, like **modesty**, fell by 52%." The word **gentleness** also shrank, as did **faithfulness**, which by the 21st century is rarely used outside religious or old-fashioned contexts. Even **patience** — once praised as a virtue — appears less often now than in our great-grandparents' time.

**Case Study — "Love":** **Love** is an interesting case. It stays one of the most common English nouns throughout the whole period, but even *love* saw a small decline in relative frequency after the mid-1900s. Earlier in the 1900s, books (especially fiction and poetry) talked about love a lot; later decades, with more scientific and informational books in the mix, its share got diluted. David Brooks notes that even basic moral words like *love* have faded from public conversation, partly because of a broader cultural shift away from sacred language. (It's worth noting that *love* in non-religious contexts also carries many non-virtuous meanings, but here we're tracking overall usage.)

**Wartime Peaks — "Peace":** The word **peace** broke the overall trend at certain moments — its frequency spiked around 1918 and 1945 (the ends of World Wars) and again in the 1960s (the "Peace" movement) as world events drove conversation. But outside these surges, *peace* declined in general use through the late 1900s, reflecting less frequent religious or philosophical discussion of "inner peace" or peace as a virtue, compared to a more practical political use (like "peace treaty"). By the 2000s, *peace* appears less often proportionally than in 1900.

**Stable or Mixed Trends:** **Joy** and **goodness** show relatively flatter trends (small declines). *Goodness* as an exclamation ("Goodness gracious!") kept it in use, though as a mild expression rather than a moral term. **Self-control**, interestingly, is one "fruit" that **did not decline** — in fact, it **increased** in usage over time. Research shows *self-control* (as a concept in psychology and self-help) rose steadily across the 20th century. This is the opposite of its biblical synonym *temperance*, which sharply declined after the early 1900s (following the temperance movement and Prohibition era). Basically, *temperance* (with its moral/religious overtones) fell out of favor, while the more secular term *self-control* gained popularity in discussions of personal development and behavior. This **exception** shows that not all virtue language vanished — some was **reframed in secular terms**.

### 1.3 The Rise of Therapeutic and Self-Help Words

In stark contrast to the shrinking of traditional virtues, our analysis finds that **therapeutic, psychological, and self-focused words have surged** in usage since the mid-1900s. We compared the "fruits" with several modern terms: **Trauma, Validation, Empowerment, Self-Care,** and **Authenticity**. The results strongly support the idea of a language shift.

**Therapy and Trauma:** The word **trauma** (in the psychological sense) was almost absent in 1900s books outside medical contexts. It started growing modestly in the mid-1900s, then skyrocketed from the 1970s to today. By the 2010s, *trauma* is a common word in books on psychology, self-help, social sciences, and even popular media. This shows how talking about personal psychological wounds has become mainstream. Some critics note that *trauma* has been **conceptually broadened** in modern usage to cover almost any hardship or emotional wound. The rising curve of *trauma* is like a mirror image of the falling curve of virtues like *patience* or *forgiveness*.

**Empowerment and Self-Care:** The word **empowerment** barely appeared in books before 1970. It gained traction with civil rights and feminist movements, and exploded in the 1990s–2000s as the idea of personal empowerment and agency became popular. **Self-care** (meaning personal well-being practices) is another late 20th-century creation — its frequency climbed steeply in the 2010s with the wellness movement. These words emphasize **individual improvement and validation** over community or spiritual virtues. The data suggest that where a 19th-century author might have urged *"charity"* or *"patience,"* a 21st-century author is more likely to speak of *"self-care"* and *"empowerment."*

**Authenticity and Validation:** **Authenticity**, meaning *genuineness* or *being true to yourself*, has existed in philosophy for a long time (like in existentialist writings), but its popular usage grew in the late 1900s. In recent decades *authenticity* is a buzzword in self-help (encouraging people to "live authentically"). Its frequency in books climbed accordingly. **Validation**, in the sense of affirming someone's feelings or identity, also rose in usage. While *validation* as a general word existed (like validating a parking ticket), the **emotional/social meaning** ("seeking validation," "validate someone's experience") is mostly a modern development. Google Books shows a noticeable uptick in *validation* from the 1980s onward, matching the rise of pop psychology and relationship vocabulary.

**Secular Replacements:** It appears that as explicit faith-based words (*sin, virtue, soul*, etc.) receded, **secular terms filled the gap** in conversations about character and well-being. For example, instead of speaking of *meekness* or *humility*, today's books stress *self-esteem* and *confidence*. Instead of *long-suffering* or *forbearance*, we hear *resilience*. This is backed up by sociological language analyses: One study of U.S. State of the Union addresses from 1790 to 2012 found a decline in "other-oriented" words (like *neighbor*) and a rise in self-focused words (*I, me, mine*) over time. Likewise, a Google Ngram of pronouns shows that while *"me"* declined in usage in the mid-1900s, it surged by **87%** in frequency from 1975 to 2007 — evidence of a growing **"Cult of Self"** in our language (as one psychologist calls it). In parallel, words like *humility* and *modesty* became rarer, while *self-expression* and *self-worth* became common.

### 1.4 Summary of Ngram Findings

In summary, the **last 100+ years have seen a measurable decline in the language of traditional virtue, alongside a rise in the language of psychology and self-focus**. We adjusted for the overall growth in book publishing, so this isn't just because there are more books — it reflects a real shift in what we pay attention to. To be clear, people didn't stop experiencing love or valuing kindness; rather, they **talk (and write) about them less** in explicit terms, especially outside of religious contexts. Meanwhile, discussions of personal feelings, mental health, and individual empowerment have exploded. This supports the idea of a long-term drift from a **moral/spiritual vocabulary** to a **therapeutic/self-focused vocabulary**.

Some researchers see this as a sign of *"lexical softening"* or **semantic drift** in our culture's vocabulary of character. The concept of **Semantic Entropy** might apply here: as classic virtue words lose frequency and clarity, the shared moral meaning in a culture could be "dissipating" or breaking apart. In the next section, we explore whether the *meaning* of these words has also changed over time, or just how often we use them.

## Section Two: How Dictionary Definitions Changed (1900 vs. 2025)

How have the dictionary definitions of the nine "fruit" terms changed, if at all, from the early 1900s to today? To find out, we compared entries from **historical dictionaries** (like Webster's 1913 Unabridged, and earlier Webster's 1828 where helpful) with **current** definitions from Merriam-Webster (2025 edition) and the Oxford English Dictionary (OED). We looked for shifts in meaning, tone, and emphasis — for example, have these virtues been *redefined, broadened, narrowed,* or given a different focus over time?

Overall, we found that the **core literal meanings stay remarkably stable** — *love is still love, patience still patience* — but there are **notable shifts in emphasis and framing**:

**From Moral Virtues to Personal Traits:** Early-1900s dictionaries often defined these words in explicitly moral or religious terms, sometimes with flowery or preachy language. Modern definitions tend to be more **concise, secular, and neutral in value**. In older editions, you see words like "virtue," "uprightness," "righteous," or even references to God or scripture in the definition. Today's definitions usually avoid obvious value judgments, describing the term in plain behavioral terms. The **tone** has moved from *ideological* to *descriptive*.

**Loss of Prescriptive Detail:** Older definitions often included an explanation of *why* the trait is good or situations that show it. Newer definitions assume users know the virtue is positive and focus on *what* it is. For example, Webster's 1828 defined **Kindness** as "Good will; benevolence; that temper or disposition which delights in contributing to the happiness of others." This not only defines kindness but promotes a worldview of finding joy in helping others. Merriam-Webster (2025) defines *kindness* simply as "the quality of being kind; a kind act" — a much barer definition that uses *kind* to define itself, leaving out the moral elaboration. The charitable dimension (benevolence, delighting in others' happiness) is left implied or pushed to examples.

**Broadening and Semantic Dilution:** Some virtues have gained broader, less intense meanings in everyday use, which dictionaries have added. **Love**, for instance, historically meant deep affection or devotion (with hints of duty and sacrifice). Earlier dictionaries struggled to define *love* — Samuel Johnson even dodged it by quoting others. By 1913, Webster's Secondary School Dictionary defined *love* as "a feeling of strong personal attachment; ardent affection; (also) desire for, and earnest effort to promote the welfare of another." This definition notably includes *unselfish concern for another's good* as part of love. In contrast, Merriam-Webster's current definition puts the romantic/family sense first: "strong affection for another arising out of kinship or personal ties," and "attraction based on sexual desire," with altruistic *"unselfish loyal concern for others"* appearing later as a subtype. The *ordering* of meanings has flipped: what 100 years ago might have been the primary essence of love (devotion and welfare of others) is now a secondary or tertiary sense, while emotional attraction is primary. This suggests an **ideological reframing** — love is framed less as virtuous commitment and more as personal feeling.

### Then and Now: A Definitional Contrast

**Webster's 1913 — Love**
> "A feeling of strong personal attachment; ardent affection; also desire for, and earnest effort to promote the welfare of another."
*Altruistic concern included as part of love*

**Merriam-Webster 2025 — Love**
> "Strong affection for another arising out of kinship or personal ties; attraction based on sexual desire; affection based on admiration or common interests."
*Love primarily as emotional affection; selfless concern appears later as "loyal benevolent concern," not central*

**Webster's 1913 — Patience**
> "The power of suffering with fortitude; uncomplaining endurance of evils or wrongs, as toil, pain, poverty, insult, oppression, calamity, etc."
*Emphasis on enduring hardship virtuously*

**Merriam-Webster 2025 — Patience**
> "The ability to accept or tolerate delay or trouble calmly without getting angry or upset."
*Emphasis on staying calm, with "suffering" toned down to any annoyance*

**Webster's 1828 — Kindness**
> "Good will; benevolence; that temper or disposition which delights in contributing to the happiness of others."
*Kindness defined by altruistic delight in others' joy*

**Merriam-Webster 2025 — Kindness**
> "The quality or state of being kind; a kind deed."
*No explicit mention of benevolence — defined by reference to itself*

**Webster's 1913 — Faithful / Faithfulness**
> "Firm in adherence to the truth and to duties, especially in religion; full of faith… true and constant in affection or allegiance."
*Religious faith and moral duty emphasized*

**Merriam-Webster 2025 — Faithful / Faithfulness**
> "Steadfast in affection or allegiance: LOYAL; firm in adherence to promises."
*Focused on loyalty/reliability in human relations; "faith" as belief is a separate sense*

**Webster's 1913 — Temperance**
> "Habitual moderation in regard to the indulgence of the natural appetites and passions; restrained or moderate indulgence… specifically, moderation in or abstinence from intoxicating drink."
*General self-restraint with a note on alcohol*

**Merriam-Webster 2025 — Temperance**
> "Moderation in action, thought, or feeling; habitual moderation in the indulgence of appetites or passions; specifically: moderation in or abstinence from alcoholic beverages."
*Marked as archaic or historical — modern use mostly limited to period contexts*

**Reduced Theological Context:** Words like **Goodness, Faithfulness,** and **Gentleness** had strong religious meanings historically. *Goodness* was often linked to divine goodness or moral excellence; *faithfulness* was tied to faith (belief in God) and fidelity; *gentleness* was associated with *meekness* (a key Christian virtue). Modern secular dictionaries still define *goodness* as "the quality or state of being good," but "good" now is defined by secular standards (like beneficial, pleasant) more than moral righteousness. *Faithfulness* today is usually defined as *loyalty or fidelity* (to a person, cause, or promise) rather than "full of faith." In 1828, Webster defined *faithful* first as *"Firm in adherence to the truth and to the duties of religion"* and "loyal" only after that — clearly a shift from a theological to a secular priority over time. **Gentleness** was once nearly the same as *meekness* (praised in older texts); now *gentleness* is often framed as mildness or kindness — still positive, but the word *meek* itself has fallen out of favor (often misunderstood as weak). So dictionaries now define *gentle/gentleness* without implying any necessary humility before God or others, just an *absence of harshness*. This shows how *gentle* remains praised, but *meek* (its closest biblical synonym) has semantically decayed to something like "timid/submissive" in common speech (a form of entropy of that concept).

**Example — Patience:** The virtue of **Patience** shows a clear definitional shift. Webster's 1913 defined *patience* as *"the state or quality of being patient; the power of suffering with fortitude; uncomplaining endurance of evils or wrongs, as toil, pain, poverty, insult, oppression, calamity, etc."* This definition explicitly mentions *suffering, afflictions, and calm endurance of trials*, capturing the classic idea of patience as a **spiritual fortitude** in the face of hardship. It even cited biblical and literary examples of bearing insults and burdens patiently. In contrast, Merriam-Webster (2025) simplifies it to *"the capacity, habit, or fact of being patient,"* with sub-senses like "the ability to accept delays in a calm way" and "the ability to remain calm under provocation." The **essence is similar** (calm endurance), but notice the difference in **tone and scope**: the 1913 version emphasized **enduring "evil" with fortitude** — a morally charged description — whereas 2025 emphasizes **not getting annoyed at delays**, a more everyday inconvenience framing. The heroic ring of virtue is toned down to a practical trait. Also, "suffering" is now just one item in a list and phrased as *adversity* or *difficulty*; the spiritual connotation of redemptive suffering is gone. This shows a subtle *entropy* in the concept: patience has shifted from an exalted moral quality involving great trials to a mundane skill of anger management in traffic jams.

**Ideological Reframing:** In some cases, the **cultural framing** of a virtue changed so much that dictionaries added or adjusted meanings. For instance, **"Temperance"** a century ago was defined broadly as *self-restraint in appetites and passions*, often with a heavy emphasis on abstaining from alcohol (the Temperance Movement). The word carried both the general virtue meaning and a specific social movement meaning. Today, *temperance* in common usage is rare (mostly historical); its general sense of self-restraint has been largely taken over by "self-control" or "moderation." Modern dictionaries note the archaic sense and the dated capital-T Temperance (anti-alcohol) context. This shows an ideological shift: the concept of *temperance* fragmented — part became **"self-control"** (still valued, but framed in psychological terms of impulse control rather than moral purity) and part faded with changing social attitudes about alcohol. Similarly, **"Chastity"** (not one of the nine fruits, but a related virtue) would be a dramatic example: once defined in relation to sexual morality and religious duty, now often considered old-fashioned or reinterpreted as "self-chosen abstinence" in niche contexts; the mainstream vocabulary replaced *chastity* with terms like *"safe sex"* or *"faithfulness"* in relationships — a profound reframing of a virtue concept over time.

In summary, **the meanings of these virtues have stayed recognizable, but the wording and framing have "neutralized" over time**. The virtues have arguably undergone a slight *semantic bleaching* — stripped of some rich connotations. What was once couched in the language of moral philosophy or theology is now given in matter-of-fact terms. This could be seen as a form of **semantic entropy**: the "charge" or depth these words carried in a religious society has leaked away as society secularized. However, it's important to note that dictionaries reflect usage, and the changes show how general usage and context of these words evolved. For instance, if fewer people speak of "the virtue of gentleness" in a spiritual sense, the dictionary will trim that from the primary definition.

## Section Three: Semantic Entropy and Social Coherence

The evidence from both frequency and definitions supports the idea that the **moral/spiritual vocabulary has weakened**, while a more individual-focused vocabulary has risen. We can understand this through the lens of **"Semantic Entropy"** — basically, a breakdown or scattering of meaning in a culture's vocabulary. As words like *love, goodness,* and *faithfulness* lose the frequency and context they once had, their meaning to the average person may become less deep or unified. They risk becoming clichés, or narrowing to personal feelings (like "love" = just a feeling, "faithfulness" = just marital loyalty) instead of including the richer community or spiritual dimensions they once had.

Jonathan Merritt, in *The Week*, argued that as the language of faith and virtue dies off, our collective worldview and behavior shift accordingly. It's a kind of **Neo-Whorfian** idea: if we stop using words like *grace, mercy, patience,* maybe we also start valuing those concepts less or at least struggle to express them. Indeed, Merritt points out that *"we speak far less of grace, mercy, patience, and compassion… [so] is it a surprise our world is less gracious, merciful, patient, or compassionate?"* While that may be a philosophical leap, it matches research showing that language and thought are connected. As a society's common vocabulary for moral ideals shrinks, it could weaken **social coherence** — the shared understanding of virtues that holds communities together.

However, skeptics (like linguist Mark Liberman on Language Log) note that the *apparent* decline of virtue-words may be partly due to neutral factors (like more science books) and that we must be careful about concluding moral decay from word frequency. In fact, some objective measures of behavior (like declines in violent crime, greater humanitarian values, etc.) suggest society hasn't *simply* lost morality. David Brooks likewise observes that while we use moral language less, it doesn't mean we are in wholesale "moral decline" — the picture is complex. We may have traded one set of terms for another. For example, public conversation now emphasizes *equality, rights,* and *well-being* (secular moral values) more than *virtue, sin,* or *holiness*.

That said, the rise of *therapeutic language* does show a real cultural shift. Psychologist Nick Haslam identifies **"concept creep"** — where concepts of harm (like *trauma, abuse, prejudice*) have expanded their meanings over time. This can be seen as semantic entropy in action: terms that once had specific thresholds (trauma = life-threatening event) are now applied to much milder experiences. *Trauma, addiction, triggered* — such words are increasingly everywhere and sometimes used metaphorically or exaggeratedly. This **inflation of psychological terms** can blur distinctions and, some argue, *"pathologize everyday experience."* In parallel, virtue terms have not broadened but simply receded, often surviving in only trivialized forms (like *"sinful chocolate cake"* — *sin* reduced to mean "rich/delicious" in marketing, rather than a moral failing). When *sin* is just a flavor of dessert, and *trauma* can describe losing a cell phone, it's fair to say we're seeing semantic shifts that **dilute the seriousness** of both moral and psychological language.

From a social coherence standpoint, a shared moral vocabulary acts like a **cultural glue** — it provides common reference points for what is admirable or shameful. If that vocabulary breaks apart (entropy), it could lead to misunderstandings or a lack of shared vision of the good. For example, older generations might speak of "building *character*" while younger ones talk of "developing *identity*" — similar goals in some ways, but framed differently. Without translation between the two, there's potential loss in understanding between generations. As another example, the word *"integrity"* may carry less weight if rarely reinforced in conversation, potentially causing confusion about why it matters. On the other hand, it's possible new secular terms *have replaced* the old in function: like *"empathy"* is now often championed where once *"compassion"* (or *"charity"*) was, and *"accountability"* where *"righteousness"* might have been. The language changed, but social cohesion might continue around new commonly understood ideals.

Still, the **erosion of explicitly spiritual language** marks a shift from transcendent or duty-driven ethics toward a more individualistic, feelings-driven ethos. This could lead to a less cohesive society if everyone's *"authentic self"* is the ultimate guide (the *"live your truth"* mentality), rather than a common story of virtue. Sociologists Bradley Campbell and Jason Manning have described how Western culture moved from a "dignity culture" emphasizing internal virtue to a "victimhood culture" where expressing grievance and trauma gains social currency. The prevalence of harm-related language (trauma, abuse, toxic, etc.) and the relative silence about old-fashioned virtues could be a symptom of that shift. In simple terms, we hear more about what's been done *to* us (violations of well-being) than what *we* should strive to be (virtuous character). That imbalance might weaken social coherence by encouraging a mindset that is *reactive* and self-focused rather than *proactive* and community-focused.

It's not all loss, however. Some of the *Fruits of the Spirit* have been repackaged in modern terms. **"Goodness"** in a religious sense might now be discussed through words like *ethics, social responsibility,* or *justice*. **"Peace"** as inner calm finds new life in words like *mindfulness* and *wellness*. **"Love"** — while used loosely — is still a dominant theme in art and life (though we might now use extra adjectives to differentiate romantic love, platonic love, altruistic love). And the lasting popularity of personality and strengths-based psychology (like *grit, emotional intelligence, optimism*) suggests a continued interest in character qualities, even if the spiritual vocabulary isn't obvious.

Language is not destiny, but it is a mirror of what we value and how we make sense of life.

**Conclusion:** The last century's language trends reveal a major cultural shift. The *Fruits of the Spirit* — once common words for virtues to cultivate — have faded in usage and been reframed in definition to fit a more secular, individualistic age. Meanwhile, psychological and self-help vocabulary flourishes, often carrying its own implied values (personal growth, healing, empowerment). This may represent a kind of **semantic entropy** where our language for virtue and vice becomes less sharply defined and less commonly shared. Some observers warn that this leads to a loss of social cohesion — if we no longer have words like *grace* or *forgiveness* on our lips, we may find it harder to practice or collectively prioritize them. The drift from a moral-spiritual vocabulary to a therapeutic-individualistic one is more than a language curiosity; it captures a broader shift in **cultural focus from the community's quest for virtue to the individual's quest for wellness and identity**.

Whether this shift leads to a net loss of "social glue" or simply a new form of it remains an open question. What is clear is that anyone comparing the conversation of 1900 to that of 2025 can see and *quantitatively* trace the transformation. In the words of one commentator, *"Sacred words and moral terms are vanishing… Here's why it matters."* It matters because words shape our thinking, and as our words change, so do the stories we tell about who we are and what kind of society we want to build.
