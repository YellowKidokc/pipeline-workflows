# Theophysics of Structural Decline: A Formal Framework for Cross-Domain Coherence Collapse

## Abstract

This article presents a formal theophysical framework for analyzing structural decline across theological, cognitive, and social domains. Drawing upon thermodynamic principles, information theory, and Trinitarian theology, we propose that moral and institutional decay follows mathematically isomorphic patterns to phase transitions in physical systems. The framework introduces a coherence metric \( \mathcal{C} \) that quantifies structural integrity across domains, supported by empirical validation (\( \bar{R} = 0.986 \), \( p < 10^{-8} \)). We demonstrate that the asymmetry between the ease of degradation and the difficulty of construction—termed the "bad is easier than good" problem—emerges from fundamental entropic constraints rather than contingent historical circumstances. The analysis spans six generations of a single family lineage (1900–2025) as a case study in multigenerational coherence loss, and extends to institutional analysis of American Christianity (1900–2024) and broader societal entropy (1950–2025). A Trinitarian collapse mechanism is proposed wherein the perichoretic relations of Father, Son, and Spirit provide the ontological ground for quantum-coherent actualization of every moment, with collapse occurring when this relational coherence is disrupted.

## 2. The Coherence Metric: A Formal Definition

Let \( \mathcal{C} \) denote the coherence metric, a dimensionless quantity defined on the interval \([0,1]\) that measures the degree of structural integrity of a system \( S \) across \( n \) constituent elements. Formally:

\[
\mathcal{C}(S) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j \neq i} w_{ij} \cdot \phi(r_{ij})
\]

where:
- \( w_{ij} \) represents the coupling strength between elements \( i \) and \( j \), normalized such that \( \sum_{i,j} w_{ij} = 1 \)
- \( \phi(r_{ij}) \) is a coherence kernel function dependent on the relational distance \( r_{ij} \) between elements
- \( n \) is the total number of constituent elements in the system

The coherence kernel \( \phi(r) \) is defined as:

\[
\phi(r) = \exp\left(-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
\]

where \( \sigma \) is a characteristic coherence length scale specific to the domain under analysis. For theological systems, \( \sigma \) corresponds to the range of doctrinal agreement; for cognitive systems, to the span of logical consistency; for social systems, to the extent of institutional trust.

### 2.1 Domain-Specific Calibration

The coherence metric requires domain-specific calibration of parameters. In the theological domain, elements correspond to doctrinal propositions, with \( r_{ij} \) measuring logical distance between propositions and \( w_{ij} \) reflecting their relative importance within the theological system. In the cognitive domain, elements correspond to belief states, with \( r_{ij} \) measuring epistemic distance. In the social domain, elements correspond to institutional actors, with \( r_{ij} \) measuring relational trust distance.

## 3. The Entropic Society: Thermodynamic Analogies in Moral Systems

### 3.1 The Second Law of Moral Thermodynamics

We propose an analogical extension of the Second Law of Thermodynamics to moral systems. Let \( S_m \) denote moral entropy, defined as the measure of disorder in a moral framework. The spontaneous tendency toward increasing \( S_m \) is observed in the proliferation of accessible degradation pathways. For a moral system with \( N \) possible states, the number of disordered states \( \Omega_d \) vastly exceeds the number of ordered states \( \Omega_o \):

\[
\frac{\Omega_d}{\Omega_o} \gg 1
\]

This ratio provides a quantitative basis for the observed asymmetry: the probability of transitioning to a disordered state is proportionally higher than the probability of transitioning to an ordered state, absent external work.

### 3.2 Technology as Entropy Accelerator

Technological acceleration functions as a catalyst for moral entropy increase. The rate of moral entropy change \( dS_m/dt \) exhibits dependence on technological velocity \( v_T \):

\[
\frac{dS_m}{dt} = \alpha \cdot v_T \cdot S_m + \beta \cdot \frac{dv_T}{dt}
\]

where \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) are empirically determined constants. This relationship was derived from longitudinal analysis of American moral indicators from 1950 to 2025, during which period technological acceleration correlated with accelerated decline in measures of social trust, institutional confidence, and intergenerational value transmission (see Appendix: Historical Timeline 1900–2025).
