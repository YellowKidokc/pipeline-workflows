# Theophysics: A Formal Derivation of the Ground of Coherence from Information-Theoretic Constraints

## Abstract

This article presents a six-part formal argument addressing a foundational question in theophysics: What ontological ground is necessary and sufficient for the simultaneous instantiation of mathematical truth, logical structure, moral normativity, and physical law? The argument proceeds through a deductive chain beginning with information theory, thermodynamics, and mathematical logic, with no theological premise introduced prior to the final stage of derivation. The structure constitutes a predictive framework—the "lock"—against which candidate worldviews are subsequently tested. The probability of any arbitrary worldview satisfying all twenty derived constraints by chance is estimated between 1 × 10⁻⁶ and 1 × 10⁻¹⁴, depending on independence assumptions. This paper provides a comprehensive overview of the argument's architecture, the twenty axioms derived from information-theoretic principles, and the formal testing procedure applied to candidate worldviews.

## 3. The Architecture: The Soteriological Limit Theorem

### 3.1 Formal Statement

The Soteriological Limit theorem states: *No finite system can fully ground itself.* This principle is instantiated in eight independently verified domains, each of which exhibits the same structural principle under different formal descriptions. These domains include:

1. **Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems (1931):** Any consistent formal system capable of expressing elementary arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency.
2. **Chaitin's Incompleteness (1974):** For any formal system, there exists a constant L such that the system cannot prove any string to have Kolmogorov complexity greater than L.
3. **Tarski's Undefinability Theorem (1936):** A formal language cannot contain its own truth predicate.
4. **The Quantum Measurement Problem:** The wavefunction collapse cannot be fully described within the unitary evolution of the quantum system itself.
5. **The Halting Problem (Turing, 1936):** No Turing machine can determine whether an arbitrary program halts.
6. **The Second Law of Thermodynamics:** A closed system cannot decrease its own entropy without external intervention.
7. **The Regress Problem in Epistemology:** Foundational justification cannot be provided by the system being justified.
8. **The Problem of Moral Grounding:** Normative ethical claims cannot be derived from purely descriptive premises (the is-ought gap).

The identification of this isomorphism was achieved through structural comparison of the formal constraints operative in each domain. In each case, a finite, self-referential system encounters a boundary condition that cannot be resolved from within the system itself. This boundary condition is termed the *Soteriological Limit*—the point at which self-grounding fails and an external ground is required.

### 3.2 Formal Theorem

Let \( S \) be a finite formal system with language \( \mathcal{L} \), axioms \( \mathcal{A} \), and inference rules \( \mathcal{R} \). Let \( G(S) \) denote the grounding predicate, meaning "\( S \) provides a complete justification for its own truth, consistency, and applicability." Then:

\[
\forall S \text{ (finite, consistent, sufficiently expressive)}: \neg G(S)
\]

*Proof sketch:* By Gödel's Second Incompleteness Theorem, \( S \) cannot prove \( \text{Con}(S) \) within \( S \). Since self-grounding requires \( \text{Con}(S) \), it follows that \( \neg G(S) \). The same structural result obtains in each of the eight domains listed above, mutatis mutandis.

## 4. The Lock: Twenty Axioms from Information Theory

### 4.1 Derivation Methodology

The twenty axioms are derived from principles that are individually undeniable—propositions whose denial would entail performative contradiction or violation of basic rationality. The derivation proceeds from Shannon entropy, Kolmogorov complexity, and Chaitin's incompleteness results, among other information-theoretic foundations. Each axiom is presented with its formal justification.

### 4.2 The Twenty Axioms

**Group A: Properties of Mathematical Truth (Axioms 1–8)**

1. **Mathematical truths exist independently of human minds.**  
   *Justification:* Mathematical truths are discovered, not invented; their truth-value does not depend on cognitive states.

2. **Mathematical truths are necessarily true.**  
   *Justification:* Mathematical propositions, when true, are true in all possible worlds (modal necessity).

3. **Mathematical truths are universal.**  
   *Justification:* Mathematical truths hold for all observers, all locations, and all times.

4. **Mathematical truths are immaterial.**  
   *Justification:* Mathematical objects have no spatial extension, mass, or energy.

5. **Mathematical truths are eternal.**  
   *Justification:* Mathematical truths do not come into or go out of existence; they are atemporal.

6. **Mathematical truths form a coherent system.**  
   *Justification:* The axioms of mathematics are mutually consistent; contradictions are excluded by definition.

7. **Mathematical truths are discoverable by minds.**  
   *Justification:* Human cognitive agents can access mathematical truths through reasoning.

8. **Mathematical truths cannot be self-grounding (Gödel).**  
   *Justification:* By Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems, no finite formal system can provide its own complete justification.

**Group B: Properties of the Ground (Axioms 9–20)**

9. **The ground of mathematical truth must be necessary.**  
   *Justification:* If the ground were contingent, the truths it grounds would be contingent, contradicting Axiom 2.

10. **The ground must be eternal.**  
    *Justification:* If the ground were temporal, the truths it grounds would be temporal, contradicting Axiom 5.

11. **The ground must be non-deceptive (moral property).**  
    *Justification:* If the ground were deceptive, the reliability of mathematical reasoning would be undermined, producing a performative contradiction for any rational agent.

12. **The ground must be immaterial.**  
    *Justification:* If the ground were material, it could not ground immaterial truths (Axiom 4).

13. **The ground must be universal.**  
    *Justification:* If the ground were local, it could not ground universal truths (Axiom 3).

14. **The ground must be coherent.**  
    *Justification:* If the ground were incoherent, it could not ground a coherent system (Axiom 6).

15. **The ground must be singular (Occam's Razor).**  
    *Justification:* Plural grounds introduce unnecessary complexity without explanatory advantage; by Occam's principle, the simplest adequate explanation is preferred.

16. **The ground must be intelligent (information requires sender).**  
    *Justification:* Information, as defined by Shannon entropy, requires a sender; mathematical truths constitute information; therefore, the ground must be an intelligent sender.

17. **The ground must be personal (morality requires persons).**  
    *Justification:* Moral normativity (Axiom 11) requires a personal ground, as impersonal entities cannot instantiate moral properties.

18. **The ground must be free (necessity without determinism).**  
    *Justification:* The ground must be necessary in its existence but free in its actions; otherwise, creativity (Axiom 19) would be impossible.

19. **The ground must be creative (generates, not merely contains).**  
    *Justification:* The ground must actively generate the truths it grounds, not merely contain them as abstract possibilities.

20. **The ground must be good (non-deception entails benevolence).**  
    *Justification:* Non-deception (Axiom 11) entails a commitment to truth; this commitment, when combined with creative agency (Axiom 19), entails benevolence toward rational creatures.

### 4.3 Formal Representation

Let \( G \) denote the ground. The twenty axioms may be expressed as a conjunction of predicates:

\[
G := \bigwedge_{i=1}^{20} P_i(G)
\]

where each \( P_i \) is the predicate corresponding to Axiom \( i \). The CKG Score for Paper II is 7.76, reflecting the high information-theoretic density of the derivation.

## 5. The Cost of Denial: The Coherence Asymmetry Theorem

### 5.1 Formal Statement

The Coherence Asymmetry Theorem states: *The denial of any of the twenty axioms entails a greater epistemic cost than its acceptance.* This theorem is demonstrated through three formal proofs:

1. **The Active Coherence Proof:** Any agent who denies an axiom while using that axiom in reasoning commits a performative contradiction.
2. **The Existential Negation Test:** The denial of an axiom leads to a worldview that cannot account for the phenomena the axiom explains.
3. **The Coherence Asymmetry Proof:** The epistemic cost of denial exceeds the epistemic cost of acceptance for all twenty axioms.

### 5.2 Methodological Note

This paper (Paper III) does not argue for the truth of the axioms; rather, it describes the cognitive and existential consequences of their denial. The CKG Score is 7.4, reflecting the primarily descriptive rather than constructive nature of the argument.

### 6.3 Probability Analysis

The probability of any worldview satisfying all twenty constraints by coincidence is estimated as follows. Let \( p_i \) be the probability that a randomly selected worldview satisfies Axiom \( i \). Under the assumption of independence, the joint probability is:

\[
P(\text{all 20}) = \prod_{i=1}^{20} p_i
\]

If each \( p_i \) is estimated at 0.5 (maximum entropy assumption), then:

\[
P(\text{all 20}) = (0.5)^{20} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}
\]

Under more conservative independence assumptions (some axioms are correlated), the probability ranges from \( 1 \times 10^{-6} \) to \( 1 \times 10^{-14} \). The CKG Score for Paper IV is 7.64.

## 7. The Cycle: Thermodynamics of Institutional Corruption

### 7.1 Formal Derivation

Paper VI presents a formal derivation demonstrating that every institution that encodes truth eventually corrupts it. This is not presented as an empirical observation but as a thermodynamic law. The derivation proceeds as follows:

Let \( I \) be an institution with truth-encoding capacity \( T(I) \) at time \( t \). The entropy of the institution, \( S(I) \), increases over time according to:

\[
\frac{dS(I)}{dt} \geq 0
\]

where \( S(I) \) is a measure of the institution's deviation from its truth-encoding function. The Second Law of Thermodynamics, applied to social systems, yields the result that institutional corruption is inevitable unless an external negentropic force intervenes.
