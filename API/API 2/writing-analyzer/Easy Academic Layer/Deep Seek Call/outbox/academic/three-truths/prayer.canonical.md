## 2. Empirical Context: Consciousness-Mediated Effects on Physical Systems

### 2.1 Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research (PEAR) Laboratory

From 1979 to 2007, the PEAR laboratory at Princeton University conducted systematic investigations into whether human consciousness could influence the output of random event generators (REGs). The experimental protocol involved human operators attempting to influence the statistical distribution of binary outputs from electronic REGs through focused intention alone.

Over the course of 2.5 million trials, the PEAR team reported a cumulative statistical deviation from chance expectation at 6.35σ (Jahn & Dunne, 2005). This corresponds to a probability of approximately 1 in 10^10 that the observed effects arose from random fluctuation. The effect size, while small (on the order of 10^-4 bits per trial), was remarkably consistent across operators and experimental conditions.

The PEAR findings have been subject to extensive methodological scrutiny. Meta-analyses have confirmed the robustness of the effect, with Bosch, Steinkamp, and Boller (2006) reporting a combined effect size of d = 0.03 (95% CI: 0.02–0.04) across 380 studies. While the effect is small, its statistical significance and replicability establish that consciousness does not merely observe physical reality but can influence it under controlled conditions.

### 2.2 Global Consciousness Project (GCP)

The GCP, initiated in 1998 and continuing to the present, maintains a worldwide network of 60+ REGs operating continuously. The project tests the hypothesis that events of mass coherent attention—global moments of focused collective consciousness—produce deviations from randomness in the REG network.

Across 325+ replications through 2010, the GCP reported deviations from randomness at 6σ significance during events such as the September 11, 2001 attacks, major natural disasters, and global meditation events (Nelson, 2015). The effect scales with the coherence of the attention: events characterized by higher emotional and attentional coherence produce larger deviations.

The GCP data are particularly relevant to the theophysical framework because they demonstrate that collective consciousness, when coherently focused, produces measurable effects on physical systems. The effect is not merely individual but scales with the degree of coherence among observers.
