# Proof by Jurisdiction: The Boundary, The Clean Answer, and The Arrival

## Abstract

This article presents a formal theological-physical argument denominated "Proof by Jurisdiction," which reconceptualizes the problem of death as a jurisdictional test rather than an abstract metaphysical quandary. The central thesis posits that the question "Who has jurisdiction over death?" constitutes a determinative boundary condition for any coherent soteriological or eschatological framework. By reframing death as a domain of authority rather than an ontological terminus, the argument establishes a structural isomorphism between legal-jurisdictional reasoning and theological-physical claims regarding post-mortem existence. This treatment serves as a reader-facing interlude within a larger canonical corpus; the complete formal derivation, including tables, proofs, and source structures, is preserved in the associated Logos Papers.

## 01 – Proof by Jurisdiction

### 1.1 Thesis Statement

The problem of death, traditionally approached as an abstract philosophical or biological phenomenon, is here redefined as a *jurisdictional test*. The operative question—*Who has jurisdiction over death?*—functions as a boundary condition that determines the logical coherence of any subsequent theological or physical claim regarding human mortality and its potential transcendence.

### 1.2 Methodological Framing

This argument proceeds through structural comparison of jurisdictional authority across legal, theological, and physical domains. The isomorphism identified between these domains is not merely analogical but is posited as formally isomorphic under specific interpretive constraints. The jurisdictional framing transforms death from an abstraction (i.e., a state to be described) into a test (i.e., a claim to be adjudicated). This transformation is achieved by identifying the *locus of authority* over the boundary between life and non-life as the decisive variable in any complete metaphysical system.

## 02 – The Boundary

### 2.1 Structural Overview in Four Moves

The argument proceeds through four sequential moves:

### 2.2 The Boundary as Structural Element

The term "Boundary" here denotes the conceptual demarcation between domains of authority. In jurisdictional theory, a boundary defines the spatial, temporal, or categorical limits within which a given authority operates. By analogy, the boundary between life and death is not merely a biological threshold but a jurisdictional frontier. The question of *who holds authority at this frontier* determines whether death constitutes a terminal boundary or a permeable membrane subject to external jurisdiction.

### 2.3 Load-Bearing Structure

The argument's load-bearing structure consists of the following logical dependencies:

- If death is a jurisdictional boundary, then the entity holding jurisdiction over that boundary possesses authority over the transition across it.
- If no entity holds such jurisdiction, then death is an ungoverned transition, and claims of post-mortem continuity lack a governing authority.
- If a specific entity (e.g., the divine) holds such jurisdiction, then the transition across death is subject to that entity's normative framework.

This structure is presented here in summary form; the full derivation, including formal logical notation and scriptural corroboration, appears in the canonical treatment.

## 03 – The Clean Answer

### 3.1 The Short Version

The "Clean Answer" is stated as follows:

> Death is turned into a jurisdictional test instead of an abstraction.

This formulation accomplishes a categorical shift: death is removed from the domain of metaphysical speculation (What *is* death?) and relocated to the domain of authority (Who *governs* death?). The test is not one of definition but of allegiance—or, more precisely, of *jurisdictional recognition*.

### 3.2 Implications for Theological-Physical Integration

Within the framework of theophysics, this jurisdictional reframing permits the integration of physical descriptions of death (e.g., thermodynamic cessation, neural decay) with theological claims regarding resurrection, judgment, or annihilation. The physical description characterizes the *state*; the jurisdictional claim characterizes the *authority over transition from that state*. These are not competing accounts but complementary descriptions operating at different logical levels.

### 3.3 Scriptural and Doctrinal Corroboration

The argument draws upon scriptural passages that explicitly frame death within a context of divine jurisdiction. For example:

- **Revelation 1:18** (NRSV): "I am the Living One; I was dead, and now look, I am alive for ever and ever! And I hold the keys of death and Hades."
- **Romans 14:9** (NRSV): "For to this end Christ died and lived again, so that he might be Lord of both the dead and the living."

These passages are interpreted within the present framework as jurisdictional claims: the "keys" denote authority over access to and egress from the domain of death. The full canonical treatment provides a systematic exegesis of these and related passages within the jurisdictional paradigm.
