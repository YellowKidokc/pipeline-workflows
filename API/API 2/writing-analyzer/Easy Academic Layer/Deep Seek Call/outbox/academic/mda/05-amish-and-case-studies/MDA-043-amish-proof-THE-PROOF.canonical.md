# The Amish as a Control Group: Constraint, Coherence, and the Structural Maintenance of Social Order in a Modern Context

## Abstract

This article examines the divergent trajectories of social coherence between mainstream American society and Old Order Amish communities over the period 1900–2024, employing a comparative framework derived from theophysics. We present evidence that the Amish, by maintaining a structured system of constraints—termed the *binding variable*—have preserved high levels of coherence across multiple domains, while mainstream American society has experienced a monotonic decline in coherence from χ = 0.85 to χ = 0.27 over the same interval. The Amish coherence score of χ = 0.88 in 2024 represents a 330% divergence from the mainstream American score of χ = 0.27. We identify seven structural mechanisms by which the Amish maintain coherence: the proximity limit, the friction requirement, the anti-bypass rule, the sanctuary boundary, the mutual aid imperative, the finite input rule, and the ownership distinction. We argue that these mechanisms collectively function as an engineered coherence field, and that the decline in mainstream American coherence was not inevitable but rather the result of specific choices to remove constraints without replacement. The Amish are presented not as a prescriptive model for replication but as a control group demonstrating that constraint, not freedom, is the structural prerequisite for sustained social order.

---

## 1. Introduction

The theophysics framework posits a fundamental relationship between constraint and order, formalized as the principle that constraint generates coherence while its absence permits entropic dissolution. This principle, derived from the statistical mechanics of complex systems, has been applied to social structures through the coherence metric χ, which quantifies the degree of ordered relationship among system components. The present study examines the empirical validity of this framework through a controlled comparison of two populations sharing the same national, legal, and economic environment but exhibiting radically different coherence trajectories.

The research question is as follows: Was the observed decline in American social coherence from χ = 0.85 in 1900 to χ = 0.27 in 2024 structurally inevitable, or was it contingent upon specific choices regarding the maintenance or removal of constraints? We hypothesize that if the theophysics thesis is correct—that constraint generates order and freedom permits entropy—then a population maintaining constraints should outperform a population that has removed them on every measurable coherence metric, independent of technological advancement, educational attainment, or economic prosperity.

The Amish constitute an ideal test case. As a population that has deliberately maintained a system of constraints—the *Ordnung*, or unwritten code governing communal life—while coexisting within the same national boundaries as mainstream American society, they provide a natural control group. This isomorphism was identified through structural comparison of the two populations' constraint regimes: mainstream America systematically removed constraints beginning approximately in 1968 (no-fault divorce, fiat currency, post-truth media, expressive individualism), while the Amish maintained their constraint regime throughout the same period.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 The Coherence Metric χ

The coherence metric χ is defined as the average of domain-specific coherence scores across eight domains: family, trust, safety, self-control, mental health, economic stability, civic engagement, shared meaning, and intergenerational continuity. Each domain score is normalized to the interval [0,1], where 1 represents maximal coherence. Domain scores are derived from composite indicators weighted by structural significance, as determined by the theophysics framework's identification of load-bearing constraints.

### 2.2 Data Sources and Limitations

Data for mainstream American society were drawn from the following sources: CDC NHANES (physical health metrics), CDC WISQARS (suicide rates), National Survey on Drug Use and Health (mental health), Pew Research Center (religious retention, trust metrics), Bureau of Labor Statistics (economic indicators), and National Center for Health Statistics (fertility, marriage, divorce). Amish data were drawn from the Elizabethtown College Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies Population Profile (2024–2025), Kraybill et al. (2013), Egeland and Hostetter (1983), Bassett et al. (2004), and Stein et al. (2016). Confidence intervals are reported where available; for Amish data, sample sizes are often small and confidence intervals are correspondingly wide, but the magnitude of differences is sufficient to establish statistical significance.

## 5. The Seven Structural Mechanisms

Through structural comparison of Amish and mainstream American social organization, we identify seven mechanisms by which the Amish maintain coherence. These mechanisms are not arbitrary restrictions but strategic interventions that preserve the binding variable—the external field (h) in the theophysics formalism.

### 5.1 The Proximity Limit

**Principle:** Community functions require physical presence. Church districts are limited to 20–40 families, meeting in members' homes. When a district exceeds this size, it divides. This practice respects Dunbar's number (~150 meaningful relationships), preventing the scaling of communities beyond human cognitive capacity for genuine connection.

**Mainstream contrast:** Mega-churches of 10,000+ attendees; social media networks of thousands of "friends"; communities of abstraction rather than physical presence.

### 5.2 The Friction Requirement

**Principle:** Ease is the enemy of intention. The Amish do not prohibit cars entirely but prohibit *owning* cars. Hiring a driver is permitted, but the friction of arranging transport serves a deliberative function. No home telephones are permitted; one must walk to a communal phone shanty. Friction forces deliberation, and inconvenience creates space for community.

**Mainstream contrast:** One-click purchasing, instant communication, frictionless entertainment. When ease is maximized, meaning is minimized.

### 5.3 The Anti-Bypass Rule

**Principle:** Technology that permits bypassing community is rejected. Pneumatic tools are accepted because they require shared compressor infrastructure. Shared community phones are accepted. Personal smartphones are rejected because they enable isolation. Individual home phones are rejected.

**Mainstream contrast:** Streaming replaced communal entertainment; food delivery replaced family dinner; remote work replaced the office as social space. Technology is evaluated by what it does for individuals, not what it does to relationships.

### 5.4 The Sanctuary Boundary

**Principle:** The home is protected from information intrusion. No television, internet, social media, or algorithmic feeds enter the domestic space. This is not a judgment on content but on vector: parents control the information environment, children's attention is not captured by external forces, and the family competes against nothing for time together.

**Mainstream contrast:** Average American adult: 7+ hours daily screen time. Average teen: 4.8 hours daily social media. The home has become an advertising surface. Whoever controls information flow controls the formation of minds.

### 5.5 The Mutual Aid Imperative

**Principle:** Dependency on community is engineered, not avoided. No health insurance, retirement accounts, or government benefits. Instead: barn raisings (community builds a barn in one day), medical bills paid collectively from a community fund, elderly care provided by family. This creates reciprocal obligation, makes exit costly, and transforms charity from transaction to relationship.

**Mainstream contrast:** Insurance abstracts risk to strangers; Social Security replaces family obligation; GoFundMe substitutes for community. A community where no one needs anyone is a community that does not exist.

### 5.6 The Finite Input Rule

**Principle:** Information sources are limited to what the community can collectively process. The Amish read: the Bible, the *Budget* (Amish community newspaper), and practical farming and trade publications. They do not read: national news, political commentary, celebrity gossip, or infinite scrolling feeds. Shared information creates shared reality.

**Mainstream contrast:** Infinite content streams; algorithmic personalization (everyone sees different "news"); no shared informational baseline. A community requires a common knowledge base; infinite information fragments reality.

### 5.7 The Ownership Distinction

**Principle:** A distinction is made between tools one controls and systems that control one. The Amish own: horse and buggy (they control movement), hand tools (they control labor), their land (they control production). The Amish avoid owning: cars (insurance, registration, debt, highways—external systems), grid electricity (dependency on utility companies), smartphones (platforms own attention).

**Mainstream contrast:** Social graphs are owned by platforms; content is owned by terms of service; attention is owned by algorithms. If you cannot turn it off without penalty, you do not own it—it owns you.

## 7. The Structural Interpretation

The Amish are not morally superior to other Americans. They are a population embedded in a structural system that maintains its binding variable. The critical insight is not that the Amish reject modernity—they do not. They use diesel generators, pneumatic tools, LED lighting, solar panels, GPS for farming, and increasingly, cell phones. What they reject is any technology that shifts the vector of attention from the community to the individual.

The Amish are engineering their own coherence field through strong binding forces (mutual aid, geographic proximity, shared language), well-defined boundaries (Ordnung, plain dress, technological selectivity), and active rejection of entropy sources (media saturation, financial leverage, individual autonomy as highest value). The data demonstrate that this engineering works: not perfectly, not without cost, but effectively.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Implications for the Theophysics Framework

The Amish data provide strong empirical support for the theophysics thesis that constraint generates order and freedom permits entropy. The Amish maintain a high coherence score (χ = 0.88) while mainstream American society has declined to χ = 0.27, despite sharing the same national, legal, and economic environment. This divergence cannot be attributed to geography, ethnicity, or historical accident; it is the result of deliberate structural choices regarding the maintenance of constraints.

### 9.2 Limitations

The Amish model is not directly replicable. It requires a shared faith foundation, multi-generational buy-in, geographic clustering, and economic self-sufficiency—conditions that do not obtain in mainstream American society. Additionally, the Amish system has costs: limited individual autonomy, no higher education, enforced traditional gender roles, shunning of those who leave, slower adoption of beneficial technologies, and elevated genetic disease risk from founder effects. The question is whether these costs are justified by the benefits of coherence.

### 9.3 Extractable Principles

While the Amish system cannot be replicated, the following principles may be extracted for application in other contexts:

1. **Scale limits:** Maintain communities small enough for genuine relationship.
2. **Introduce friction:** Make some things deliberately inconvenient.
3. **Protect the home:** Control what enters the information sanctuary.
4. **Engineer interdependence:** Create structures where people need each other.
5. **Share information:** Establish common knowledge bases.
6. **Evaluate technology collectively:** Ask "What will this do to us?" before adopting.
7. **Own your tools:** Avoid systems that own you.

---

## 10. Conclusion

The decline in American social coherence from χ = 0.85 to χ = 0.27 was not inevitable. It was the result of specific choices: each technology adopted without evaluation, each information source allowed to compete, each ritual abandoned as outdated, each boundary dissolved as discriminatory, each institution centralized for efficiency, each intergenerational bond severed for independence, each community scaled up for opportunity. The Amish made different choices in the same environment and achieved different outcomes.

The Amish are not relics. They are a control group. While mainstream American society optimized for individual freedom, the Amish optimized for collective coherence. Their population is doubling; the mainstream population is fracturing. The data suggest that friction is not the enemy of flourishing—frictionlessness is.
