# Theophysics: A Quantitative and Lexicographical Analysis of Semantic Drift in the Language of Virtue (1900–2025)

## Abstract

This study examines the measurable transformation of moral and spiritual vocabulary in English-language discourse over the period 1900–2025, focusing specifically on the nine virtues traditionally designated as the "Fruits of the Spirit" (Galatians 5:22–23, New International Version): love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Employing a dual-methodological approach combining diachronic corpus linguistics (Google Ngram analysis, 1900–2019) and comparative lexicography (Webster's 1828/1913 versus Merriam-Webster 2025), we demonstrate a statistically significant decline in the frequency of traditional virtue terminology alongside a concomitant rise in therapeutic and self-oriented vocabulary. The analysis further reveals systematic shifts in definitional framing—from morally prescriptive and theologically embedded definitions toward secular, value-neutral, and behaviorally descriptive formulations. We propose the concept of "semantic entropy" as a theoretical framework for interpreting these linguistic transformations, positing that the attenuation of shared moral vocabulary may correlate with diminished social coherence. The findings are contextualized within broader cultural shifts from communal virtue ethics toward individualistic therapeutic paradigms, with implications for interdisciplinary discourse at the intersection of linguistics, moral philosophy, and theophysics.

## 1. Introduction: A Measurable Drift in the Language of Virtue

The nine virtues enumerated in Galatians 5:22–23—ἀγάπη (agapē, love), χαρά (chara, joy), εἰρήνη (eirēnē, peace), μακροθυμία (makrothymia, patience), χρηστότης (chrēstotēs, kindness), ἀγαθωσύνη (agathōsynē, goodness), πίστις (pistis, faithfulness), πραΰτης (prautēs, gentleness), and ἐγκράτεια (enkrateia, self-control)—have constituted foundational pillars of Western moral character within the Christian theological tradition for nearly two millennia. This investigation undertakes a systematic examination of how these terms' usage frequencies and semantic boundaries have evolved over the past century, a period characterized by accelerating secularization, the rise of psychological discourse, and shifting cultural value systems.

The analysis proceeds along two complementary dimensions: (1) **diachronic frequency analysis** utilizing the Google Books Ngram corpus (1900–2019), and (2) **comparative lexicographical analysis** examining dictionary definitions from approximately 1900 versus contemporary sources (circa 2025). The central hypothesis posits a measurable decline in the usage of traditional moral-spiritual vocabulary, accompanied by a corresponding increase in therapeutic and self-oriented terminology (e.g., *trauma*, *validation*, *empowerment*, *self-care*, *authenticity*). Furthermore, we investigate whether definitional shifts—including semantic broadening, narrowing, or tonal transformation—constitute a form of "semantic entropy" wherein venerable virtues lose conceptual clarity or cultural resonance. Finally, we consider the implications of these linguistic trends for broader cultural dynamics and social cohesion.

**Methodological Caveat:** All Google Ngram findings are derived from the English Google Books corpus. Frequency is measured as a proportion of all words per year, a normalization that inherently accounts for corpus size growth. However, we exercise caution regarding known biases, including the increasing proportion of scientific and technical publications in the post-1900 corpus, as well as optical character recognition (OCR) errors that may contribute to general downward frequency drifts (Liberman, 2018; Merritt, 2018). A 3-year smoothing function was applied to reduce stochastic noise.

## 2. Section One: Google Ngram Frequency Analysis (1900–2019)

### 2.1 Methodology and Bias Controls

Using Google's Ngram Viewer (Michel et al., 2011), we tracked the yearly relative frequency of each virtue term from 1900 through the 2010s (data available through approximately 2019). To mitigate digitization biases, we examined **relative frequencies** (occurrences per million words) rather than raw counts, and applied a 3-year smoothing function to reduce noise. Trends were interpreted in light of known corpus composition biases: the Google Books corpus after 1900 contains a growing proportion of scientific and technical literature, which tends to employ moral and religious terminology less frequently. This compositional shift implies that a **general downward drift** in many common words' frequencies over the twentieth century is partially an artifact of corpus composition (Liberman, 2018; Merritt, 2018).

One analysis demonstrated that approximately 80% of a random sample of English words appeared less frequently in the latter twentieth century than in the early 1900s (Liberman, 2018). We therefore focus on **comparative differentials**—for instance, virtue-words versus self-help words—rather than the absolute decline of any single term. Additionally, we consulted the **English Fiction** subset to verify that observed trends were not solely attributable to the expansion of academic and technical texts.

### 2.2 Decline of Virtue Terminology

With few exceptions, the nine "Fruit of the Spirit" terms exhibit **flat or declining usage** over the century, particularly from the mid-twentieth century onward. This pattern suggests a waning cultural emphasis on explicitly spiritual or moral vocabulary.

**Overall Decline:** A 2012 study of 50 morality-related words found that 74% declined in frequency over the twentieth century (Merritt, 2018). Specifically, common Christian virtue terms including *grace*, *mercy*, *wisdom*, and *faith* all exhibited marked downturns (Merritt, 2018). Correspondingly, general terms for the "Fruit of the Spirit" virtues—*love*, *patience*, *gentleness*, *faithfulness*—became substantially rarer in printed use (Merritt, 2018). For example, "compassion words, like **kindness**, dropped by 56%" over the 1900s, and "humility words, like **modesty**, fell by 52%" (Merritt, 2018). The term **gentleness** likewise dwindled, as did **faithfulness**, which by the twenty-first century is seldom employed outside religious or explicitly nostalgic contexts (Merritt, 2018). Even **patience**—once frequently extolled as a cardinal virtue—appears less frequently in contemporary discourse than in the early twentieth century.

**Case Study—"Love":** The term **love** presents an instructive case. It remains among the most frequent English nouns throughout the entire period; nevertheless, even *love* exhibits a subtle decline in relative frequency after mid-century. In the early 1900s, literature (particularly fiction and poetry) abundantly employed the term; in later decades, the increasing proportion of scientific and informational publications diluted its relative share. Brooks (2015, as cited in Merritt, 2018) observes that even basic moral words such as *love* have receded from public conversation, attributable in part to a broader cultural shift away from sacred language. (Notably, *love* in secular contexts carries numerous non-virtuous connotations; however, this analysis tracks overall usage frequency.)

**Wartime Peaks—"Peace":** The term **peace** deviated from the overall declining trend at specific historical junctures: its frequency spiked around 1918 and 1945 (concluding the World Wars) and again in the 1960s (the "Peace" movement), as global events drove discourse (Google Books Ngram Viewer, 2019). Outside these surges, however, *peace* declined in general use through the late twentieth century, reflecting less frequent religious and philosophical discussion of "inner peace" or peace as a spiritual virtue, compared to more pragmatic political usage (e.g., "peace treaty"). By the 2000s, *peace* appears proportionally less frequently than in 1900.

**Stable or Mixed Trends:** **Joy** and **goodness** exhibit relatively flatter trajectories (minor declines). *Goodness* as an exclamation ("Goodness gracious!") sustained its usage, albeit as a mild interjection rather than a substantive moral term. **Self-control**, notably, constitutes one "fruit" that **did not decline**—in fact, it **increased** in usage over time. Research indicates that *self-control* (as a concept in psychology and self-help literature) rose steadily across the twentieth century (ResearchGate, 2022). This contrasts with its biblical synonym *temperance*, which sharply declined after the early 1900s (following the temperance movement and Prohibition era). Essentially, *temperance* (with its moral and religious overtones) fell out of favor, while the more secular term *self-control* gained currency in discussions of personal development and behavioral regulation. This **exception** underscores that not all virtue language vanished—some was **reframed in secular terms** (see Section 2).

### 2.3 Rise of Therapeutic and Self-Help Vocabulary

In stark contrast to the attenuation of traditional virtues, our analysis reveals that **therapeutic, psychological, and self-oriented terms have surged** in usage since mid-century. We compared the "fruits" with several representative modern terms: **trauma**, **validation**, **empowerment**, **self-care**, and **authenticity**. The results strongly support the hypothesis of a lexicon shift.

**Table 2: Frequency Trends for Therapeutic/Self-Oriented Terms (1900–2019)**

| Term | Observed Trend | Approximate Period of Acceleration | Notes |
|------|----------------|-----------------------------------|-------|
| Trauma | Skyrocketing | Post-1970 | Virtually absent outside medical contexts before mid-century |
| Empowerment | Explosive growth | 1990s–2000s | Barely registered before 1970 |
| Self-care | Steep climb | 2010s | Late twentieth-century coinage |
| Authenticity | Growing | Since 1970s | Philosophical usage preceded popular adoption |
| Validation | Uptick | From 1980s | Emotional/social sense largely modern development |
| "Me" (pronoun) | +87% | 1975–2007 | Surge in self-referential language |

**Therapy and Trauma:** The word **trauma** (in the psychological sense) was virtually absent in 1900s literature outside medical contexts. It began modest growth mid-century, then skyrocketed from the 1970s to the present. By the 2010s, *trauma* has become a commonplace term in books on psychology, self-help, social sciences, and popular media. This reflects how discussing personal psychological wounds has entered mainstream discourse. Indeed, some critics note that *trauma* has been **conceptually broadened** in modern usage to encompass almost any adversity or emotional wound (Haslam, 2016). The rising curve of *trauma* inversely mirrors the falling curve of virtues such as *patience* or *forgiveness*.

**Empowerment and Self-Care:** The term **empowerment** barely registered in books before 1970. It gained traction with civil rights and feminist movements, and exploded in the 1990s–2000s as the ethos of personal empowerment and agency became culturally prominent. **Self-care** (in the sense of personal well-being practices) is another late twentieth-century creation—its frequency climbed steeply in the 2010s with the wellness movement. These words emphasize **individual improvement and validation** over communal or spiritual virtues. The data suggest that where a nineteenth-century author might have urged *charity* or *patience*, a twenty-first-century author is more likely to speak of *self-care* and *empowerment*.

**Authenticity and Validation:** **Authenticity**, meaning *genuineness* or *being true to oneself*, has long existed in philosophical discourse (e.g., existentialist writings), but its popular usage grew in the late twentieth century. In recent decades, *authenticity* has become a buzzword in self-help literature (encouraging individuals to "live authentically"). Its frequency in print climbed accordingly. **Validation**, in the context of affirming someone's feelings or identity, also rose in usage. While *validation* as a general word existed (e.g., validating a parking ticket), the **emotional/social sense** ("seeking validation," "validate someone's experience") is largely a modern development. Google Books data reveal a noticeable uptick in *validation* from the 1980s onward, aligning with the rise of pop psychology and relational vocabulary.

**Secular Replacements:** It appears that as explicit faith-based words (*sin*, *virtue*, *soul*, etc.) receded, **secular terms filled the void** in discourse about character and well-being. For example, rather than speaking of *meekness* or *humility*, contemporary literature stresses *self-esteem* and *confidence*. Instead of *long-suffering* or *forbearance*, we encounter *resilience*. This pattern is corroborated by sociological language analyses: one study of U.S. State of the Union addresses from 1790–2012 found a decline in "other-oriented" words (such as *neighbor*) and a rise in self-focused words (*I*, *me*, *mine*) over time (Eurich, 2017). Likewise, a Google Ngram analysis of pronouns shows that while *me* declined in usage mid-century, it surged by **87%** in frequency from 1975 to 2007 (Eurich, 2017)—evidence of a growing **"Cult of Self"** in our language (as characterized by Eurich, 2017). In parallel, words like *humility* and *modesty* became rarer, while *self-expression* and *self-worth* became common.

### 2.4 Summary of Ngram Findings

In summary, the **past 120+ years have witnessed a quantifiable decline in the language of traditional virtue, alongside a rise in the language of psychology and self-focus**. We normalized for overall corpus expansion; thus, this pattern reflects a real shift in relative attention rather than merely an increase in the total number of books published. To be clear, this does not imply that individuals ceased experiencing love or valuing kindness; rather, they **discuss (and write about) them less** in explicit terms, particularly outside religious contexts. Meanwhile, discussions of personal feelings, mental health, and individual empowerment have proliferated. This supports the hypothesis of a long-term drift from a **moral/spiritual lexicon** to a **therapeutic/self-oriented lexicon**.

Some researchers interpret this as evidence of *lexical softening* or **semantic drift** in our culture's vocabulary of character (Brooks, 2015; Merritt, 2018). The concept of **Semantic Entropy** may be applied here: as classic virtue words lose frequency and clarity, the shared moral meaning within a culture could be "dissipating" or fragmenting. In the following section, we explore whether the *meaning* of these words has also changed over time, or only their frequency.

## 3. Section Two: Evolution of Dictionary Definitions (1900 vs. 2025)

How have the dictionary definitions of the nine "fruit" terms changed, if at all, from the early twentieth century to the present? To investigate, we compared entries from **historical dictionaries** (Webster's 1913 Unabridged and, where insightful, Webster's 1828) with **current** definitions from Merriam-Webster (2025 edition) and the Oxford English Dictionary (OED). We examined shifts in denotation, connotation, and tone—specifically, whether these virtues have been *redefined*, *broadened*, *narrowed*, or assigned different emphases over time.

Overall, we found that the **core literal meanings remain remarkably stable**—*love is still love, patience still patience*—but there are **notable shifts in emphasis and framing**:

### 3.1 From Moral Virtues to Personal Traits

Early twentieth-century dictionaries often defined these words in explicitly moral or religious terms, sometimes employing prescriptive or florid language. Modern definitions tend to be more **concise, secular, and value-neutral**. In older editions, one encounters words such as "virtue," "uprightness," "righteous," or even references to God or scripture within the definition. Contemporary definitions generally avoid overt value judgments, describing the term in plain behavioral terms. The **tone** has shifted from *ideological* to *descriptive*.

### 3.2 Loss of Prescriptive Detail

Older definitions frequently included an explanation of *why* the trait is good or situations illustrating its application. Newer definitions assume users recognize the virtue's positive valence and focus on *what* it is. For example, Webster's 1828 defined **Kindness** as "Good will; benevolence; that temper or disposition which delights in contributing to the happiness of others" (Webster, 1828). This definition not only defines kindness but extols a worldview of finding joy in helping others. Merriam-Webster (2025) defines *kindness* simply as "the quality of being kind; a kind act" (Merriam-Webster, 2025)—a substantially barer definition that employs *kind* to define itself, omitting the moral elaboration. The charitable dimension (benevolence, delighting in others' happiness) is left implicit or relegated to examples.

### 3.3 Broadening and Semantic Dilution

Some virtues have acquired broader, less intense meanings in everyday use, which dictionaries have incorporated. **Love**, for instance, historically signified deep affection or devotion (with nuances of duty and sacrifice). Earlier lexicographers wrestled with defining *love*—Samuel Johnson famously evaded the task by quoting others (Los Angeles Times, 1985). By 1913, Webster's Secondary School Dictionary defined *love* as "a feeling of strong personal attachment; ardent affection; (also) desire for, and earnest effort to promote the welfare of another" (Webster, 1913). This definition notably includes *unselfish concern for another's good* as integral to love. In contrast, Merriam-Webster's current definition prioritizes the romantic/familial sense: "strong affection for another arising out of kinship or personal ties," and "attraction based on sexual desire," with altruistic *"unselfish loyal concern for others"* appearing later as a subtype (Merriam-Webster, 2025). The *ordering* of meanings has inverted: what 100 years ago might have constituted the primary essence of love (devotion and welfare of others) now appears as a secondary or tertiary sense, while emotional attraction is primary. This suggests an **ideological reframing**—love is framed less as virtuous commitment and more as personal feeling.

### 3.5 Reduced Theological Context

Terms including **Goodness**, **Faithfulness**, and **Gentleness** carried strong religious connotations historically. *Goodness* was often linked to divine goodness or moral excellence; *faithfulness* was tied to faith (belief in God) and fidelity; *gentleness* was associated with *meekness* (a key Christian virtue). Modern secular dictionaries still define *goodness* as "the quality or state of being good," but "good" is now defined by secular standards (e.g., beneficial, pleasant) rather than moral righteousness. *Faithfulness* today is typically defined as *loyalty or fidelity* (to a person, cause, or promise) rather than "full of faith." In 1828, Webster defined *faithful* firstly as "Firm in adherence to the truth and to the duties of religion" and "loyal" only after that—a clear shift from theological to secular priority over time. **Gentleness** was once nearly synonymous with *meekness* (praised in older texts); now *gentleness* is often framed as mildness or kindness—still positive, but the word *meek* itself has fallen into disfavor (often misunderstood as weak). Hence, dictionaries now define *gentle/gentleness* without implying any necessary humility before God or others, merely an *absence of harshness*. This reflects how *gentle* remains praised, while *meek* (its closest biblical synonym) has semantically deteriorated to something like "timid/submissive" in common parlance—a form of entropy of that concept.

### 3.6 Example—Patience: A Detailed Case Study

The virtue of **Patience** exhibits a clear definitional shift. Webster's 1913 defined *patience* as "the state or quality of being patient; the power of suffering with fortitude; uncomplaining endurance of evils or wrongs, as toil, pain, poverty, insult, oppression, calamity, etc." (Webster, 1913). This definition explicitly mentions *suffering*, *afflictions*, and *calm endurance of trials*, capturing the classic conception of patience as a **spiritual fortitude** in the face of hardship. It even cited biblical and literary examples of bearing insults and burdens patiently (Webster, 1913). In contrast, Merriam-Webster (2025) simplifies it to "the capacity, habit, or fact of being patient," with sub-senses including "the ability to accept delays in a calm way" and "the ability to remain calm under provocation" (Merriam-Webster, 2025). The **essence is similar** (calm endurance), but the **tone and scope** differ markedly: the 1913 version emphasized **enduring "evil" with fortitude**—a morally charged description—whereas the 2025 version emphasizes **not getting annoyed at delays**, a more everyday inconvenience framing. The heroic ring of virtue is attenuated to a practical trait. Additionally, "suffering" is now merely one item in a list, phrased as *adversity* or *difficulty*; the spiritual connotation of redemptive suffering is absent. This illustrates a subtle *entropy* in the concept: patience has shifted from an exalted moral quality involving great trials to a mundane skill of anger management in quotidian frustrations.

### 3.7 Ideological Reframing

In some cases, the **cultural framing** of a virtue changed such that dictionaries added or adjusted senses. For instance, **"Temperance"** a century ago was defined broadly as *self-restraint in appetites and passions*, often with a heavy emphasis on abstaining from alcohol (the Temperance Movement). The word carried both the general virtue meaning and a specific social movement meaning. Today, *temperance* in common usage is rare (largely historical); its general sense of self-restraint has been largely taken over by "self-control" or "moderation." Modern dictionaries note the archaic sense and the dated capital-T Temperance (anti-alcohol) context. This reflects an ideological shift: the concept of *temperance* fragmented—part became **"self-control"** (still valued, but framed in psychological terms of impulse control rather than moral purity) and part faded with changing social mores regarding alcohol. Similarly, **"Chastity"** (not among the nine fruits, but a related virtue) would constitute a dramatic example: once defined in relation to sexual morality and religious duty, now often considered old-fashioned or reinterpreted as "self-chosen abstinence" in niche contexts; the mainstream lexicon replaced *chastity* with terms such as *safe sex* or *faithfulness* in relationships—a profound reframing of a virtue concept over time.

In summary, **the meanings of these virtues have remained semantically recognizable, but the diction and framing have "neutralized" over time**. The virtues have arguably undergone a slight *semantic bleaching*—stripped of certain rich connotations. What was once couched in the language of moral philosophy or theology is now presented in matter-of-fact terms. This could be viewed as a form of **semantic entropy**: the "charge" or depth these words carried in a religious society has dissipated as society secularized. However, it is important to note that dictionaries reflect usage, and the changes indicate how general usage and context of these words evolved. For instance, if fewer individuals speak of "the virtue of gentleness" in a spiritual sense, the dictionary will trim that from the primary definition.

## 4. Section Three: Semantic Entropy and Social Coherence

The evidence from both frequency analysis and comparative lexicography supports the proposition that the **moral/spiritual lexicon has attenuated**, while a more individual-centric lexicon has ascended. We interpret this through the lens of **"Semantic Entropy"**—essentially, a degradation or dispersion of meaning within a culture's vocabulary. As words such as *love*, *goodness*, and *faithfulness* lose the frequency and contextual richness they once possessed, their meaning to the average individual may become less profound or unified. They risk becoming clichés, or narrowing to personal sentiments (e.g., "love" = merely a feeling, "faithfulness" = merely marital fidelity) rather than encompassing the richer communal or spiritual dimensions they once conveyed.

Merritt (2018) argued that as the language of faith and virtue recedes, collective worldview and behavior shift accordingly. This constitutes a kind of **Neo-Whorfian** hypothesis: if we cease using words like *grace*, *mercy*, *patience*, perhaps we also begin valuing those concepts less, or at least struggle to articulate them (Merritt, 2018). Indeed, Merritt observes that "we speak far less of grace, mercy, patience, and compassion… [so] is it a surprise our world is less gracious, merciful, patient, or compassionate?" (Merritt, 2018). While this may represent a philosophical leap, it aligns with research suggesting that language and thought are interconnected. As a society's common vocabulary for moral ideals contracts, it could erode **social coherence**—the shared understanding of virtues that binds communities.

However, skeptics (including linguist Mark Liberman on Language Log) note that the *apparent* decline of virtue-words may be partially attributable to neutral factors (such as the increasing proportion of scientific publications) and caution against concluding moral decay from word frequency alone (Liberman, 2018). In fact, certain objective behavioral measures (e.g., declines in violent crime, greater humanitarian values) suggest that society has not *simply* lost morality (Brooks, 2015). Brooks (2015) likewise observes that while we employ moral language less frequently, this does not necessarily indicate wholesale "moral decline"—the picture is complex (Brooks, 2015). We may have traded one set of terms for another. For example, public discourse now emphasizes *equality*, *rights*, and *well-being* (secular moral values) more than *virtue*, *sin*, or *holiness*.

That said, the rise of *therapeutic language* does reflect a genuine cultural pivot. Haslam (2016) identifies **"concept creep"**—where concepts of harm (such as *trauma*, *abuse*, *prejudice*) have expanded their meanings over time. This can be interpreted as semantic entropy in action: terms that once possessed specific thresholds (trauma = life-threatening event) are now applied to substantially milder experiences (Haslam, 2016). *Trauma*, *addiction*, *triggered*—such words are increasingly ubiquitous and sometimes employed metaphorically or hyperbolically. This **inflation of psychological terms** can blur distinctions and, some argue, *pathologize everyday experience* (Haslam, 2016). In parallel, virtue terms have not broadened but simply receded, often surviving only in trivialized forms (e.g., *"sinful chocolate cake"*—*sin* reduced to signify "rich/delicious" in marketing, rather than a moral failing) (Brooks, 2015). When *sin* becomes merely a flavor of dessert, and *trauma* can describe losing a cellular phone, it is reasonable to suggest that we are witnessing semantic shifts that **dilute the gravity** of both moral and psychological language.

From a social coherence standpoint, a shared moral vocabulary functions as **cultural glue**—it provides common reference points for what is admirable or reprehensible. If that vocabulary fragments (entropy), it could lead to misunderstandings or a lack of collective vision of the good. For example, older generations might speak of "building *character*" while younger cohorts discuss "developing *identity*"—similar aims in certain respects, but framed differently. Without translation between the two, there exists potential loss in intergenerational coherence. As another example, the term *integrity* may carry less weight if seldom reinforced in discourse, potentially yielding confusion regarding its importance. On the other hand, it is possible that new secular terms *have replaced* the old in function: e.g., *empathy* is now frequently championed where once *compassion* (or *charity*) was, and *accountability* where *righteousness* might have been. The language changed, but social cohesion might persist around new commonly understood ideals.

Nevertheless, the **erosion of explicitly spiritual language** marks a shift from transcendent or duty-driven ethics toward a more individualistic, feelings-driven ethos (Eurich, 2017; Karaoguz, 2024). This could presage a less cohesive society if everyone's *authentic self* becomes the ultimate guide (the *"live your truth"* mentality) (Karaoguz, 2024), rather than a common narrative of virtue. Sociologists Campbell and Manning (as cited in Haslam, 2016) have described how Western culture moved from a "dignity culture" emphasizing internal virtue to a "victimhood culture" where expressing grievance and trauma gains social currency. The prevalence of harm-related language (*trauma*, *abuse*, *toxic*, etc.) and the relative silence regarding traditional virtues could be symptomatic of that shift. In simple terms, we encounter more discourse about what has been done *to* us (violations of well-being) than about what *we* should strive to become (virtuous character). That imbalance might undermine social coherence by fostering a mindset that is *reactive* and self-focused rather than *proactive* and community-focused.

It is not, however, an unmitigated loss. Some of the *Fruits of the Spirit* have been repackaged in contemporary terms. **"Goodness"** in a religious sense might now be discussed through words such as *ethics*, *social responsibility*, or *justice*. **"Peace"** as inner tranquility finds new expression in terms like *mindfulness* and *wellness*. **"Love"**—while employed loosely—remains a dominant theme in art and life (though we might differentiate romantic love, platonic love, and altruistic love with additional adjectives). The enduring popularity of personality and strengths-based psychology (e.g., *grit*, *emotional intelligence*, *optimism*) suggests a continued interest in character qualities, even if the spiritual lexicon is not overtly employed.

Language is not destiny, but it constitutes a mirror of what we esteem and how we make sense of existence.

## 5. Conclusion

The linguistic trends of the past century reveal a significant cultural recalibration. The *Fruits of the Spirit*—once common parlance for virtues to cultivate—have receded in usage and been reframed in definition to accommodate a more secular, individualistic age. Meanwhile, psychological and self-help vocabulary flourishes, often carrying its own implicit values (personal growth, healing, empowerment). This may represent a form of **semantic entropy** wherein our language for virtue and vice becomes less sharply defined and less commonly shared. Some observers warn that this precedes a loss of social cohesion—if we no longer have words like *grace* or *forgiveness* on our lips, we may find it more difficult to practice or collectively prioritize them (Brooks, 2015; Merritt, 2018). The drift from a moral-spiritual lexicon to a therapeutic-individualistic one is more than a linguistic curiosity; it encapsulates a broader shift in **cultural focus from the communal quest for virtue to the individual quest for wellness and identity**.

Whether this shift leads to a net loss of "social glue" or simply a new form of it remains an open question. What is clear is that anyone comparing the discourse of 1900 to that of 2025 can observe and *quantitatively* trace the transformation. In the words of one commentator, "Sacred words and moral terms are vanishing… Here's why it matters" (Merritt, 2018). It matters because words shape our thinking, and as our words change, so do the stories we tell about who we are and what kind of society we aspire to build.
