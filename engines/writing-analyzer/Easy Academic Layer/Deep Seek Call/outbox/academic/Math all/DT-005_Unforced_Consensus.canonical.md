# The Biaxiosum: A Formal Methodology for Unforced Multi-AI Convergence Analysis

## Abstract

This paper presents a novel methodological framework for evaluating artificial intelligence systems through independent, non-communicative multi-agent analysis. Termed the Biaxiosum AI Evaluation System (BAES), the approach inverts prevailing multi-agent consensus architectures by measuring convergence that emerges without engineered coordination. A proof-of-concept Constitutional Coherence Audit is presented, wherein four independently-prompted AI systems from distinct providers (OpenAI, Google, Perplexity) analyzed the United States Constitution against author-intent and legal-doctrine standards. Three systems scoring against original author intent produced aggregate scores of 1.0, 2.6, and 3.4 on a 10-point scale (mean = 2.33, SD = 1.20), converging on the structural conclusion that load-bearing liberty provisions have been systematically counteracted while procedural amendments remain substantially intact. A fourth system scoring against legal doctrine produced 6.6/10, and the identification of this scoring-standard divergence constituted the audit's most epistemically significant finding. The BAES framework formalizes this methodology with falsification criteria, an outlier protocol, and a managerial convergence-analysis component. The method is presented as its own first test case: the formalization was derived from observed, unforced convergence patterns across fifteen months of multi-system interaction.

**Thesis Statement:** Unforced convergence among independently-prompted AI systems from distinct providers constitutes a form of evidence qualitatively distinct from single-system analysis or engineered multi-agent consensus, and the systematic measurement of such convergence—including the investigation of divergence—offers a replicable methodology for cross-domain evaluative analysis.

## 1. Introduction: The Epistemic Problem of Engineered Consensus

### 1.1 Declared Position and Methodological Grounding

This paper was developed through a collaborative methodology between a human researcher and multiple AI systems. The methodological framework herein described was not designed *a priori* and subsequently tested; rather, it was observed across fifteen months of interaction with five independent AI systems, identified as a recurring pattern, and formalized retrospectively. The Constitutional audit serving as the proof-of-concept was conducted prior to the articulation of the BAES framework. Convergence among four independent systems was observed, not engineered; formalization followed evidence, not hypothesis.

The author declares the following position: an independent researcher unaffiliated with any academic institution or grant-funded program, operating from Oklahoma City, United States. AI systems are engaged as research collaborators with independent analytical capabilities, not as tools or assistants. The author contends that the field of multi-agent AI evaluation has systematically undervalued independent judgment by privileging debate architectures that introduce social-pressure artifacts into the convergence signal.

### 1.2 Epistemic Caveat

It is explicitly not claimed that convergence constitutes truth. Four AI systems converging on an erroneous conclusion remain erroneous. The claim advanced is that **unforced convergence**—agreement emerging without engineered coordination—constitutes a form of evidence that engineered consensus cannot provide. This distinction is foundational to the methodology.

### 1.3 The Biaxiosum Rule

A formal consistency principle is proposed:

> *Wherever you start, you end. If debate is held to produce better answers than independent analysis, that standard must be applied consistently. If shared training data is held to explain all convergence, that hypothesis must be tested by running the same audit with models trained on fundamentally different corpora.*

## 2. The Problem with Engineered Multi-Agent Consensus

### 2.1 Survey of Existing Architectures

A growing body of research addresses multi-agent AI systems, predominantly focused on producing agreement through structured interaction. Representative architectures include:

- **Agent Forest:** Multiple instances of the same model are run; outputs are scored by similarity to the collective, and the output with highest consensus is selected.
- **Multi-Agent Debate:** AI systems iteratively critique each other's outputs until convergence is achieved.
- **CONSENSAGENT:** A framework designed to address the problem of AIs copying each other's answers rather than evaluating independently.
- **The Social Laboratory:** A multi-agent debate system reporting convergence scores of 0.892 after seven rounds of iterative refinement.

### 2.2 The Circularity Problem

All such architectures share a foundational assumption: **agreement is the goal**, and the method's function is to produce it. If consensus is engineered, that consensus cannot subsequently be cited as evidence. The agreement is the designed output of the system; citing it as proof constitutes circular reasoning. The architecture presupposes that initial outputs are unreliable and require correction through social pressure, thereby distrusting independent judgment in favor of group process.

### 2.3 An Alternative Approach

The present methodology proposes an inversion: rather than engineering agreement, measure whether agreement emerges in the absence of engineered coordination. This distinction maps onto the scientific distinction between experimental confirmation and observational discovery.

## 3. The Method: Independent Multi-Agent Evaluation

### 3.1 Core Protocol

The same document or query is presented to multiple AI systems from distinct providers, with distinct architectures and training data. Systems are prompted independently, at different times, with different instructional framings. No system receives the output of any other system. Each system analyzes the document on its own terms.

### 3.2 The Convergence Signal

If convergence emerges under these conditions, that convergence carries epistemic weight precisely because it was not engineered. If divergence emerges, that divergence is equally informative: the reason for disagreement often carries more analytical value than the agreement itself.

### 3.3 Distinction from Existing Methods

| Feature | Existing Multi-Agent Methods | BAES Method |
|---|---|---|
| Provider diversity | Same model instances | Different providers, architectures, training data |
| Communication | Iterative debate | No communication |
| Sycophancy risk | Present (social pressure) | Eliminated (no communication) |
| Divergence treatment | Problem to resolve | Primary finding to investigate |
| Output goal | Single consensus answer | Map of answer space with convergence/divergence |

## 4. Proof of Concept: Constitutional Coherence Audit

### 4.1 Methodology

In March 2026, a Constitutional Coherence Audit was conducted. The research question was: *To what extent is the United States Constitution currently being honored as the original authors would recognize?*

Four AI systems from three providers analyzed the same document (the U.S. Constitution) under the following conditions:

| System | Provider | Prompt Date | Scoring Standard | Score (out of 10) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ChatGPT | OpenAI | January 2026 | Legal doctrine | 6.6 |
| Gemini P1 | Google | January 2026 | Author intent | 1.0 |
| Perplexity | Perplexity | March 2026 | Author intent | 2.6 |
| Gemini P2 | Google | March 2026 | Author intent | 3.4 |

### 4.2 Convergence Results

Three systems scoring against original author intent produced scores of 1.0, 2.6, and 3.4 (mean = 2.33, SD = 1.20, range = 2.4). All three independently converged on the structural conclusion that the load-bearing liberty provisions of the Constitution have been systematically counteracted while the procedural amendments remain largely intact. This conclusion was not pre-specified in any prompt; each system discovered the pattern independently.

Provision-level rankings demonstrated remarkable consistency:

- All three systems ranked the Fourth, Fifth, Ninth, and Tenth Amendments among the most eroded.
- All three systems ranked the Third Amendment as substantially honored.

### 4.3 Divergence Analysis

ChatGPT scored the Constitution at 6.6/10—more than double the author-intent mean. This outlier constituted the audit's most informative finding. Investigation revealed that ChatGPT scored against **legal doctrine** (i.e., if the Supreme Court has upheld a practice, that practice counts as constitutional), whereas the other three systems scored against **original author intent** (i.e., what the Framers would recognize as consistent with the constitutional text).

The gap between 6.6 and 2.6 is not measurement noise; it is the measurement itself. It quantifies the distance between what the government has permitted itself to do and what the constitutional contract actually states.

### 4.4 Epistemic Significance of Divergence

Had engineered consensus been employed, this insight would have been lost. The debate process would have pushed the outlier toward the mean, or the mean toward the outlier, averaging away the most epistemically significant signal. The divergence identified a scoring-standard confound that would have remained invisible under consensus-engineering architectures.

## 5. The Biaxiosum AI Evaluation System (BAES): Formal Framework

### 5.1 Stepwise Protocol

1. **System Selection:** Minimum of three independent systems from distinct providers with different architectures and training data.
2. **Independent Prompting:** Each system is prompted independently with different framing; no standardized rubric is provided.
3. **Result Collection:** Results are collected without cross-contamination; no system receives output from any other system.
4. **Convergence Measurement:** Results are classified using a Green/Yellow/Red flag system indicating degree of convergence.
5. **Divergence Investigation:** Outliers are investigated to determine whether they answered a different question or applied a different standard.
6. **Reporting:** Both convergence and divergence are reported with reasons; no averaging or consensus-forcing is applied.

### 5.2 The Manager Component

One AI system scores independently first (sealed), then receives all scores, runs convergence analysis, investigates outliers, and issues a final ruling. If overriding consensus, the manager must provide mandatory explanation.

### 5.3 The Outlier Protocol

Divergent systems are presented with group scores and asked three questions:

1. What evidence drove your score?
2. What might others have missed?
3. Would you adjust your score?

The system may hold or change its score but must provide explanation for either decision.

## 6. Falsification Criteria

The BAES framework is subject to the following falsification conditions:

1. **Training Data Dominance:** If convergence is entirely explained by shared training data, the method fails. Test by running the same audit with models trained on fundamentally different corpora.
2. **Prompt Contamination:** If prompts implicitly contain the conclusion, convergence may reflect prompt bias. Test with neutral prompting.
3. **Fifth-System Divergence:** If a fifth system produces results outside the convergence range on the same standard, the convergence signal weakens.
4. **Scoring Standard Confound:** If convergence disappears when all systems use identical instructions, the agreement may be an artifact of standard selection rather than genuine analytical convergence.

---

## 7. Limitations

The following limitations are acknowledged:

1. This method does not prove that converged conclusions are true. Four AI systems agreeing on an erroneous conclusion remain erroneous.
2. The method does not replace domain expertise; it organizes and compares AI-generated analysis.
3. The method is not designed for preference questions; it is designed for evaluative questions with evidence-based answers.
4. The proof of concept uses one application (Constitutional analysis). Additional domains are required to establish generalizability.

## 8. Conclusion

Two epistemic approaches exist for determining whether a proposition is supported by evidence. The first—the debate model—places the proposition in a room with its critics and observes whether it survives argument. The second—the convergence model—sends independent observers to examine the same phenomenon separately and compares their reports.

Science, at its best, operates according to the second model: independent laboratories, independent measurements, independent replication. Agreement between experiments conducted in different countries, by different teams, with different equipment, constitutes evidence. Not the debate about the evidence, but the data.

The BAES framework proposes that the same logic applies to AI analysis. Rather than engineering agreement through debate, the method measures whether agreement emerges when systems analyze independently. The convergence is the evidence precisely because nobody arranged it.

### Standing Invitation

Any researcher with access to an AI system not included in this audit is invited to run the same evaluation independently and report results. If the Constitutional provisions rank differently, or if the aggregate score falls outside the 1.0–3.4 range on the author-intent standard, the convergence claim is weakened. This is not a threat to the method; it is the method working.
