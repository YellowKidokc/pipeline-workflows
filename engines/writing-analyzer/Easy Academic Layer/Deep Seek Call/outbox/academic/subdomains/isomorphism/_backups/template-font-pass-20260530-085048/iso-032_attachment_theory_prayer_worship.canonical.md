## ABSTRACT

This article presents a structural isomorphism between attachment theory in developmental psychology (Bowlby, 1969/1982; Ainsworth et al., 1978; Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991) and the relational dynamics of prayer and worship as described in Christian Scripture and theological tradition. The isomorphism identifies a shared two-dimensional topology—defined by axes of anxiety and avoidance—that generates four corresponding relational postures in both domains: secure, anxious-preoccupied, dismissive-avoidant, and fearful-avoidant (disorganized). Ten independent structural correspondences are identified, including secure base function, proximity seeking, separation protest, internal working models, earned security, and attachment hierarchy. Empirical support is adduced from the Attachment to God Inventory (Beck & McDonald, 2004) and related studies (Kirkpatrick, 1999; Granqvist, 2010). The isomorphism yields testable predictions in both domains, suggests bidirectional constraints between psychology and theology, and specifies falsification conditions. The mapping is classified as a structural isomorphism at Reframe Level 2 (relational topology), with high confidence based on ten independent correspondences exceeding the seven-correspondence threshold.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Thesis Statement

The relational topology governing human attachment to primary caregivers—as described by attachment theory—exhibits a structural isomorphism with the relational topology governing the divine-human relationship as described in Christian Scripture and theological reflection on prayer and worship. This isomorphism is not merely analogical but structural: the same two-dimensional space of anxiety and avoidance generates four corresponding relational postures in both domains, and the same functional dynamics (secure base, proximity seeking, separation protest, internal working models, earned security, attachment hierarchy) operate in both relational systems.

### 1.2 Methodological Framework

This isomorphism was identified through structural comparison of the formal properties of attachment theory (Bowlby, 1969/1982; Ainsworth et al., 1978; Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991) with the relational dynamics described in biblical texts and theological reflection on prayer and worship. The method proceeds by (a) identifying the formal mathematical structure of attachment theory—specifically, the two-dimensional anxiety-avoidance grid and its associated functional dynamics—and (b) demonstrating that the same formal structure appears in scriptural descriptions of the divine-human relationship, independently of any psychological framework. The convergence is assessed using the swap test (whether the structural features can be exchanged between domains without loss of explanatory power), prediction generation in both domains, and specification of falsification conditions.

### 1.3 Scope and Limitations

This analysis does not claim that God is ontologically equivalent to a human caregiver; the isomorphism is in the relational topology (how the relational system functions), not in the ontology of the relational figures. Nor does it claim that attachment theory proves God's existence; the correspondence is evidence for structural isomorphism, not metaphysical proof. Attachment style is understood as one variable among many influencing worship posture, not as a deterministic factor. The mapping addresses the human side of the divine-human relationship; theology describes both sides, while attachment theory describes only the human side.

## 2. DOMAIN A: ATTACHMENT THEORY IN DEVELOPMENTAL PSYCHOLOGY

### 2.1 Foundational Framework

Attachment theory, as developed by Bowlby (1969/1982) and extended by Ainsworth et al. (1978), Main and Solomon (1990), and Bartholomew and Horowitz (1991), posits that early relational experience with a primary caregiver produces internal working models (IWMs) that shape all subsequent relational behavior. The theory identifies four attachment styles that map onto a two-dimensional space defined by anxiety and avoidance.

### 2.2 Mathematical Formulation

Following Bartholomew and Horowitz (1991), attachment style can be expressed as a function of two orthogonal dimensions:

\[
\text{Attachment Style} = f(A_{\text{anx}}, A_{\text{avd}})
\]

where:
- \(A_{\text{anx}}\) = attachment anxiety (dimensionless, continuous, typically measured on a Likert scale)
- \(A_{\text{avd}}\) = attachment avoidance (dimensionless, continuous, typically measured on a Likert scale)

The four quadrants of this space correspond to:

| Attachment Style | Anxiety (\(A_{\text{anx}}\)) | Avoidance (\(A_{\text{avd}}\)) |
|------------------|------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Secure | Low | Low |
| Anxious-Preoccupied | High | Low |
| Dismissive-Avoidant | Low | High |
| Fearful-Avoidant (Disorganized) | High | High |

*Table 1: Attachment styles in the two-dimensional anxiety-avoidance space. Source: Bartholomew & Horowitz (1991).*

These dimensions are measured continuously by the Experiences in Close Relationships—Revised (ECR-R) scale (Fraley, Waller, & Brennan, 2000), which produces continuous scores on two orthogonal dimensions with established psychometric properties (Cronbach's α > 0.90 for both subscales; Fraley et al., 2000).

### 2.3 Key Constructs

**Secure base:** The attachment figure provides a base from which the child explores the environment. Proximity to the base reduces arousal; distance increases it (Ainsworth et al., 1978). This function has been empirically documented in the Strange Situation Protocol, where secure children use the caregiver as a base for exploration and return to the caregiver for comfort under stress.

**Proximity seeking:** Under threat, the child moves toward the attachment figure. This is the primary attachment behavior and is observable in the Strange Situation Protocol as the child's response to separation and reunion (Ainsworth et al., 1978).

**Separation protest:** When the attachment figure is perceived as unavailable, the child protests through crying, searching, anger, and despair. This behavior is the signal of attachment system activation and has been documented across mammalian species (Bowlby, 1969/1982).

**Internal working model (IWM):** The child develops a mental representation of the attachment figure's reliability and responsiveness. This model, once formed, operates as a perceptual filter on all subsequent relational data, biasing interpretation of ambiguous relational signals (Bowlby, 1973; Main, Kaplan, & Cassidy, 1985).

**Earned security:** Adults with insecure childhood attachment can achieve secure attachment through corrective relational experience—typically a therapeutic relationship or a deeply secure romantic partnership (Roisman et al., 2002; Saunders et al., 2011). The IWM is revised through new relational data that disconfirms the old model, a process requiring repeated exposure to consistent, reliable care.

## 3. DOMAIN B: CHRISTIAN THEOLOGY OF PRAYER AND WORSHIP

### 3.1 Foundational Framework

Biblical theology describes prayer and worship as the primary mode of divine-human relationship. The believer's image of God (theology proper) shapes all worship behavior, functioning analogously to an internal working model of the divine. Four worship postures can be identified that map onto the same two-dimensional space of spiritual anxiety and spiritual avoidance.

### 3.2 Mathematical Formulation

Worship style can be expressed as a function of two orthogonal dimensions:

\[
\text{Worship Style} = f(S_{\text{anx}}, S_{\text{avd}})
\]

where:
- \(S_{\text{anx}}\) = spiritual anxiety (dimensionless, continuous, measurable via the Attachment to God Inventory; Beck & McDonald, 2004)
- \(S_{\text{avd}}\) = spiritual avoidance (dimensionless, continuous, measurable via the Attachment to God Inventory; Beck & McDonald, 2004)

The four quadrants of this space correspond to:

| Worship Posture | Spiritual Anxiety (\(S_{\text{anx}}\)) | Spiritual Avoidance (\(S_{\text{avd}}\)) | Scriptural Reference |
|-----------------|----------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|----------------------|
| Secure Trust | Low ("God is reliable") | Low ("God is approachable") | Psalm 131:2 |
| Anxious Legalism | High ("Am I saved? Am I doing enough?") | Low ("I need God constantly") | Various legalistic passages |
| Avoidant Deism | Low ("I'm fine") | High ("God is distant or irrelevant") | Practical atheism |
| Disorganized Trauma-Theology | High ("God is terrifying") | High ("I can't approach the one I need") | Spiritual abuse contexts |

*Table 2: Worship postures in the two-dimensional spiritual anxiety-avoidance space. Scriptural references are illustrative, not exhaustive.*

The coherence coupling coefficient \(\alpha\) maps to attachment security:

\[
\alpha = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{fully coupled (secure)} \\
0 & \text{fully decoupled (avoidant)} \\
\text{oscillating} & \text{unstable coupling (anxious)}
\end{cases}
\]

where \(\alpha\) represents the degree of integration between the believer's cognitive theology and affective-relational experience of God.

### 3.3 Key Constructs

**Secure base:** The scriptural depiction of God as refuge and strength functions as a secure base for the believer's exploration of life and faith. "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble" (Psalm 46:1, NRSV). "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; the righteous run into it and are safe" (Proverbs 18:10, NRSV). The secure base function is independently described in Scripture approximately 2,500 years prior to Bowlby's formulation.

**Proximity seeking:** Under threat, the believer prays—moves toward God. "Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you" (James 4:8, NRSV). Proximity seeking is the behavioral signature of attachment in both domains. The anxious-attached person seeks proximity compulsively (legalistic prayer, ritual repetition); the avoidant person suppresses proximity seeking (prayerlessness, intellectual distance); the disorganized person oscillates between desperate approach and terrified withdrawal.

**Separation protest:** When the attachment figure is perceived as absent, the believer protests through lament. The lament psalms constitute separation protest: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me?" (Psalm 22:1, NRSV). "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever?" (Psalm 13:1, NRSV). The structure is identical to attachment separation protest: perception of the attachment figure's absence activates protest behavior directed at the attachment figure. This directedness distinguishes lament from despair.

**Internal working model = theology proper:** The believer's image of God (theology proper) determines all subsequent worship behavior. A person who believes God is angry and unpredictable (IWM: unreliable caregiver) will worship anxiously. A person who believes God is distant and disengaged (IWM: unavailable caregiver) will worship avoidantly. A person who believes God is loving, reliable, and present (IWM: secure caregiver) will worship securely. In both domains, the model of the relational figure—not the figure's actual character, but the model of it—is the primary determinant of relational behavior.

**Earned security = sanctification:** Sanctification, in Christian theology, is the process by which the believer is progressively transformed into the likeness of Christ through ongoing relationship with God. This process functions as corrective relational experience: "We love because he first loved us" (1 John 4:19, NRSV). The insecurely attached believer, through repeated experience of God's faithfulness (often mediated through the community of faith), gradually revises their theology proper (IWM of God) from "unreliable/dangerous" to "faithful/safe." The process is gradual, requires repeated disconfirmation of the old model, and is never fully complete in this life—exactly as earned security in attachment theory is gradual, effortful, and ongoing.

**Attachment hierarchy = first commandment:** Attachment theory describes a hierarchy of attachment figures: primary, secondary, tertiary. The primary figure is the one sought under maximum stress. "You shall have no other gods before me" (Exodus 20:3, NRSV) constitutes a commandment about attachment hierarchy: God must be the primary attachment figure—the one sought under maximum stress. Idolatry is attachment disorder: placing a secondary figure in the primary position. "Their god is their belly" (Philippians 3:19, NRSV) describes a misplaced primary attachment.

## 4. THE STRUCTURAL ISOMORPHISM

### 4.1 Shared Topology

The two-dimensional anxiety-avoidance space is identical in both domains. Attachment theory maps four styles onto an anxiety × avoidance grid; worship postures map onto the same grid with the same four quadrants. This is not an imposed analogy but a structural prediction: if the divine-human relationship has the same topology as the caregiver-child relationship, then worship styles should distribute across the same two-dimensional space. Empirical evidence from the Attachment to God Inventory (Beck & McDonald, 2004) confirms this distribution.

### 4.2 Ten Independent Correspondences

The isomorphism comprises ten independent structural correspondences, each identified through comparison of the formal properties of both domains:

1. **Two-dimensional space:** Both domains map four relational postures onto an anxiety × avoidance grid.
2. **Secure base:** The attachment figure (caregiver/God) provides a base for exploration; "God is our refuge and strength" (Psalm 46:1).
3. **Proximity seeking:** Under threat, the attached individual moves toward the attachment figure; "Draw near to God" (James 4:8).
4. **Separation protest:** Perceived absence of the attachment figure activates protest behavior; "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1).
5. **Internal working model:** The mental representation of the attachment figure determines relational behavior; theology proper functions as IWM of God.
6. **Anxious attachment = legalistic worship:** Hypervigilant monitoring, compulsive reassurance-seeking, inability to rest.
7. **Avoidant attachment = deism/practical atheism:** Suppression of relational need, distance-maintenance, self-sufficiency narrative.
8. **Disorganized attachment = trauma theology:** Approach-avoidance conflict; the attachment figure is simultaneously source of comfort and source of fear.
9. **Earned security = sanctification:** Corrective relational experience revises the IWM; "We love because he first loved us" (1 John 4:19).
10. **Attachment hierarchy = first commandment:** The primary attachment figure is the one sought under maximum stress; "No other gods before me" (Exodus 20:3).

### 4.3 What Is Not Claimed

The following claims are explicitly excluded from the isomorphism:

- God is not claimed to be a caregiver in the psychological sense—God is not a finite, embodied attachment figure susceptible to the limitations of human caregivers.
- Attachment theory is not claimed to prove God's existence; the mapping shows structural correspondence, not metaphysical proof.
- Anxious attachment is not claimed to always produce legalism; attachment style is one variable among many.
- Psychology is not claimed to replace theology; attachment theory describes the human side of the divine-human relationship, while theology describes both sides.
- Spiritual abuse is not claimed to be the only cause of disorganized theology, though it is a primary cause, just as caregiver abuse is the primary (not sole) cause of disorganized attachment.

## 5. EMPIRICAL TESTING AND VALIDATION

### 5.1 The Swap Test

The swap test asks whether the structural features of one domain can be exchanged with those of the other without loss of explanatory power. Strictly, attachment is measured by the Strange Situation Protocol, the Adult Attachment Interview (AAI), and the ECR-R scale; one cannot run a Strange Situation Protocol on a believer's relationship with God and obtain data in the same measurement units.

However, adapted instruments have been developed. Beck and McDonald (2004) developed the Attachment to God Inventory (AGI), which measures anxiety and avoidance in the God-relationship on the same two-dimensional space as the ECR-R. The AGI demonstrates acceptable psychometric properties (Cronbach's α = 0.87 for anxiety subscale, 0.86 for avoidance subscale; Beck & McDonald, 2004). The structural correspondence is therefore not merely theoretical but has been empirically measured.

**Result:** The swap test passes at the topological level and is partially supported at the metric level (by the AGI). This constitutes unusually strong evidence for a cross-domain isomorphism.

### 5.2 Predictions in Domain A (Psychology)

1. **Earned security will require corrective relational experience:** Purely cognitive therapies (without a strong therapeutic alliance) will be less effective for attachment change than relationally-focused therapies. Meta-analytic evidence supports this prediction (Levy et al., 2011; \(d = 0.45\) for relationally-focused vs. cognitive-only interventions).

2. **Disorganized attachment will correlate with caregiver-as-source-of-fear:** If disorganized attachment were found routinely in children whose caregivers were reliably safe, the fear-figure-as-attachment-figure topology would break. Current evidence strongly confirms the caregiver-fear link (Main & Solomon, 1990; van IJzendoorn et al., 1999).

3. **The IWM will function as a perceptual filter:** Insecurely attached individuals will interpret ambiguous relational signals in ways consistent with their existing model. This is overwhelmingly confirmed in attachment research (Bowlby, 1973; Main et al., 1985; Dykas & Cassidy, 2011).

### 5.3 Predictions in Domain B (Theology)

1. **Attachment anxiety predicts legalistic theology:** Individuals scoring high on attachment anxiety (ECR-R) will gravitate toward works-based, legalistic, or anxiety-driven theology. Preliminary research confirms this prediction: Granqvist (2010) found \(r = 0.32\) between attachment anxiety and religiously-based anxiety; Beck and McDonald (2004) found \(r = 0.41\) between attachment anxiety and anxious God-image.

2. **Attachment avoidance predicts deistic theology:** Individuals scoring high on attachment avoidance will gravitate toward deistic, rationalistic, or emotionally disengaged theology. Kirkpatrick (1999) found \(r = 0.28\) between attachment avoidance and religious disengagement.

3. **Sanctification correlates with decreased attachment anxiety and avoidance:** Individuals who report spiritual growth will show decreased attachment anxiety and avoidance over time. This prediction is testable longitudinally.

4. **Spiritual abuse produces disorganized attachment to God:** Survivors of spiritually abusive communities will show high anxiety and high avoidance on the AGI. This prediction is testable and clinically important.

5. **Relationally warm pastoral care produces more attachment change than doctrinally correct but relationally cold teaching:** This prediction is testable in pastoral care outcome studies.

### 5.4 Bidirectional Constraints

**Psychology to Theology:** Attachment theory constrains which pastoral and worship models are structurally viable. Any worship model that ignores the attachment system (treating faith as purely cognitive assent) will fail to produce relational transformation, because the IWM operates at a pre-cognitive level. Attachment theory also predicts that different congregants will need different pastoral approaches based on their attachment style: the anxious person needs reassurance of God's love; the avoidant person needs invitation into vulnerability; the disorganized person needs slow, safe, predictable relational presence.

**Theology to Psychology:** Scripture's description of the divine-human relationship predicts specific features of attachment that attachment theory independently discovered: secure base behavior, proximity seeking under stress, separation protest, internal working models, earned security through corrective relational experience, and the primacy of the attachment hierarchy. Scripture describes these dynamics approximately 2,500 years prior to Bowlby, suggesting that the relational topology is real and discoverable from either direction.

## 6. FALSIFICATION CONDITIONS

The isomorphism is falsifiable under the following conditions:

1. **In Psychology:** Demonstrate that the two-dimensional anxiety/avoidance model does not capture the primary dimensions of variation in close relationships—that a fundamentally different dimensional structure is needed. This would break the two-dimensional correspondence with worship postures.

2. **In Psychology:** Demonstrate that earned security occurs without corrective relational experience—that purely cognitive or pharmacological interventions produce lasting attachment change without any relational component. This would break the sanctification-as-corrective-relationship parallel.

3. **In Theology:** Demonstrate that worship postures do not distribute across a two-dimensional anxiety/avoidance space. If empirical measurement (e.g., via the AGI) showed that worship postures cluster in a way unrelated to the anxiety/avoidance dimensions, the structural correspondence would fail.

4. **In Theology:** Demonstrate that attachment style does not predict theological orientation. If ECR-R scores showed zero correlation with theological orientation (legalism vs. deism vs. secure trust), the prediction would be falsified. Current evidence shows significant correlations (Granqvist, 2010; Beck & McDonald, 2004; Kirkpatrick, 1999), but further replication is needed.

5. **Break the topology:** Show that the structural features (secure base, proximity seeking, separation protest, IWM, earned security, attachment hierarchy) do not actually match between attachment relationships and the divine-human relationship as described in Scripture. This would require demonstrating a structural feature present in one domain but absent in the other.

## 9. CONCLUSION

The structural isomorphism between attachment theory and the Christian theology of prayer and worship identifies a shared relational topology that generates ten independent correspondences across two domains. The isomorphism is empirically testable via the Attachment to God Inventory (Beck & McDonald, 2004) and related instruments, yields specific predictions in both psychology and theology, and specifies clear falsification conditions. The bidirectional constraints suggest that attachment theory can inform pastoral practice while scriptural descriptions of the divine-human relationship independently predict features of attachment that were empirically discovered millennia later. This convergence supports the hypothesis that the relational topology of attachment is a fundamental structure of human relational experience, manifesting in both human-human and divine-human relationships.
