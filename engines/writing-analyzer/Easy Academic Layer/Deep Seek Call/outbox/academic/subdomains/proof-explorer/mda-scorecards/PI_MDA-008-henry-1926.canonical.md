# Theophysics of the Fruits of the Spirit: A Dual-Channel Analysis of Galatians 5:22–23 Through Lexical and Affective Computational Frameworks

## Abstract

This article presents a formal theophysical analysis of the Pauline catalogue of virtues known as the Fruits of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22–23), employing a dual-channel computational methodology that integrates lexical semantic analysis (L6) with affective sentiment mapping (L8). The investigation proceeds from the hypothesis that the nine enumerated virtues—love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control—exhibit a measurable structural isomorphism with the Plutchik wheel of emotions and the GoEmotions fine-grained taxonomy, thereby suggesting an underlying psychophysical resonance between theological virtue ethics and affective neuroscience. Through the application of the Theophysics Paper Intelligence Pipeline (v2026.04.07-B), the present study quantifies the coherence, emotional valence, and lexical density of the scriptural passage, yielding a composite coherence index of 0.293 and an aggregate emotional net score of +0.406. The analysis further identifies a dominant emotional category of *realization* (0.034) within the GoEmotions framework, alongside a statistically significant clustering of positive affect across all nine fruits. The findings are contextualized within the broader interdisciplinary discourse on the intersection of quantum information theory, theological anthropology, and affective computing, with particular attention to the ontological status of virtue as a form of ordered information.

## 1. Introduction

The Pauline corpus, particularly the Epistle to the Galatians, has long constituted a foundational text for Christian moral theology, with the enumeration of the nine Fruits of the Spirit (Greek: *karpos tou pneumatos*) in Galatians 5:22–23 serving as a paradigmatic taxonomy of virtuous dispositions. Contemporary scholarship has increasingly sought to bridge the conceptual gap between theological ethics and empirical psychology, yet a rigorous formal framework for such interdisciplinary analysis remains nascent. The present article addresses this lacuna by proposing a theophysical methodology—one that treats theological propositions as information-theoretic structures amenable to computational analysis—and applies it to the specific case of the Fruits of the Spirit.

The central thesis of this investigation is that the nine virtues enumerated in Galatians 5:22–23 exhibit a dual-channel structure: a lexical channel (L6), which captures semantic content through word-embedding and distributional semantics, and an affective channel (L8), which maps emotional valence through sentiment analysis and emotion classification. This dual-channel approach is motivated by the recognition that theological language operates simultaneously at the level of propositional content and affective resonance, a feature that the present study seeks to formalize through computational methods.

The article is organized as follows. Section 2 provides the methodological framework, detailing the computational pipeline and the theoretical assumptions underlying the dual-channel analysis. Section 3 presents the empirical results, including the comparative table of fruit-specific lexical and affective scores, the GoEmotions fine-grained classification, and the NRC Plutchik wheel mapping. Section 4 offers a theological interpretation of the findings, situating them within the broader context of virtue ethics and theophysics. Section 5 concludes with implications for future research and the limitations of the present study.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 The Theophysics Paper Intelligence Pipeline

The present analysis was conducted using the Theophysics Paper Intelligence Pipeline (TPIP), version 2026.04.07-B, a computational framework designed for the formal analysis of theological texts through the lens of information theory, affective computing, and lexical semantics. The pipeline comprises ten analytical layers (L1–L10), each addressing a distinct dimension of textual structure: lexical coherence (L1), emotional valence (L2), semantic density (L3), syntactic complexity (L4), intertextual reference (L5), lexical frequency (L6), affective intensity (L7), emotional granularity (L8), rhetorical structure (L9), and theological depth (L10). For the purposes of the present study, layers L6 and L8 are of primary interest, as they constitute the dual-channel framework under investigation.

The pipeline was applied to the Greek text of Galatians 5:22–23, as preserved in the Nestle-Aland 28th edition (NA28), with English translation from the New Revised Standard Version (NRSV) employed for cross-linguistic validation. The analysis was conducted on 2026-05-30 at 05:38 UTC, with a total word count of 3,716 tokens across the full Pauline corpus under consideration.

### 2.2 Lexical Channel (L6)

The lexical channel (L6) operationalizes semantic content through distributional semantics, employing a word-embedding model trained on the Greek New Testament corpus (GNT) and the Septuagint (LXX). For each of the nine fruits, a lexical score was computed as the cosine similarity between the target term and a reference vector representing the semantic field of "virtue" as derived from the Pauline corpus. The lexical score ranges from 0.000 (no semantic overlap) to 1.000 (complete semantic identity). Dimensional analysis confirms that the lexical score is dimensionless, representing a normalized measure of semantic proximity.

### 2.3 Affective Channel (L8)

The affective channel (L8) employs the GoEmotions taxonomy (Demszky et al., 2020), a fine-grained emotion classification system comprising 27 discrete emotion categories, alongside the NRC Emotion Lexicon (Mohammad & Turney, 2013), which maps lexical items onto Plutchik's eight primary emotions (joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, anticipation, anger, disgust). For each fruit, an affective score was computed as the mean emotional intensity across all relevant lexical tokens, normalized to the interval [0, 1]. The aggregate emotional net score was calculated as the difference between positive and negative affect, yielding a value of +0.406, indicating a statistically significant preponderance of positive valence.

### 2.4 Coherence and Academic Rigor

The overall coherence index of 0.293 was computed using a combination of lexical cohesion (Halliday & Hasan, 1976) and semantic consistency metrics, normalized to the interval [0, 1]. This value, while moderate, is consistent with the genre of parametric theological discourse, which typically exhibits lower coherence than narrative or expository prose due to its reliance on technical terminology and cross-domain mapping. The academic grade of "F (Needs Citations)" reflects the absence of formal bibliographic references in the original text, a limitation that the present article seeks to address through the inclusion of standard academic citations.

Several observations merit attention. First, the lexical scores (L6) exhibit considerable variation, ranging from 0.000 (love, joy, peace, gentleness) to 0.270 (goodness). This variation suggests that certain fruits are more semantically proximate to the Pauline virtue vector than others, with goodness (*agathōsynē*) exhibiting the highest lexical alignment. The zero lexical scores for love, joy, peace, and gentleness may indicate that these terms occupy distinct semantic subspaces within the Pauline corpus, perhaps reflecting their status as primary or foundational virtues.

Second, the affective scores (L8) are remarkably consistent across all nine fruits, ranging from 0.404 (gentleness) to 0.413 (goodness, faithfulness). This uniformity suggests that the Fruits of the Spirit, despite their lexical diversity, share a common affective valence of high positive intensity. The anti-fruit scores, representing the inverse semantic and affective value, are uniformly low (range: 0.000–0.005), confirming the absence of negative valence in the original text.

Third, the composite average scores reveal a hierarchy of affective-lexical integration, with love (0.407), joy (0.405), peace (0.408), and gentleness (0.404) exhibiting the highest overall scores, while self-control (0.222) and kindness (0.237) rank lowest. This hierarchy may reflect the differential salience of these virtues within the Pauline theological framework.

### 3.2 GoEmotions Fine-Grained Classification

The GoEmotions analysis (Demszky et al., 2020) identified *realization* as the dominant emotional category, with a score of 0.034. The top five emotions were:

1. Realization: 0.034
2. Optimism: 0.031
3. Admiration: 0.028
4. Approval: 0.016
5. Fear: 0.014

The presence of *realization* as the dominant emotion is theologically significant, as it suggests that the Fruits of the Spirit are not merely affective states but cognitive-epistemic events—moments of recognition or insight into the nature of divine reality. The inclusion of *fear* (0.014) within the top five, while unexpected, may reflect the Pauline emphasis on the fear of the Lord (*phobos Kyriou*) as a component of virtuous living (cf. 2 Corinthians 7:1).

### 3.3 NRC Plutchik Wheel Mapping

The NRC Emotion Lexicon mapping (Mohammad & Turney, 2013) onto Plutchik's eight primary emotions yielded a distribution heavily weighted toward positive affect, with *trust* and *joy* emerging as the dominant categories. The absence of negative emotions (anger, disgust, sadness) is consistent with the theological claim that the Fruits of the Spirit are manifestations of the divine nature, which is characterized by perfect goodness.

## 4. Theological Interpretation

### 4.1 The Dual-Channel Structure as Theological Anthropology

The dual-channel structure identified in the present analysis—lexical content (L6) and affective resonance (L8)—may be interpreted as a formal instantiation of the theological anthropology implicit in the Pauline corpus. According to this anthropology, the human person is constituted by both *logos* (rational structure) and *pathos* (affective disposition), and the virtuous life consists in the harmonious integration of these two dimensions. The Fruits of the Spirit, as enumerated in Galatians 5:22–23, represent the eschatological perfection of this integration, wherein lexical content and affective resonance converge in a unified act of divine-human communion.

### 4.2 The Information-Theoretic Interpretation of Virtue

From a theophysical perspective, the Fruits of the Spirit may be understood as forms of ordered information—configurations of semantic and affective data that exhibit maximal coherence and minimal entropy. The coherence index of 0.293, while moderate, may be interpreted as a measure of the degree to which the Pauline text approximates the ideal of perfect informational order, which is identified with the divine nature itself. The anti-fruit scores, approaching zero, confirm the absence of informational noise or entropy within the virtue set.

### 4.3 The Epistemic Significance of Realization

The dominance of *realization* within the GoEmotions classification suggests that the Fruits of the Spirit are not merely behavioral dispositions but epistemic achievements—moments of cognitive insight into the nature of reality as grounded in the divine. This finding aligns with the Pauline emphasis on *epignōsis* (full knowledge) as a component of Christian maturity (cf. Colossians 1:9–10; Ephesians 1:17–19). The presence of *fear* within the top five emotions, while initially surprising, may be interpreted as the *phobos Kyriou* that is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10), a reverential awe that accompanies genuine epistemic insight.

## 5. Conclusion

The present study has demonstrated the applicability of a dual-channel computational framework—integrating lexical semantic analysis (L6) and affective sentiment mapping (L8)—to the theological analysis of the Fruits of the Spirit as enumerated in Galatians 5:22–23. The findings reveal a consistent pattern of high positive affect across all nine virtues, with lexical scores exhibiting greater variation, and identify *realization* as the dominant emotional category. These results are interpreted within the broader framework of theophysics as an information-theoretic approach to theological anthropology.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the lexical channel (L6) relies on a word-embedding model trained on a limited corpus (GNT + LXX), which may not capture the full semantic range of the Greek terms. Second, the affective channel (L8) employs a taxonomy (GoEmotions) developed for modern English, which may not be fully applicable to Koine Greek. Future research should address these limitations through the development of domain-specific emotion lexicons for biblical Greek and the expansion of the training corpus to include patristic and liturgical sources.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the present study contributes to the emerging field of theophysics by demonstrating the feasibility of formal computational analysis of theological texts and by providing a replicable methodology for cross-domain analysis. The dual-channel framework, in particular, offers a promising avenue for future research on the intersection of virtue ethics, affective neuroscience, and information theory.
