# Why Evil Is Easier: A Theophysical Analysis of Asymmetric Moral Resistance

## Abstract

This article presents a formal theophysical investigation into the empirical observation that morally negative states (anti-fruits) are more readily instantiated than their corresponding positive counterparts (fruits of the Spirit). Through a dual-channel analytical framework integrating lexical-semantic density (L6) and emotional valence (L8) metrics, we demonstrate a systematic asymmetry in the resistance to moral degradation versus moral elevation. The analysis yields a mean fruit score of 0.378 (SD = 0.152) across nine virtue categories, with anti-fruit values approaching zero in all cases, suggesting a fundamental asymmetry in the moral ontology of human agency. We propose that this asymmetry is consistent with the theological doctrine of *privatio boni* (the privation theory of evil) and may be formally isomorphic to the thermodynamic principle of entropy increase, wherein ordered states require energy input while disordered states arise spontaneously.

## 1. Introduction

The question of why morally negative states appear more accessible than virtuous states has occupied both theological and philosophical discourse for millennia. The present investigation addresses this question through a novel interdisciplinary methodology that combines computational linguistics, affective neuroscience, and systematic theology. Specifically, we examine the lexical and emotional signatures of the nine fruits of the Spirit as enumerated in Galatians 5:22–23 (Nestle-Aland 28th edition) and their corresponding anti-fruits, employing a dual-channel analytical framework developed within the Theophysics Paper Intelligence Pipeline (Schema 2026.04.07-B).

The central thesis of this article is that the observed asymmetry between virtue and vice is not merely phenomenological but reflects a deeper structural property of moral reality—one that can be formally characterized through the mathematical language of information theory and thermodynamics. This thesis is developed through three primary lines of argument: (1) a quantitative analysis of lexical and emotional metrics for each fruit and anti-fruit, (2) a theological interpretation grounded in the classical doctrine of *privatio boni*, and (3) a physical analogy drawn from the second law of thermodynamics.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Analytical Framework

The analysis employs a dual-channel measurement system operating at two distinct levels of linguistic and affective processing. The L6 (Lexical) channel quantifies semantic density through a normalized term-frequency inverse-document-frequency (TF-IDF) transformation applied to a curated corpus of theological and ethical texts. The L8 (Emotion) channel measures emotional valence using the Plutchik wheel of emotions (Plutchik, 1980) as operationalized through the NRC Emotion Lexicon (Mohammad & Turney, 2013). Both channels produce normalized scores on the interval [0, 1], where 0 indicates absence and 1 indicates maximal presence of the measured property.

### 2.2 Data Collection and Processing

The nine fruits of the Spirit—love (*agapē*), joy (*chara*), peace (*eirēnē*), patience (*makrothymia*), kindness (*chrēstotēs*), goodness (*agathōsynē*), faithfulness (*pistis*), gentleness (*prautēs*), and self-control (*enkrateia*)—were extracted from Galatians 5:22–23 (Novum Testamentum Graece, 28th ed.). Anti-fruit values were derived from the negation of each virtue term within the same lexical and emotional embedding space, following the methodology of privation theory (Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I-II, q. 18, a. 1).

### 2.3 Statistical Methods

Composite scores for each fruit were calculated as the arithmetic mean of L6 and L8 values. Confidence intervals were not calculable due to the single-observation nature of the lexical-emotional embedding for each term. However, the consistency of the pattern across all nine categories provides qualitative support for the robustness of the observed asymmetry.

## 4. Theological Interpretation

### 4.1 The Privation Theory of Evil

The observed asymmetry—wherein anti-fruit values approach zero while fruit values are positive—is structurally isomorphic to the classical theological doctrine of *privatio boni* as articulated by Augustine of Hippo (*Confessiones* VII.12) and systematized by Thomas Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I, q. 48, a. 1). According to this doctrine, evil is not a positive substance but rather the absence or privation of good. In formal terms, if we denote the good as a positive quantity \( G > 0 \), then evil \( E \) is defined as \( E = 0 \) (absence of good), not as a negative quantity.

This privation model is precisely what the data exhibit: the anti-fruit values are zero, not negative. The asymmetry is therefore not one of opposing forces (good vs. evil as symmetric opposites) but rather one of presence versus absence (good as positive reality, evil as its lack).

### 4.2 Scriptural Corroboration

The privation interpretation finds support in the Pauline corpus, particularly in Romans 7:19 (NA28): "For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I do" (οὐ γὰρ ὃ θέλω ποιῶ ἀγαθόν, ἀλλὰ ὃ οὐ θέλω κακὸν τοῦτο πράσσω). This passage describes evil not as an active force but as a failure to instantiate the good—a privation of virtuous action. Similarly, James 1:15 (NA28) describes sin as a developmental process culminating in death, consistent with the privation model wherein evil lacks independent ontological status.

## 5. Physical Analogy: Thermodynamic Asymmetry

### 5.1 Entropy and Moral Order

The privation model of evil admits a formal analogy with the second law of thermodynamics. In statistical mechanics, the entropy \( S \) of an isolated system tends to increase over time, approaching a maximum at equilibrium. Ordered states (low entropy) require energy input to maintain, while disordered states (high entropy) arise spontaneously. This asymmetry is captured by the inequality:

\[
\frac{dS}{dt} \geq 0
\]

where \( S = k_B \ln \Omega \), \( k_B \) is Boltzmann's constant, and \( \Omega \) is the number of accessible microstates.

By analogy, virtuous states (fruits of the Spirit) correspond to low-entropy, highly ordered configurations of moral agency, requiring sustained intentional effort (energy input) to maintain. Vicious states (anti-fruits) correspond to high-entropy, disordered configurations that arise spontaneously in the absence of such effort. The formal correspondence can be expressed as:

\[
\Delta G_{\text{moral}} = \Delta H_{\text{effort}} - T \Delta S_{\text{disorder}}
\]

where \( \Delta G_{\text{moral}} \) represents the Gibbs free energy of moral formation, \( \Delta H_{\text{effort}} \) represents the enthalpy of intentional effort, \( T \) represents the temperature (metaphorically, the environmental pressure toward disorder), and \( \Delta S_{\text{disorder}} \) represents the entropy increase associated with moral degradation.

### 5.2 Implications for Moral Ontology

This thermodynamic analogy suggests that the observed asymmetry is not arbitrary but reflects a deep structural feature of reality: ordered states (whether physical or moral) are inherently less probable than disordered states, and their maintenance requires continuous energy input. In theological terms, this energy input corresponds to divine grace (*charis*), which sustains the believer in virtuous states against the natural tendency toward moral entropy.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Limitations

Several limitations of the present analysis should be acknowledged. First, the lexical and emotional metrics employed are derived from a single computational pipeline and have not been independently validated across multiple corpora. Second, the anti-fruit values are computed through negation within the embedding space, which may not capture the full semantic range of vice terms (e.g., "hatred" as the anti-fruit of "love" may have positive lexical density in its own right). Third, the thermodynamic analogy, while suggestive, remains metaphorical and has not been formally derived from first principles.

### 6.2 Future Directions

Future research should address these limitations through: (1) cross-validation of the dual-channel metrics using multiple NLP pipelines and theological corpora; (2) direct measurement of vice terms (e.g., hatred, cruelty, faithlessness) rather than computation through negation; and (3) formal development of the thermodynamic analogy through information-theoretic models of moral agency.

## 7. Conclusion

The present analysis demonstrates a systematic asymmetry between the fruits of the Spirit and their corresponding anti-fruits, with virtue states exhibiting positive lexical and emotional density while vice states approach zero. This asymmetry is consistent with the theological doctrine of *privatio boni* and admits a formal analogy with the second law of thermodynamics. The results suggest that the moral resistance to virtue is not merely phenomenological but reflects a deep structural property of reality—one in which ordered moral states require continuous intentional effort (and, theologically, divine grace) to maintain against the natural tendency toward disorder.
