# The Entropic Society: Technology, Obsolescence, and the Devaluation of American Moral Capital (1950–2025)

## Abstract

This investigation examines the correlation between technological evolution and the degradation of moral capital in American society from 1950 to 2025. Through a tripartite framework of relational coherence, informational integrity, and psychological entropy, we demonstrate that technological systems—conceived as exteriorizations of human will—have exerted differential and sequential effects on social stability. The analysis identifies three distinct technological phases: Mass Mediation (1950–1985), Individualized Access (1985–2005), and Perpetual Connectivity (2005–Present). Longitudinal data synthesis reveals that Phase 1 technologies (primarily broadcast television) contributed to the gradual erosion of civic participation and institutional trust through passive consumption patterns; Phase 2 technologies facilitated structural dispersion without constituting a primary causal mechanism; and Phase 3 technologies (social media platforms and mobile devices) exhibit the strongest temporal correlation with acute psychological entropy, particularly among adolescent populations. Causal evidence from experimental studies supports the claim that algorithmic attention-capture architectures interfere with biological mechanisms of emotional regulation. The analysis further identifies planned obsolescence as an engineered aesthetic of transience that structurally models moral decay. We conclude that the technological imperative of efficiency and maximization, when exteriorized without adequate institutional control, produces systemic instability culminating in the engineered destabilization of the human psyche. Policy recommendations include democratizing technology design, implementing right-to-repair frameworks, and mandating age-appropriate usage restrictions.

**Keywords:** technological determinism, moral capital, social entropy, planned obsolescence, psychological entropy, critical theory of technology

## Section I: The Technology of Human Will

### I.A. Conceptual and Critical Definition

The investigation into the moral trajectory of American society requires a philosophical definition of technology that transcends mere instrumentalism. Technology, within this framework, is understood not simply as hardware or software but as a cultural and ontological force—an **exteriorization of human will** (Feenberg, 2003). It constitutes the primary mechanism by which contemporary society organizes itself and achieves coherence (Ihde, 1990). Consequently, the trajectory of technological development reflects fundamental shifts in collective human values, particularly the impulse toward maximization and control.

### I.B. Technology as Exteriorized Intent and Cultural Force

Technology's role as a cultural determinant situates its study within the humanities philosophy of technology, which emphasizes its meaning and societal impact (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2020). If philosophy seeks to understand how things in the broadest sense relate, then the vast scope and complexity of technological systems compel analysis of their moral footprint. This conceptual framework requires acknowledging that technology is fundamentally **value-laden** (Feenberg, 2002). Technical systems embody the goals of efficiency and power. When humans engage with the world through technology, they are, by necessity, adopting a maximizing and controlling approach. This approach, when applied ubiquitously, determines what may be termed a "technological way of life."

### I.C. The Moral Status of the Artifact: Critical Theory and Controllability

A Critical Theory of technology provides the analytical lens necessary to investigate the observed entropic decay. This perspective agrees with instrumentalism that technology is controllable, yet agrees with substantivism that it is inherently value-laden (Feenberg, 2002). This seemingly paradoxical position suggests that while technology embeds values such as efficiency, it is possible to tame it by submitting development to a more democratic process of design and deployment. The central premise is that the societal problem resides not in technology itself, but in the **institutional failure to design systems and institutions that allow human control over technological development** (Feenberg, 2002).

### I.D. The Architectural Vectors of Technological Emergence

The technological landscape of the period under investigation may be partitioned into three distinct phases, each characterized by specific architectural features and societal penetration rates.

**Phase 1: 1950–1985 — Mass Mediation**

This phase is characterized by unidirectional, passive, communal consumption. Dominant technologies include broadcast television, radio, and landline telephony. Television approached near-saturation during this period, fundamentally reshaping attention patterns and domestic temporal organization (DataTrek Research, 2020). This phase established centralized cultural narratives and imposed high passive attention demands (Klofstad, 2010).

**Phase 2: 1985–2005 — Individualized Access**

This phase is characterized by personalized computing and active information retrieval. Dominant technologies include the personal computer and early internet. By 2000, approximately half of U.S. adults were online (Pew Research Center, 2024). This phase decentralized information and empowered individual retrieval. By 2005, two-thirds of U.S. adults owned cell phones (OCLC, 2010).

**Phase 3: 2005–Present — Perpetual Connectivity**

This phase is characterized by mobile devices, high-speed connectivity, and social platforms. As of 2024, 96% of U.S. adults are online (Pew Research Center, 2024). Facebook experienced 1,300% growth between 2005 and 2010 (OCLC, 2010). Notably, 95% of youth aged 13–17 use social media, with many reporting "almost constant" usage (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2023).

## Section II: The Aesthetic Transition — Analog Durability to Digital Entropy

### II.A. The Patina of Permanence: Analog Durability

The earlier period of manufacturing embraced an aesthetic of analog durability. This design philosophy valued high-quality materials, products built for repair, and longevity (Sustainability Directory, 2023). The physical artifact was intended to absorb the effects of time, accruing subjective value—the "patina" of use—which encouraged long-term investment, repair, and transmission across generations. This embedded cultural value in physical goods naturally reinforced social stability through material permanence.

### II.B. The Ascent of Planned and Perceived Obsolescence

The critical aesthetic shift involves the acceptance of an **expendable aesthetic** and **planned obsolescence** (Harvard Design Magazine, 2013). This principle dictates that products are designed with an intentionally short lifespan, viewing objects as "short-life toys" or replaceable components within temporary frames. This represents a fundamental departure from the durability paradigm.

### II.C. Entropy as a Digital Design Principle

In modern, highly specialized consumer electronics, entropy is not a failure but an engineered feature—the **commodification of systemic decay** (Scientific.Net, 2013). Digital entropy manifests through closed hardware ecosystems, non-replaceable components, and software incompatibility. A physically functioning device is rendered useless by digital decay, ensuring perpetual consumption. If technological artifacts are the exteriorization of human intent, then the shift toward designed decay indicates a societal and corporate will that prioritizes maximum extraction over cultural permanence and stability.

## Section III: Longitudinal Data Synthesis — Technology and Decline Vectors (1950–2025)

### III.A. Relational Coherence: Fragmentation of Social Bonds

#### III.A.1. Decline in Civic Participation

The citizen withdrawal from voluntary associations and the unraveling of social connections occurred **gradually and steadily after the 1950s and 1960s** (Klofstad, 2010). Social capital theory attributes this decline principally to **generational replacement** (approximately 50% of the decline) and the rise of **television-watching** (approximately 25% of the decline) (Klofstad, 2010). One-person households rose from 7.7% (1940) to 27.6% (2020)—a systemic trend toward structural isolation spanning all three technological phases (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023).

#### III.A.2. Marital Coherence and Isolation

The U.S. divorce rate peaked around 1980 and has generally declined since (Bowling Green State University NCFMR, 2024). This peak occurred during the transition from Phase 1 to Phase 2. The fact that marital instability peaked before mass proliferation of personal computers, the Internet, and smartphones suggests that changes in family structure were driven by macro-social and cultural forces **distinct from and preceding the digital revolution**.

### III.B. Informational Integrity: Erosion of Institutional Trust

Trust in government declined from approximately 75% (1958) to below 30% (since 2007) (Pew Research Center, 2024, 2025). Trust began eroding during the 1960s amid the Vietnam War and continued falling through the 1970s with Watergate (Pew Research Center, 2024). Media confidence fell from 68–72% in the 1970s to 53% by 1997 (Gallup, 2024). Phase 1 generated **disillusionment**—loss of faith in centralized authorities. Phase 3 then accelerated **fragmentation**, providing a platform for decentralized, often dubious information that thrives where foundational trust has already been destroyed (The Trust Project, 2023).

### III.C. Psychological Entropy: Internal Systemic Breakdown

The contemporary crisis is marked by temporal alignment of the mental distress spike with the saturation point of social media adoption (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024). Experimental studies establish a **causal link** between limited platform use and improved well-being (University of Pennsylvania, 2018). Frequent social media use may be associated with distinct changes in the developing brain's amygdala (emotional learning) and prefrontal cortex (impulse control and emotional regulation) (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2023). These changes increase sensitivity to social rewards and punishments, engineering a perpetually unstable internal state.

## Section IV: Correlation Analysis — Establishing Linkages Between Entropy and Decline

### IV.A. Phase 1: Foundation of Decay

Television functionally replaced active group participation with isolated, domestic leisure, leading to the gradual "unraveling" of social capital (Klofstad, 2010). Simultaneously, broadcast media centralized dissemination of political failures, generating pervasive disillusionment (Pew Research Center, 2024).

### IV.B. Phase 2: Structural Dispersion

Phase 2 functions less as a primary cause and more as a structural accelerant of existing trends. It provided tools for individualized retreat, contributing to the accelerating one-person household trend (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023). The mode of interaction remained active (retrieval-based) rather than passive.

### IV.C. Phase 3: Acute Psychological Entropy

Phase 3 exhibits the highest and most acute correlation with decline, specifically internal psychological stability. Mass smartphone and social media adoption (post-2013) precisely corresponds with the youth anxiety and depression spike (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024). Causal evidence has been confirmed through experimental studies (University of Pennsylvania, 2018). The algorithmic objective of maximizing attention capture leads to a technological environment that interferes with the biological mechanisms of emotional regulation and sleep. The efficiency goal of the technical system directly causes internal systemic breakdown.

## Section V: Falsification and Alternative Pathways

### V.A. The Non-Deterministic Counter-Thesis

Technology primarily accelerates or provides a platform for pre-existing societal tensions and trends (Pew Research Center, 2020). The societal harms observed are not necessarily the inevitable outcome of technological progress, but rather the consequence of society failing to implement adequate civic or institutional structures to manage the pace of change.

### V.B. Socioeconomic and Political Drivers

#### V.B.1. Economic Inequality

Rising inequality fosters a competitive economic climate that directly erodes social cohesion (PNAS Nexus, 2024). It correlates with increased moralization—the rise in harsh moral language and judgments. Economic strain acts as a major confounding variable that both initiates and magnifies technological harm.

#### V.B.2. Political Polarization

The feeling of moral free fall is a point of agreement across political affiliations (Columbia Magazine, 2024). Political and ideological conflicts substantially predate Phase 3 technology. Technology did not create the division but provided a highly efficient platform for existing moral conflict to be amplified.

### V.C. Generational Replacement and Cultural Momentum

Putnam's (2010) definitive analysis places overwhelming emphasis on **generational replacement**. The replacement of the "long civic generation" by generations with less inherent proclivity for group involvement accounts for approximately half of the decline in relational coherence. The decay of civic bonds was fundamentally a cultural shift—facilitated by Phase 1 technology (television) but driven primarily by demographic momentum.

## Section VI: Conclusion and Policy Recommendations

The investigation confirms a profound, temporally specific correlation between technological evolution and the degradation of American moral capital from 1950 to the present. The analysis rejects a simple, single-cause narrative and establishes that the impact of technology has been **differential and sequential**.

### VI.A. Core Finding

The technological imperative of efficiency and maximization, when exteriorized without institutional control, creates an aesthetic of entropic transience (planned obsolescence) that structurally models and enables moral decay, culminating in the **engineered instability of the human psyche**.

### VI.B. Recommendations for Restoring Moral Capital

#### VI.B.1. Democratizing Technology Design

Mandate design transparency, allow public scrutiny of algorithmic objectives, and hold corporations accountable for negative externalities. Establish institutions directing technological development toward social stability (Feenberg, 2002).

#### VI.B.2. Countering Digital Entropy

Implement stringent "Right-to-Repair" legal frameworks for hardware and software. By encouraging durability and discouraging planned obsolescence, reinforce cultural values of investment, repair, and long-term stability (Sustainability Directory, 2023).

#### VI.B.3. Mitigating Psychological Entropy

Mandate age-appropriate usage limits, restrict features interfering with sleep, and prohibit designs engineered to overstimulate developing brains. Prioritize community-based interventions emphasizing non-mediated social interaction (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2023).
