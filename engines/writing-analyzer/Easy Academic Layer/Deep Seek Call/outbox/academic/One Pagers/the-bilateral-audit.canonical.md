# Theophysics: A Bilateral Audit of Scientific and Theological Epistemological Foundations

## Abstract

For approximately five decades, the John Templeton Foundation has sponsored inquiry into the possibility of genuine dialogue between scientific and religious modes of knowing. Despite substantial scholarly output, the anticipated convergence has not materialized. This article identifies a structural impediment that has remained unaddressed in the literature: the asymmetric epistemological dependency of scientific methodology upon premises that are not empirically derivable yet are structurally isomorphic with theological claims. A bilateral audit protocol is proposed—one that subjects both scientific and theological propositions to identical standards of falsifiability, predictive power, explanatory scope, internal consistency, and reproducibility. The audit reveals six specific leak points in naturalistic epistemology, six domains in which theological claims demonstrate structural correspondence with empirical findings, and a cross-domain coherence-decoherence binary that surfaces independently across seventy distinct fields of inquiry. A formal framework—the χ-substrate—is introduced as a generating architecture from which both scientific and theological domains may be understood as dual projections of a single coherence structure. The method is operationalized through the 7Q Reverse protocol, Isomorphic Event Density mapping, and Lean 4 formal verification. Results suggest that the boundary between science and theology is not a natural division but a constructed partition that has been accepted without sufficient epistemological examination.

## 1. Introduction: The Structural Contradiction in Science-Religion Discourse

The contemporary public intellectual landscape exhibits a de facto division of epistemic authority. Empirical science commands approximately eighty percent of institutional credibility, encompassing logic, mathematics, prediction, technology, medicine, policy, and the operational definition of knowledge itself. Christian theology occupies the remaining twenty percent, confined to personal devotion, private morality, and the acknowledgment that religious belief may hold subjective meaning for adherents—provided such belief does not contest scientific claims within the public sphere.

This arrangement is widely perceived as the earned outcome of four centuries of scientific progress over religious dogma. The present analysis contends that this perception is epistemologically unsound. The division rests upon a structural contradiction that has not been formally named: the scientific enterprise, prior to its first measurement, must borrow a set of premises that it cannot prove through its own methodological apparatus.

These premises include:

1. The external world exists independently of observation.
2. The external world is orderly and knowable.
3. Logical inference is valid and corresponds to reality.
4. Human cognitive faculties reliably track truth.
5. Mathematics—an abstract formal system—describes physical structure.
6. Nature behaves consistently across time (uniformitarianism).
7. Truth exists as a normative concept.
8. Intellectual honesty is a binding obligation.
9. Simpler explanations are epistemically preferable (Occam's razor).
10. The universe is comprehensible to finite minds.

None of these premises is empirically derivable. Each is required before empirical investigation can be designed or interpreted. These premises do not describe a neutral, indifferent universe. They describe a universe that is rational, ordered, knowable, and accessible to minds—a description that theology has articulated for approximately three millennia. Science did not generate this foundation through its own methods; it borrowed it from a worldview it subsequently declared out of bounds.

This constitutes a structural contradiction: one party operates on premises taken from the other without acknowledgment, then uses those premises to exclude the lender from public epistemic authority. The consequence is that fifty years of well-funded dialogue have produced extensive literature but no resolution of the underlying epistemological asymmetry.

## 2. Method: The Bilateral Audit Protocol

The standard move in science-religion discourse is to ask whether religious claims survive scientific scrutiny. This is a unidirectional audit. The present method proposes a bilateral audit: both domains are subjected to identical standards of evaluation.

### 2.1 Audit Standards

The following criteria are applied symmetrically:

- **Falsifiability**: Can the claim be specified such that counterevidence would disconfirm it?
- **Predictive power**: Does the claim generate testable expectations that are confirmed?
- **Explanatory scope**: How many independent phenomena does the claim coherently account for?
- **Internal consistency**: Are the claim's components logically coherent?
- **Reproducibility**: Can the claim's empirical consequences be independently verified?

### 2.2 The 7Q Reverse Protocol

The bilateral audit is operationalized through the 7Q Reverse method, a seven-question procedure that converts epistemological posture into repeatable inquiry:

1. **Posture**: Is the inquirer willing to be corrected by the evidence?
2. **Grounding**: Can the worldview under examination account for its own conditions of possibility?
3. **Translation**: Can cross-domain claims be mapped through a neutral information-theoretic layer?
4. **Mechanism**: Is the causal chain specified, or is correlation mistaken for explanation?
5. **Simplicity**: Does the explanation minimize unnecessary postulates?
6. **Completion**: Are all relevant domains of evidence addressed?
7. **Falsification**: Are kill conditions publicly named and actionable?

This protocol is designed to be adversarially testable: any researcher may apply it to any worldview and publish the results.
