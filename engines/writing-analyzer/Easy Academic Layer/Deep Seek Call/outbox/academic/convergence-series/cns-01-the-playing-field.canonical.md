# Epistemic Foundations: The Structural Parallel Between Scientific Naturalism and Biblical Theism

## Abstract

This article examines the foundational epistemic commitments underlying both scientific naturalism and biblical theism, demonstrating that both systems rest upon unprovable axioms that cannot be validated by their own internal methodologies. Through structural analysis of presuppositional frameworks, we identify a formal isomorphism between the circular reasoning patterns inherent in both worldviews. The scientific enterprise requires at least ten presuppositions—including the existence of an external world, the uniformity of nature, the reliability of sensory perception, and the applicability of mathematics to physical reality—none of which can be empirically verified. Similarly, theological systems grounded in divine revelation exhibit self-referential validation structures, wherein Scripture attests to its own authority through what Calvin termed *autopistia* (self-authentication). We argue that the relevant epistemological question is not whether faith or evidence constitutes the proper foundation for knowledge, but rather which foundational axiom, once granted, provides greater explanatory power for observed phenomena. This analysis establishes the methodological framework for subsequent interdisciplinary investigation at the intersection of physics and theology.

## 1. Introduction: The Problem of Foundational Validation

The question of epistemic foundations—what ultimately grounds our claims to knowledge—remains unresolved across disciplinary boundaries. Both scientific naturalism and biblical theism present themselves as comprehensive explanatory frameworks, yet each confronts a fundamental challenge: the inability to validate its own starting point without recourse to circular reasoning.

This article identifies and analyzes the structural parallels between these two systems, arguing that both rest upon *axiomatic commitments* that cannot be demonstrated by the methods they employ. The significance of this observation lies not in any equivalence of content between the two frameworks, but rather in the formal similarity of their epistemic architecture. Understanding this structural parallel reframes the perennial debate between scientific and theological worldviews, moving it from a contest between "faith" and "evidence" to a comparative analysis of which foundational commitment accounts more comprehensively for observed reality.

## 2. The Presuppositional Structure of Scientific Inquiry

### 2.1 The Ten Presuppositions of Science

Hugh Gauch (2003) systematically identified the presuppositions that undergird scientific methodology. These assumptions, which cannot themselves be empirically verified, include:

1. **The existence of an external world** independent of the observer
2. **The orderliness and knowability of nature**
3. **The validity of logical reasoning**
4. **The reliability of sensory perception** as a source of truthful information about reality
5. **The applicability of mathematics** to physical phenomena
6. **The uniformity of natural laws** across time and space
7. **The existence of truth** as a meaningful concept
8. **The value of intellectual honesty** and truth-seeking
9. **The reality of abstract entities** (including numbers and mathematical structures)
10. **The principle of parsimony** (simpler explanations are epistemically preferable)

Gauch (2003, p. 78) explicitly states that these foundations are "legitimated by appeal to common sense and sincerity" rather than by empirical demonstration. This constitutes a critical admission: the methodological naturalism that defines scientific practice cannot itself be scientifically justified.

### 2.2 Hume's Problem of Induction

The epistemological challenge identified by David Hume (1739/2000) remains unresolved within the scientific framework. Induction—the inference from observed regularities to unobserved instances—is both essential to scientific reasoning and incapable of logical justification. The argument that induction has been successful in the past is itself an inductive argument, generating an infinite regress. Formally:

Let \( P \) represent the proposition that "induction is reliable." The scientific justification of \( P \) requires an inductive argument of the form:

\[
\forall t \in [t_0, t_n]: \text{Induction has been reliable at time } t
\]
\[
\therefore \text{Induction will be reliable at time } t_{n+1}
\]

This argument presupposes the very principle it seeks to justify. No non-circular empirical demonstration of induction's reliability has been produced since Hume's original formulation.

### 2.3 Causal Closure as Foundational Axiom

The operational axiom of scientific naturalism is the principle of causal closure: every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause. This principle is methodologically necessary—without it, the reproducibility of experimental results cannot be guaranteed, as supernatural intervention could disrupt causal chains at any point. However, causal closure is not a conclusion derived from empirical investigation but rather a presupposition that makes empirical investigation possible. It constitutes what Plantinga (2011) terms a *basic belief*—a commitment that is properly basic within the scientific framework but cannot be demonstrated by it.

## 3. The Self-Referential Structure of Biblical Authority

### 3.1 The Autopistia of Scripture

The theological claim that Scripture is the Word of God exhibits a structurally analogous circularity. If Scripture is the highest epistemic authority, then appealing to any external standard—human reason, archaeological evidence, or scientific consensus—to validate that authority would logically subordinate Scripture to that external standard. The Reformed theological tradition, following Calvin (1559/1960), articulates this as the *autopistia* or self-authentication of Scripture.

Calvin (1559/1960, I.7.5) writes:

> "The Scripture indeed is self-authenticated (*αὐτόπιστος*); hence, it is not right to subject it to proof and reasoning. And the certainty which it deserves with us, it attains by the testimony of the Spirit."

This position finds scriptural warrant in Hebrews 6:13 (ESV): "For when God made a promise to Abraham, since he had no one greater by whom to swear, he swore by himself." The theological claim is that divine revelation, as the ultimate epistemic foundation, cannot be validated by anything external to itself without compromising its ultimacy.

### 3.2 Structural Circularity in Theological Epistemology

The circularity inherent in theological epistemology is formally analogous to that of scientific epistemology. Just as science uses induction to validate induction, theology uses Scripture to validate Scripture's authority. In both cases, the circularity is not an accidental flaw but a necessary feature of any system that claims foundational status. If a foundation could be validated by something external, that external thing would constitute the actual foundation.

## 4. Comparative Structural Analysis

### 4.1 Formal Isomorphism

The structural parallel between scientific naturalism and biblical theism can be represented as follows:

| Dimension | Scientific Naturalism | Biblical Theism |
|-----------|---------------------|-----------------|
| Foundational Axiom | Causal closure of the physical | Divine aseity and revelation |
| Validation Method | Induction (self-referential) | Autopistia (self-referential) |
| Unprovable Starting Point | Uniformity of nature | Character of God |
| Epistemic Circularity | Induction validates induction | Scripture validates Scripture |
| External Validation | Impossible by definition | Impossible by definition |

### 4.2 The Question of Explanatory Power

The relevant epistemological question, properly framed, is not whether one system possesses evidence while the other requires faith. Both systems require faith—understood as the acceptance of an unprovable starting point. The question is rather: *which foundational commitment, once granted, accounts for more of what we actually observe?*

This formulation avoids the category error of demanding that one system validate itself by the other's standards. Scientific naturalism cannot adjudicate theological claims because its method presupposes naturalism; biblical theism cannot adjudicate scientific claims by theological fiat without undermining the autonomy of empirical investigation. The appropriate comparison is one of explanatory scope and coherence.

---

## 5. Methodological Implications

### 5.1 Reframing the Debate

The recognition of structural circularity in both systems reframes the science-religion debate. The claim that "science has disproven God" commits a category error: science tests natural causes and finds them adequate to explain natural effects, but the assertion that natural causes are *all that exist* is not a scientific finding but the axiomatic presupposition with which science begins. Conversely, the claim that one should "just have faith" misrepresents the epistemic situation: faith, properly understood, is not the absence of evidence but the acceptance of a starting point that cannot be externally validated—a position structurally identical to the scientific commitment to methodological naturalism.

### 5.2 Establishing the Playing Field

This analysis establishes the methodological framework for subsequent interdisciplinary investigation. The following questions become tractable within this framework:

1. Does the Bible contain predictive claims that anticipate scientific discoveries?
2. Do the mathematical structures that describe physical reality point toward a transcendent ground?
3. What explanatory power does theism provide for the existence of objective moral values?
4. How do the patterns of paradigm shifts in scientific history relate to theological claims about human cognition?

These questions can be investigated without either side claiming an epistemic high ground that neither can legitimately occupy.

---

## 6. Conclusion

Both scientific naturalism and biblical theism rest on foundational axioms that cannot be validated by their own methods. The circularity inherent in both systems is not a defect but a necessary feature of any claim to ultimacy. The relevant question is not whether one system has "evidence" while the other requires "faith," but rather which foundational commitment, once granted, provides greater explanatory coherence across the full range of human experience.

This conclusion does not diminish the extraordinary achievements of science, which has successfully mapped the genome, split the atom, and explored the solar system. Nor does it reduce theology to mere fideism, as theological claims about revelation, creation, and redemption can be examined for internal consistency and explanatory power. Rather, it establishes a level playing field for genuine interdisciplinary dialogue—one in which both sides acknowledge their axiomatic commitments and proceed to the substantive work of comparative analysis.
