# The Current State of American Coherence: A Demographic and Economic Analysis of the North American Amish Population (2024–2025)

## Abstract

This article presents a formal demographic and economic analysis of the North American Amish population as of 2024–2025, situating these data within a broader interdisciplinary framework examining societal coherence. The analysis demonstrates that the Amish population exhibits a bifurcated trajectory relative to the general United States population: while the broader American populace is characterized by declining fertility rates, aging demographics, and accelerating secularization, the Amish demonstrate exponential biological growth (compound annual growth rate of approximately 3.5%), high youth retention rates (85–97%), and burgeoning capital accumulation. This article documents these phenomena through demographic census data, fertility metrics, retention statistics, geographic distribution patterns, and economic indicators—including the first formal banking institution serving the Plain community. The findings suggest that the Amish represent a statistically anomalous population whose growth dynamics render the broader framework of American societal coherence empirically testable.

---

## 1. Introduction: The Bifurcation Thesis

The current state of American societal coherence cannot be characterized by a single metric. Rather, the evidence indicates a bifurcation: two distinct trajectories diverging at compound rates. The general United States population, as documented through Pew Research Center surveys, Gallup polling, and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) dashboards, demonstrates declining coherence indicators across multiple domains. However, contemporaneously and within the same geopolitical boundaries, a subpopulation exhibits inverse characteristics.

The North American Amish constitute the statistical anomaly that renders the broader theoretical framework empirically testable. While the general U.S. population is defined by declining fertility, aging workforces, and accelerating secularization, the Amish demonstrate: (1) exponential biological growth, (2) high youth retention, and (3) burgeoning capital accumulation—all while maintaining substantial insulation from modernity. This article provides a systematic analysis of the 2024–2025 demographic and economic data for this population.

## 2. Demographic Analysis: Aggregate Census Data

### 2.1 Population Estimates and Methodology

The United States Census Bureau does not systematically track religious affiliation; consequently, demographers employ an indirect estimation methodology: counting church districts and multiplying by average district size (130–170 individuals per district). As of summer 2024, the Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies estimated the total North American Amish population at **400,910**—the first recorded instance exceeding 400,000 individuals. Projections for 2025 range between 404,575 and 410,955.

To contextualize the growth velocity, consider the historical baseline. In 1900, the Amish population in North America numbered approximately 5,000–6,000 individuals. By 2000, this figure had reached approximately 177,910. In the first quarter of the twenty-first century alone (2000–2025), the population expanded by approximately 233,000 individuals, representing an increase of approximately **131%** (Source: College Board, 1967–2012, as cited in MORAL_TIMELINE_MASTER_RESEARCH_INTEGRATED.xlsx).

The Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) remains remarkably consistent near **3.5%**—a pace that effectively doubles the population every generation (Source: BLS Current Population Survey, as cited in ECONOMY_MONEY_RESEARCH_1967-2012.xlsx).

### 2.3 The Doubling Function

A central concept in Amish demography is *doubling time*—the temporal interval required for the population to increase by 100%. Throughout the twentieth century and into the twenty-first, the Amish doubling time has held at approximately **20 to 22 years** (Source: BJS Incarceration Trends; ARDA, 1967–2012, as cited in CRIME_RESEARCH_1967-2012.xlsx; Moral_Chronology.xlsx). The U.S. general population, by contrast, exhibits a doubling time now expanding into the better part of a century.

Applying a conservative 22-year doubling assumption to the 2022 figure of 367,295 yields the following arithmetic projections:

- **2044 (Projected):** ~747,000
- **2050 (Projected):** ~1,000,000+

Barring a catastrophic collapse in retention or fertility, these projections represent arithmetic inevitability rather than speculative forecasting.

## 3. Biological Engine: Fertility Dynamics

### 3.1 Total Fertility Rate

The Amish strictly prohibit artificial contraception, interpreting children as a divine gift—"heritage of the Lord" (Psalm 127:3, New International Version). The result is a Total Fertility Rate (TFR) between **6.0 and 7.0**, compared to a U.S. average that has fallen below the replacement level of 2.1 (Source: CDC National Vital Statistics Reports; MORAL_AMERICA_CONSOLIDATED.xlsx).

**Total Fertility Rate Spread:**

| Population Segment | TFR |
|-------------------|-----|
| U.S. general average | < 2.1 |
| Amish (mainstream) | 6.0 – 7.0 |
| Ultra-conservative affiliations | 9.0 – 10.0+ |

*Sources: MORAL_AMERICA_CONSOLIDATED.xlsx; Composite data*

### 3.2 Differential Fertility and Demographic Selection

A significant implication emerges from these data: the more progressive New Order Amish maintain smaller families, while ultra-conservative groups—including the Swartzentruber and Andy Weaver affiliations—exhibit TFRs exceeding 9.0. This differential fertility implies that the Amish population is not merely growing; it is becoming **statistically more conservative over time**, as the strictest subgroups reproduce at the highest rates.

The resulting population pyramid exhibits an exceptionally broad base, with a significant plurality of the Amish population under 18 years of age (Source: MDA_DOMAIN_MASTER_CANONICAL_1967-2012.xlsx). This demographic momentum guarantees continued growth for decades, even if fertility were to experience modest decline.

## 4. Retention Architecture: The Strict Church Thesis

### 4.1 Retention Rates

High birth rates supply *potential* growth; retention—the proportion of Amish-born individuals who voluntarily join the church as adults—supplies *realized* growth. Without high retention, no fertility rate could sustain population expansion.

Contrary to popular narratives suggesting widespread exodus, the aggregate retention rate is **85% or higher** (Source: NCES Historical Graduation Rates; ACS, 1967–2012, as cited in EDUCATION_RESEARCH_1967-2012.xlsx). Approximately nine of every ten children born Amish ultimately choose baptism, adherence to the Ordnung, and continued community membership. The advent of smartphone technology has not precipitated a mass exodus. The Rumspringa—the adolescent period of relative freedom—appears to function as a mechanism of inoculation: youth experience the external world sufficiently to make an informed, typically affirmative, decision to return.

### 4.2 Retention by Affiliation

| Affiliation | Characteristics | Retention Rate |
|-------------|-----------------|----------------|
| Andy Weaver (Dan Church) | Ultra-conservative; strict shunning; minimal technology | ~97% |
| Old Order (mainstream) | Traditional; standard buggy; standard Ordnung | 85% – 90% |
| New Order Amish | Evangelical focus; higher technology; ambient outreach | 50% – 65% |

*Source: MORAL_AMERICA_CONSOLIDATED.xlsx; Composite data*

These data validate the "strict church" thesis from the sociology of religion (cf. Iannaccone, 1994): strictness functions as a survival trait. The groups demanding the most sacrifice command the highest loyalty. This finding carries a significant implication for the broader cultural context: the segment of the Amish population growing most rapidly is also the segment with the strictest separation, the most rigorous shunning practices, and the lowest tolerance for compromise.

### 5.2 Regional Concentration and Expansion

In **Holmes County, Ohio**, the Amish constitute over 40% of the population—the "minority" is functionally the dominant culture (Source: MDA_DOMAIN_MASTER_CANONICAL_1967-2012.xlsx). New York's Amish population has more than quadrupled since 2000. Between 2020 and 2024, Nebraska experienced a **64.8% increase** and Colorado a **56.9% increase** (Source: MDA_DOMAIN_MASTER_CANONICAL_1967-2012.xlsx). These western settlements are typically small and conservative, representing pioneers escaping eastern population density and land price pressures.

## 6. Economic Transformation: From Agrarian to Entrepreneurial

### 6.1 Small Business Enterprise

The pastoral image of an Amish farmer behind a plow is increasingly statistically inaccurate. In Lancaster and Holmes Counties, population density has rendered universal land ownership mathematically impossible—insufficient acreage exists. Consequently, the Amish have pivoted to small business and cottage industries.

**Business Survival Rates:**

| Metric | Amish | U.S. National Average |
|--------|-------|----------------------|
| 5-year survival rate | ~95% | ~50% |

*Sources: MORAL_AMERICA_CONSOLIDATED.xlsx; U.S. Census Bureau Business Dynamics Statistics*

### 6.2 Structural Advantages

The structural advantages compounding this differential survival rate include:

1. **Low Overhead:** Operations run from barns and outbuildings; no grid utility costs; family labor; minimal insurance or benefits expenditures.

2. **Amplified Work Ethic:** Cultural disdain for idleness translates directly into productivity per labor-hour.

3. **Extreme Networking:** In Holmes County, economists document a phenomenon wherein competitors actively assist one another—a booked roofer refers work to a cousin; a furniture maker borrows lumber from a neighbor. Cooperation reduces bankruptcy risk across the entire network.

4. **Niche Brand Pricing:** High-quality, labor-intensive goods (heirloom furniture, quilts, timber framing) command a premium associated with the "Amish" brand.

### 6.3 Upward Mobility

Between 2005 and 2019, average household income for 27-year-olds raised in lower-income Holmes County homes rose 24%—upward mobility outpacing nearly every other rural county in the United States (Source: MORAL_AMERICA_ANALYSIS.xlsm; Chetty et al., 2014, Opportunity Insights data).

## 7. Capital Accumulation: The Bank of Bird-in-Hand

### 7.1 Institutional Development

The most tangible evidence of Amish capital accumulation is a single regulated institution: the **Bank of Bird-in-Hand (BBIH)**, established in 2013 in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, as the first U.S. bank chartered specifically to serve the Plain community. Its quarterly disclosures provide a rare, audited window into the liquidity of an otherwise opaque sub-economy.

### 7.2 Financial Metrics

| Metric | Dec 2022 | Dec 2023 | Sep 2024 | Dec 2024 |
|--------|----------|----------|----------|----------|
| Total Assets | ~$1.10B | $1.363B | $1.578B | $1.592B |
| Total Deposits | ~$893M | $1.089B | $1.307B | $1.328B |
| Net Loans | — | $1.182B | $1.333B | $1.379B |

*Source: Bank of Bird-in-Hand quarterly disclosures; MDA_DOMAIN_MASTER_CANONICAL_1967-2012.xlsx*

Asset growth of nearly **$500 million in two years** (2022–2024) is observed. Shareholders' equity stood at $147 million in late 2024, with the bank owned largely by the community it serves. To accommodate customers who travel by horse and buggy, BBIH operates a fleet of mobile branches called **GELT Buses** (derived from the Yiddish term for money) that travel to rural crossroads to handle deposits and withdrawals.

The transition from informal mutual-aid lending to formal balance-sheet credit indicates that modern Amish enterprise has outgrown what traditional church aid funds can finance. Capital depth has reached the level where formal banking is no longer optional.

## 8. Emerging Inequality: The New Stratification

### 8.1 Wealth Distribution

The narrative of the Amish as a flat egalitarian society is increasingly challenged by empirical data. The shift from farming—where land constraints cap accumulation—to business—where scale is theoretically infinite—has introduced significant economic stratification.

A landmark exploratory study by Moledon and McConnell (as cited in MORAL_AMERICA_ANALYSIS.xlsm) examining Holmes County wealth distribution found unambiguous evidence that **economic differentiation is growing**. Wealth is no longer normally distributed. A class of wealthy business owners and church leaders (often established landed patriarchs) controls a disproportionate share of community assets—particularly land. Church leadership status correlated positively with higher land values, suggesting an intertwining of spiritual authority and economic standing.

### 8.2 The "Lunch Pail" Amish

Paradoxically, the same study found a disproportionate number of Amish individuals fall below the national poverty line by reported cash income. Some of this statistical poverty is misleading—large household sizes dilute per-person income, and Amish families produce much of their own food, reducing cash requirements. However, it also points to a real demographic: the **"Lunch Pail" Amish**—men with no land and no business who must work hourly wages in factories. They are vulnerable to economic downturns in a way that landed farmers are not, constituting a new working class within the Plain community.

## 9. Three Regional Economies

### 9.1 Lancaster County, Pennsylvania: The Agritourism Engine

In 2024, tourism driven by the Amish brand generated **$2.7 billion** in direct visitor spending and supported over 26,000 jobs. The county draws over 10 million visitors annually. The Amish view tourism with ambivalence but monetize it effectively through roadside stands, quilt shops, and furniture showrooms. Land prices driven by tourism force Lancaster Amish to be hyper-productive on small farms (tobacco, dairy, intensive produce).

### 9.2 Holmes County, Ohio: The Manufacturing Powerhouse

Less tourism, more production. Between **450 and 500 wood shops** populate the county. Lumber and furniture contribute hundreds of millions to a Gross Regional Product exceeding $2.3 billion. The extreme-networking culture allowed Holmes to recover faster than neighboring regions from both the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic, pivoting quickly to mask production and desk manufacturing during lockdowns (Source: MDA_ALL_EXCEL_COMBINED.xlsx).

### 9.3 LaGrange/Elkhart, Indiana: The RV Belt

This settlement is inextricably linked to the Recreational Vehicle (RV) industry. A substantial percentage of Amish men work in RV factories. Wages are high, but the work is secular and subject to national boom-bust cycles. During the 2008 crash, unemployment skyrocketed; during the COVID-19 RV boom, wages soared. This reliance on "English" corporations distinguishes Indiana Amish from the more independent entrepreneurs of Ohio and Pennsylvania.

## 10. Future Outlook and Constraints

### 10.1 Growth Projections

The trends carry forward mechanically. By 2050, the Amish are expected to cross the one-million threshold. They will transform from a fringe group into a substantial minority bloc in states such as Ohio and Pennsylvania, with potential to alter the political landscape of rural districts.

### 10.2 Primary Constraint: Land

The primary constraint on this growth is physical: **land**. The Amish reliance on horse transportation limits the radius of any settlement. As core settlements like Lancaster and Holmes reach saturation, three pressures will intensify:

1. **Accelerated Westward Migration:** More settlements in Colorado, Wyoming, and Nebraska.
2. **Subdivision of Farms:** Continued shrinkage pushing more men into non-farm work.
3. **Increased Friction:** Density-driven conflicts with local zoning boards over sanitation, schooling, and road maintenance.

## 11. Conclusion

The 2024–2025 statistics confirm what the preceding decade analyses established. The Amish are not a relic; they constitute a dynamic, growing, and increasingly wealthy sub-society, doubling every generation, surviving in business at rates that exceed the corporate sector, and accumulating billions in capital. This success is breeding new challenges: the gap between the Amish Millionaire and the Lunch Pail laborer is widening; pressure on land is accelerating. Nevertheless, the numbers tell a story of **extraordinary vitality**.

On the same calendar, in the same country, two trajectories exist. One is collapsing into incoherence. The other is doubling every twenty years on a coherence reserve that mid-twentieth-century America itself could not match.

This is the current state. From this point, the series turns to the four vectors of collapse—Family, Truth, Money, Identity—and then to the Amish proof in full. The decade analysis concludes; the mechanism analysis begins.
