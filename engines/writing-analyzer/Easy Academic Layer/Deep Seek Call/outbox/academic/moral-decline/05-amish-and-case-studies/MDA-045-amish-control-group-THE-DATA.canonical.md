# The Amish as a Controlled Experiment: Structural Coherence, Binding Variables, and the Thermodynamics of Social Systems

## Abstract

Between 1960 and 2025, every measurable indicator of American social coherence underwent sustained decline: family structure fragmented, institutional trust eroded, mental health deteriorated, substance abuse escalated, and civic participation diminished across all demographic categories and geographic regions. During this identical period, the Old Order Amish—occupying the same national territory, subject to the same federal jurisdiction, participating in the same macroeconomic environment, and exposed to the same cultural pressures—exhibited systematic improvement across analogous metrics. This paper proposes that the Amish constitute a natural controlled experiment: the broader American society serves as the test group, the Amish as the control, and the independent variable is the maintenance or dissolution of a binding field—designated here as the Ordnung, or in the formal notation of the Theophysics framework, the parameter χ. The United States effectively set the external field parameter *h* → 0 beginning circa 1968 through the systematic dismantling of institutional authority structures. The Amish never did. The resulting divergence across six decades of demographic, psychological, economic, and health data is presented as evidence for the thermodynamic necessity of binding constraints in maintaining complex social coherence.

---

## I. Introduction: The Controlled Experiment Framework

The present investigation treats the Old Order Amish not as an ethnographic curiosity but as a structurally controlled population within which the binding variable—external authority codified as the Ordnung—has been maintained continuously across the period under examination. The broader American society, by contrast, underwent a phase transition in which the same class of binding variables was systematically attenuated or eliminated across legal, monetary, epistemic, familial, and identity domains.

This isomorphism was identified through structural comparison of two populations sharing the same national boundaries, legal framework, economic conditions, and cultural environment, yet diverging sharply in outcomes following the late 1960s. The methodological premise is that this divergence cannot be attributed to geographic, legal, or macroeconomic confounders, as both populations share these factors. The remaining explanatory variable is the presence or absence of a maintained binding field.

The data presented herein are not intended as sentimental advocacy for the Amish way of life. The Amish exhibit documented structural failures—genetic bottleneck diseases, inadequate abuse reporting infrastructure, educational ceilings limiting individual mobility—which are acknowledged explicitly because they strengthen rather than weaken the coherence argument. A system need not be perfect to be structurally sound; it requires only that its binding variable be maintained.

## II. Demographic Trajectory: Population Growth Against the National Trend

While the United States total fertility rate declined from 3.65 children per woman in 1960 to 1.62 in 2023—falling below replacement level for the first time in national history—the Amish population followed a markedly divergent trajectory.

**Table 1: Amish and U.S. Population Growth, 1960–2025**

| Year | Amish Population | U.S. Population | Amish % of U.S. |
|------|-----------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 1960 | ~28,000 | 179 million | 0.016% |
| 1970 | ~39,000 | 203 million | 0.019% |
| 1980 | ~57,000 | 226 million | 0.025% |
| 1990 | ~80,000 | 249 million | 0.032% |
| 2000 | ~177,910 | 281 million | 0.063% |
| 2010 | ~249,000 | 309 million | 0.081% |
| 2020 | ~350,000 | 331 million | 0.106% |
| 2025 | 410,955 | 340 million | 0.121% |

*Sources: Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies, Amish Population Profile 2025; U.S. Census Bureau decennial census and population estimates.*

The Amish population doubled approximately every 20–22 years throughout this period. Between 2000 and 2025, the population grew by 131%—from 177,910 to 410,955. During this same interval, U.S. total fertility fell below replacement, the "deaths of despair" epidemic (Case & Deaton, 2020) claimed over one million lives, and the Census Bureau began projecting population decline by mid-century.

This growth is not attributable to immigration or conversion. The Amish do not actively proselytize, and conversion to the faith is rare. Population increase is entirely endogenous: large families averaging 6–7 children combined with high retention rates (see Section III).

In 2025 alone, nine new settlements were established and zero dissolved. The Amish added 76 new church districts in a single year. Geographic expansion has extended into states—Montana, Idaho, Maine, South Dakota—that had no Amish presence a generation ago. Kentucky, which had near-zero Amish population in 1990, exceeded 15,000 by 2024.

Approximately 40–45% of new settlements fail within 20 years (Luthy, 2009). This failure rate is not concealed but is integral to the system: the Amish permit experimental communities to form, test viability, and dissolve without treating failure as catastrophic. The net vector remains sharply upward.

## III. Retention: The Rumspringa Data

The popular narrative—that Amish adolescents experience a period of freedom (Rumspringa) and subsequently depart the community in large numbers—is empirically unsupported.

**Table 3: Amish Youth Retention Rates by Period**

| Period | Retention | Source |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| Pre-1950s | ~50–70% (variable) | Meyers (1994) |
| 1960s–1970s | ~70–75% | Hostetler (1993) |
| 1980s–1990s | ~80–85% | Kraybill (2001) |
| 2000s–2010s | ~85–95% | Kraybill, Johnson-Weiner, & Nolt (2013) |

*Sources: Kraybill, Johnson-Weiner, & Nolt, The Amish (2013); Stevick, Growing Up Amish (2014).*

Subgroup variation is instructive. Lancaster County reports 90–95% retention. The conservative Swartzentruber Amish retain approximately 90%. Andy Weaver Amish report approximately 97%. Even the more progressive New Order Amish retain 60–70%.

The trend is upward: retention has increased over the same decades that American institutional membership—churches, civic organizations, marriages, political parties—hemorrhaged members. The Amish retained more of their young as the surrounding culture lost more of theirs.

The most counterintuitive finding is that retention improved after Amish parochial schools replaced public schools, following *Wisconsin v. Yoder* (1972). Separating Amish children from the public school system did not produce ignorant defectors; it produced committed members who chose to remain at rates their grandparents never achieved.

Of the 10–15% who leave permanently, most depart before baptism, meaning they face no shunning (*Meidung*). The system provides an exit without maximum-cost punishment for those who choose it unbaptized.

## IV. Mental Health: The Egeland Data

In 1983, Janice Egeland and Andrea Hostetter published a landmark three-part study in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* examining affective disorders among 12,500 Old Order Amish in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania.

### Key Findings (Egeland & Hostetter, 1983)

| Metric | Amish | U.S. General Population (same era) |
|--------|-------|-----------------------------------|
| Total active mental illness cases identified | 112 / 12,500 | — |
| Overall prevalence of any mental illness | ~0.9% | — |
| Major depression prevalence | ~0.33% | ~5–6% |

*Source: American Journal of Psychiatry* 140 (1983): 56–61, 62–66, 67–71.

The Amish depression rate was approximately 1/15 of the national average. A critical methodological note from Egeland: because alcohol abuse, drug abuse, and sociopathy were "essentially absent" in the Amish community, diagnosis was cleaner than in general population studies. These confounding variables—which complicate psychiatric research in other populations—simply did not exist.

### Suicide

| Population | Rate (per 100,000) |
|------------|-------------------|
| Amish (Lancaster County, 1980) | 5.5 |
| U.S. national average (same period) | ~13 |
| U.S. national average (2022) | ~14.5 |

*Sources: Egeland data; CDC WISQARS.*

The Amish suicide rate was less than half the national rate. The national rate has since risen; the Amish rate has not tracked upward.

### The Youth Mental Health Contrast

Between 2013 and 2023, the prevalence of depression among American adolescents rose from 8.2% to 13.1% (CDC). Sixteen percent of U.S. adolescents aged 12–17 now carry diagnosed anxiety. The Surgeon General declared loneliness a public health epidemic (U.S. Surgeon General, 2023).

Amish adolescents—who have no smartphones, no social media, no algorithmic attention capture—do not appear in these statistics. No rigorous prevalence study of Amish adolescent mental health exists (a genuine research gap), but the absence of the technological vectors that drive the American youth mental health crisis is itself data.

## V. Physical Health

### Obesity (Bassett et al., 2004)

| Metric | Amish Men | Amish Women | U.S. Average |
|--------|-----------|-------------|--------------|
| Steps per day | 18,425 | 14,196 | ~3,000–5,000 |
| Obesity rate (BMI ≥30) | 0% | 9% | 31% (2004) / 42% (2023) |
| Overweight (BMI ≥25) | 25% | 27% | ~70% |

*Source: Bassett et al., Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* (January 2004); University of Tennessee.

Zero percent of Amish men were obese. The U.S. male obesity rate has since climbed to over 40%. Amish men averaged over 18,000 steps per day—nearly four times the American average. This is not exercise but work: farming, construction, carpentry. The physical demands of Amish life correspond to the demands for which human bodies evolved.

### Asthma and Immune Function (Stein et al., 2016)

The *New England Journal of Medicine* published a study comparing Amish farm children to Hutterite farm children—two genetically similar Anabaptist populations with different farming practices.

| Metric | Amish vs. Hutterite |
|--------|-------------------|
| Asthma prevalence | 4× lower |
| Allergic sensitization | 6× lower |
| Endotoxin in house dust | 6.8× higher |

*Source: Stein et al., NEJM* 375 (2016): 411–421.

The Amish immune advantage derives from traditional farming practices that expose children to microbial diversity. The immune system, evolved for a world of dust and animals, malfunctions in sterile environments.

### Cardiovascular Protection (Pollin et al., 2008)

Approximately 5% of Lancaster County Amish carry the R19X null mutation in the *APOC3* gene. Carriers exhibit significantly lower triglycerides, higher HDL cholesterol, and 65% lower odds (OR = 0.35) of coronary artery calcification. No detrimental effects were observed.

*Source: Pollin et al., Science* 322 (2008): 1702–1705; University of Maryland.

This represents a genetic lottery win specific to the Amish founder population—a reminder that the same genetic bottleneck causing elevated rates of rare diseases also delivered protective variants. Biology is a trade-off matrix, not a morality tale.

## VI. Economics: The 95% Survival Rate

| Metric | Amish | U.S. Average |
|--------|-------|-------------|
| 5-year business survival | ~95% | ~50% |

*Sources: Wesner, Success Made Simple* (2010); Kraybill report (2009); CNN Money; Kauffman Foundation. Settlement-level failure rates: 2.6%–4.2%.

The explanatory factors are structural rather than cultural:

1. **Community Pre-Screening**: Elders and experienced businesspeople evaluate ideas before launch. Bad plans are redirected before capital is expended.

2. **Zero Debt Startup Culture**: Most Amish businesses launch with personal savings and family loans, not bank debt. No leverage means no interest payments driving premature failure.

3. **Built-In Customer Base**: The Amish community provides initial demand, reducing the cold-start problem.

4. **Low Overhead**: No commercial health insurance premiums. No Social Security taxes (exempt since 1965). Often no rent—workshops built on family land.

5. **Failure Is Not Catastrophic**: Mutual aid catches failed entrepreneurs. They do not face homelessness or bankruptcy.

### The Mutual Aid Economy

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Monthly mutual aid contribution | ~$90–110 / member |
| Coverage of major medical bills | 80% (member pays 20%) |
| Membership rate | 81% report membership |
| Will community help with healthcare? | >90% agree |
| Barn raising rebuild time | 1–3 days |
| Single fundraiser auction (catastrophic care) | Up to $300,000 |

*Sources: "Sharing the Load: Amish Healthcare Financing," PMC (2016); Young Center; Kraybill et al. (2013).*

Compare this to the American system: average employer-sponsored family health insurance premium in 2024 was $25,572 per year. The Amish pay approximately $1,200 per year per member and receive 80% coverage backed by community obligation rather than actuarial tables.

The trade-off is real: 39% of Amish respondents reported "sometimes" avoiding recommended care due to cost. This is not a system without friction. However, it is a system without medical bankruptcy—which destroys approximately 530,000 American families per year (Himmelstein et al., 2019).

## VII. Criminal Justice: The Missing Data Point

There are virtually no Amish people in American prisons. No formal per-capita study exists comparing Amish incarceration to surrounding counties. This absence is itself significant: the population is large enough (410,955, approximately the size of Cleveland) that if they incarcerated at the national rate (~0.7% of adults), there would be approximately 2,000 Amish inmates. There are not.

Amish criminal violations tend to be regulatory: building code infractions, buggy safety equipment disputes, puppy mill violations in some settlements. Violent crime within Amish communities is extremely rare in reported data. The church discipline system—confession before the congregation, temporary or permanent shunning (*Meidung*)—handles most interpersonal offenses internally. This constitutes both a strength and a vulnerability, as discussed in Section VIII.

*Sources: Young Center, "Deviance"; Plain Anabaptist Journal*, "The Crime Experiences of the Amish."

## VIII. Documented Structural Failures

A case study that conceals flaws constitutes propaganda, not analysis. The Amish system exhibits documented structural failures.

### Genetic Disease

The founder effect—descent from approximately 200 18th-century immigrants—concentrates recessive genetic disorders:

| Disorder | Amish Prevalence | General Population Prevalence |
|----------|-----------------|------------------------------|
| Maple syrup urine disease | 1 / 358 | 1 / 185,000 |
| Glutaric aciduria type I | Elevated | — |
| Ellis-van Creveld syndrome | Elevated | — |
| Cohen syndrome / Crigler-Najjar | Elevated | — |

The Clinic for Special Children in Strasburg, Pennsylvania (founded by Dr. Holmes Morton, MacArthur Fellow) treats these conditions. It is funded largely by Amish community fundraisers—mutual aid applied to genetic inevitability.

### Sexual Abuse and Domestic Violence

This constitutes the system's most significant failure. Investigative journalism (Reuters, 2020; *Cosmopolitan*, 2020) documented cases across multiple settlements where sexual abuse was handled through church confession rather than law enforcement. Structural factors enabling this include:

- Patriarchal authority structures
- Theology of forgiveness that pressures victims to reconcile
- Cultural reluctance to involve "English" law enforcement
- Limited access to outside support services
- No reliable prevalence data because reporting rates are extremely low

No system built on human authority is immune to the abuse of that authority. The Amish binding variable (external authority/Ordnung) constrains entropy—but when the authority itself becomes the source of harm, the system's own design impedes correction. This is the cost of strong boundaries: they retain harmful elements as effectively as they exclude them. Some settlements have begun forming Amish Safety Committees. Progress is slow.

### Educational Ceiling

*Wisconsin v. Yoder* (1972) granted the Amish legal permission to end formal education at eighth grade. The consequences:

- Functional literacy is very high (~97%+)
- Vocational competency is strong (construction, farming, small engine repair, furniture making)
- Higher-order analytical skills, scientific literacy, and critical reasoning are not formally developed
- Individuals who leave the community face significant educational disadvantage in the broader economy

This is by design. The Amish are not failing to educate; they are choosing what to educate *for*. The trade-off is explicit: depth of community integration versus breadth of individual capability. Justice Douglas's partial dissent in *Yoder*—arguing that the children's own wishes should have been considered, not merely the parents'—remains unresolved. The tension between communal coherence and individual autonomy is real, and the Amish system resolves it in favor of the community.

## IX. Decade-by-Decade Comparative Analysis

### 1960s

**America**: The Pill introduced (1960). Virtue word frequency begins terminal decline (1962). SAT scores peak and turn downward (1963). Violent crime inflection point (1964). Great Society expansion (1964–1965). Hays Code abandoned (1968). No-fault divorce begins (1969).

**Amish**: Population ~28,000→39,000. Retention ~70–75%. Begin establishing parochial schools, withdrawing children from consolidated public schools. The Ordnung holds. No metrics invert.

### 1970s

**America**: Gold standard abandoned (1971). *Roe v. Wade* (1973). Watergate (1974). Trust in government falls from 77% to 36%. Divorce rate peaks (~1980). Violent crime rate doubles from 1965 levels.

**Amish**: *Wisconsin v. Yoder* (1972) grants legal protection for educational system. Population growing steadily. Retention begins improving. New settlements at ~55 per decade. The binding variable is legally ratified.

### 1980s

**America**: Latchkey children generation. Crack epidemic. AIDS crisis. National debt triples under Reagan. Non-marital births continue climbing (28% by 1990). "Defining Deviancy Down" becomes visible.

**Amish**: Population ~57,000→80,000. Business enterprise shifts from farming to small manufacturing and construction (driven by rising farmland costs). The 95% business survival rate emerges in this transition. Mutual aid systems scale. Retention climbs to ~80–85%.

### 1990s

**America**: Internet arrives. Oklahoma City bombing. Columbine. Monica Lewinsky. Trust in media falls below 55%. Non-marital births hit 33%. "Bowling Alone" documented (Putnam, 2000).

**Amish**: Population ~80,000→177,910. Settlement count nearly doubles (148→276). Geographic expansion into Wisconsin, Michigan, Kentucky begins. The community that rejected television in the 1950s now rejects the internet—same principle, new technology, same decision.

### 2000s

**America**: 9/11. Iraq War. Hurricane Katrina. Financial crisis (2008). Trust in government permanently below 30%. Smartphone introduced (2007). Facebook grows 1,300% (2005–2010). Opioid epidemic begins.

**Amish**: Population nearly doubles (177,910→249,000). 180 new settlements founded in a single decade—the fastest expansion in their history. After the Nickel Mines schoolhouse shooting (2006), the Amish response—immediate, public forgiveness of the shooter's family—becomes one of the most-covered stories of the decade.

### 2010s

**America**: Youth depression rises from 8.2% to 13.1% (2013–2023). Social media saturates: 95% of teens aged 13–17 use it, many "almost constantly." Surgeon General declares loneliness an epidemic. Obesity reaches 42%. Trust in media hits all-time low (28%). Deaths of despair exceed 100,000/year.

**Amish**: Population ~249,000→350,000. Retention remains 85–95%. Zero percent obesity among men. Depression prevalence unchanged from Egeland baseline. Cell phone adoption occurs selectively—for business, kept outside the home, evaluated district-by-district through the Ordnung process. The binding variable adapts without dissolving.

### 2020s

**America**: COVID-19 (1.2M+ deaths). January 6. AI disruption begins. Birth rate hits record low (1.62). Church membership falls below 50% for first time in Gallup's 80-year history. 54% of adults report feeling lonely. National debt exceeds $35 trillion.

**Amish**: Population passes 400,000. 684 settlements across 32 states. 3,114 church districts. Nine new settlements founded in 2025 alone, zero dissolved. Population growth rate: 3–3.5% per year. Doubling time: ~20 years.

## X. Structural Interpretation

The Amish are not better people. They are not smarter, kinder, or more virtuous than other Americans. They constitute a population embedded in a structural system that maintains its binding variable.

### America after 1968

The external field (*h*) was set to zero. Authority was dismantled—in law (no-fault divorce), in money (fiat currency), in truth (post-truth media), in identity (expressive individualism). The system underwent a phase transition from ordered to disordered. Entropy maximized. This is thermodynamically inevitable when constraints are removed from a complex system.

### The Amish (Always)

The external field (*h*) was maintained. The Ordnung is the field. It is not a law imposed from outside but an unwritten code maintained by consensus within each church district of 25–35 families. It is evaluated, updated, and enforced at the local level. Technologies are assessed by whether they strengthen or weaken the field. Those that weaken it are rejected. Those that strengthen or are neutral are adopted.

The critical insight is not that the Amish reject modernity. They do not. They use diesel generators, pneumatic tools, LED lighting, solar panels, GPS for farming, and increasingly, cell phones. What they reject is any technology that shifts the vector of attention from the community to the individual.

The automobile is banned not because it is complex but because it dissolves geographic necessity. Television is banned not because it is entertaining but because it replaces active participation with passive consumption. The smartphone is banned from the home not because it is useful (it is—they use them) but because it brings the infinite outside world into the finite domestic sphere without friction.

The Amish are engineering their own coherence field. They are doing so with the same physics governing every other system: strong binding forces (mutual aid, geographic proximity, shared language), well-defined boundaries (Ordnung, plain dress, technological selectivity), and active rejection of entropy sources (media saturation, financial leverage, individual autonomy as highest value).

The data indicate that this approach is effective. Not perfectly. Not without cost. But effective.

## XI. Conclusion: The Experiment Continues

America and the Amish began from the same point. They diverged at the same moment—the late 1960s. One set *h* = 0. The other held *h* constant. Sixty years later, the results are unambiguous.

The system that maintained its binding variable—its external authority, its shared definitions, its constraints on individual entropy—grew 1,367% (from 28,000 to 410,955). Its members are less depressed, less obese, less isolated, less indebted, less incarcerated, and more likely to remain in their community than at any point in their history.

The system that removed its binding variable—in law, in money, in truth, in family, in identity—faces population decline, a mental health crisis, institutional collapse, $35 trillion in debt, and a loneliness epidemic affecting a majority of its citizens.

The Amish did not require a government program. They did not require a platform. They did not require an algorithm. They required the Ordnung—the field—and 25 families willing to enforce it upon themselves.

The mathematics is identical across domains. The domain of application is sociology. The variable is χ.
