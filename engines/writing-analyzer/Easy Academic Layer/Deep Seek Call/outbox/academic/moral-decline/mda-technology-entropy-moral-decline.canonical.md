# The Entropic Society: Technology, Obsolescence, and the Devaluation of American Moral Capital (1950–2025)

## Abstract

This investigation examines the relationship between technological evolution and the degradation of moral capital in American society from 1950 to the present. Through a critical-theoretic framework integrating philosophy of technology with longitudinal sociological data, the analysis posits that technology functions not merely as instrumental apparatus but as an exteriorization of human will—a cultural and ontological force that embodies and propagates specific value orientations. The study identifies three sequential technological phases—Mass Mediation (1950–1985), Individualized Access (1985–2005), and Perpetual Connectivity (2005–present)—and correlates each phase with distinct patterns of moral decline across three domains: relational coherence, informational integrity, and psychological entropy. The central finding indicates that the technological imperative of efficiency and maximization, when exteriorized without adequate institutional control, generates an aesthetic of entropic transience that structurally models and enables moral decay, culminating in the engineered instability of the human psyche. The analysis rejects simple technological determinism, instead identifying differential and sequential technological impacts mediated by pre-existing cultural, economic, and demographic forces.

## Section I: The Technology of Human Will

### A Conceptual and Critical Definition

The investigation into the moral trajectory of American society requires a philosophical definition of technology that transcends mere tool-usage. Technology, within this framework, is understood not simply as hardware or software, but as a cultural and ontological force—an **exteriorization of human will** (Feenberg, 2002).¹ It constitutes the primary mechanism by which contemporary society organizes itself and achieves coherence.² Consequently, the trajectory of technological development reflects fundamental shifts in collective human values, particularly the impulse toward maximization and control.

### A. Technology as Exteriorized Intent and Cultural Force

Technology’s role as a cultural determinant situates its study within the humanities philosophy of technology, which emphasizes its meaning and societal impact.² If philosophy seeks to understand how things in the broadest sense relate, then the vast scope and complexity of technological systems compel analysis of their moral footprint. This conceptual framework requires acknowledging that technology is fundamentally **value-laden**.¹ Technical systems embody the goals of efficiency and power. When humans engage with the world through technology, they are, by necessity, adopting a maximizing and controlling approach. This approach, when applied ubiquitously, determines what may be termed a "technological way of life."

### B. The Moral Status of the Artifact: Critical Theory and Controllability

A Critical Theory of technology provides the analytical lens necessary to investigate the observed entropic decay. This perspective agrees with instrumentalism that technology is controllable, yet concurs with substantivism that it is inherently value-laden.¹ This seemingly paradoxical position suggests that while technology embeds values such as efficiency, it is possible to tame it by submitting development to a more democratic process of design and deployment. The central premise holds that the societal problem resides not in technology itself, but in the **institutional failure to design systems and institutions that allow human control over technological development**.¹

### C. The Architectural Vectors of Technological Emergence

The three-phase technological progression identified in this analysis is characterized as follows:

**Phase 1: 1950–1985 — Mass Mediation.** This period was defined by unidirectional, passive, communal consumption through broadcast television, radio, and landline telephony. Television approached near-saturation during this era, fundamentally reshaping attention patterns and domestic time allocation.³ This phase established centralized cultural narratives and imposed high passive attention demands.⁴

**Phase 2: 1985–2005 — Individualized Access.** This phase featured personalized computing and active information retrieval via the personal computer and early internet. By 2000, approximately half of U.S. adults were online.⁵ This period decentralized information and empowered individual retrieval. By 2005, two-thirds of U.S. adults owned cell phones.⁶

**Phase 3: 2005–Present — Perpetual Connectivity.** This current phase is characterized by mobile devices, high-speed connectivity, and social media platforms. As of the most recent data, 96% of U.S. adults are online.⁵ Facebook experienced 1,300% growth between 2005 and 2010.⁶ Currently, 95% of youth aged 13–17 use social media, with many reporting "almost constant" usage.⁷

## Section II: The Aesthetic Transition

### From Analog Durability to Digital Entropy

### A. The Patina of Permanence (Analog Durability)

The earlier period of manufacturing embraced an aesthetic of analog durability. This design philosophy valued high-quality materials, products built for repair, and longevity.⁸ The physical artifact was intended to absorb the effects of time, accruing subjective value—the "patina" of use—which encouraged long-term investment, repair, and transmission across generations. This embedded cultural value in physical goods naturally reinforced social stability through material continuity.

### B. The Ascent of Planned and Perceived Obsolescence

The critical aesthetic shift involves the acceptance of an **expendable aesthetic** and **planned obsolescence**.⁹ This principle dictates that products are designed with an intentionally short lifespan, viewing objects as "short-life toys" or replaceable components within temporary frames. The transition from durable to disposable artifacts represents a fundamental reorientation of the relationship between material culture and temporal value.

### C. Entropy as a Digital Design Principle

In modern, highly specialized consumer electronics, entropy is not a failure but an engineered feature—the **commodification of systemic decay**. Digital entropy manifests through closed hardware ecosystems, non-replaceable components, and software incompatibility. A physically functioning device is rendered useless by digital decay, ensuring perpetual consumption. If technological artifacts constitute the exteriorization of human intent, then the shift toward designed decay indicates a societal and corporate will that prioritizes maximum extraction over cultural permanence and stability.

## Section III: Longitudinal Data Synthesis

### Technology and Decline Vectors (1950–2025)

### A. Relational Coherence: Fragmentation of Social Bonds

#### 1. Decline in Civic Participation

The withdrawal of citizens from voluntary associations and the unraveling of social connections occurred **gradually and steadily after the 1950s and 1960s**.⁴ Social capital theory attributes this decline principally to **generational replacement** (accounting for approximately 50% of the decline) and the rise of **television-watching** (accounting for approximately 25% of the decline).⁴ One-person households rose from 7.7% (1940) to 27.6% (2020)—a systemic trend toward structural isolation spanning all three technological phases.¹¹

#### 2. Marital Coherence and Isolation

The U.S. divorce rate peaked around 1980 and has generally declined since.¹² This peak occurred during the transition from Phase 1 to Phase 2. The fact that marital instability peaked before the mass proliferation of personal computers, the Internet, and smartphones suggests that changes in family structure were driven by macro-social and cultural forces **distinct from and preceding the digital revolution**.

### B. Informational Integrity: Erosion of Institutional Trust

Trust in government declined from 75% (1958) to below 30% (2007–present).¹³ Trust began eroding during the 1960s amid the Vietnam War and continued falling through the 1970s with Watergate.¹³ Media confidence fell from 68–72% in the 1970s to 53% by 1997.¹⁵ Phase 1 generated **disillusionment**—loss of faith in centralized authorities. Phase 3 subsequently accelerated **fragmentation**, providing a platform for decentralized, often dubious information that thrives where foundational trust has already been destroyed.¹⁶

### C. Psychological Entropy: Internal Systemic Breakdown

The contemporary crisis is marked by temporal alignment of the mental distress spike with the saturation point of social media adoption.¹⁷ Experimental studies establish a **causal link** between limited platform use and improved well-being.²³ Frequent social media use may be associated with distinct changes in the developing brain's amygdala (emotional learning) and prefrontal cortex (impulse control and emotional regulation).⁷ These changes increase sensitivity to social rewards and punishments, engineering a perpetually unstable internal state.

## Section IV: Correlation Analysis

### Establishing Linkages Between Entropy and Decline

**Phase 1: Foundation of Decay.** Television functionally replaced active group participation with isolated, domestic leisure, leading to the gradual "unraveling" of social capital.⁴ Simultaneously, broadcast media centralized the dissemination of political failures, generating pervasive disillusionment.¹³

**Phase 2: Structural Dispersion.** This phase functions less as a primary cause and more as a structural accelerant of existing trends. It provided tools for individualized retreat, contributing to the accelerating trend toward one-person households.¹¹ The mode of interaction remained active (retrieval-based).

**Phase 3: Acute Psychological Entropy.** This phase exhibits the highest and most acute correlation with decline, specifically in internal psychological stability. Mass smartphone and social media adoption (post-2013) precisely corresponds with the youth anxiety and depression spike.¹⁷ Causal evidence has been confirmed.²³ The algorithmic objective of maximizing attention capture leads to a technological environment that interferes with the biological mechanisms of emotional regulation and sleep. The efficiency goal of the technical system directly causes internal systemic breakdown.

## Section V: Falsification and Alternative Pathways

### A. The Non-Deterministic Counter-Thesis

Technology primarily accelerates or provides a platform for pre-existing societal tensions and trends.²⁴ The societal harms observed are not necessarily the inevitable outcome of technological progress, but rather the consequence of society failing to implement adequate civic or institutional structures to manage the pace of change.

### B. Socioeconomic and Political Drivers

**Economic Inequality.** Rising inequality fosters a competitive economic climate that directly erodes social cohesion.²⁶ This correlates with increased moralization—the rise in harsh moral language and judgments. Economic strain acts as a major confounding variable that both initiates and magnifies technological harm.

**Political Polarization.** The perception of moral free fall constitutes a point of agreement across political affiliations.²⁷ Political and ideological conflicts substantially predate Phase 3 technology. Technology did not create the division but provided a highly efficient platform for existing moral conflict to be amplified.

### C. Generational Replacement and Cultural Momentum

Robert Putnam's definitive analysis places overwhelming emphasis on **generational replacement**.⁴ The replacement of the "long civic generation" by generations with less inherent proclivity for group involvement accounts for approximately half of the decline in relational coherence. The decay of civic bonds was fundamentally a cultural shift—facilitated by Phase 1 technology (television) but driven primarily by demographic momentum.

## Section VI: Conclusion and Policy Recommendations

This investigation confirms a profound, temporally specific correlation between technological evolution and the degradation of American moral capital from 1950 to the present. The analysis rejects a simple, single-cause narrative and establishes that the impact of technology has been **differential and sequential**.

### Core Finding

The technological imperative of efficiency and maximization, when exteriorized without institutional control, creates an aesthetic of entropic transience (planned obsolescence) that structurally models and enables moral decay, culminating in the **engineered instability of the human psyche**.

### Recommendations for Restoring Moral Capital

**1. Democratizing Technology Design.** Mandate design transparency, allow public scrutiny of algorithmic objectives, and hold corporations accountable for negative externalities. Establish institutions directing technological development toward social stability.¹

**2. Countering Digital Entropy.** Implement stringent "Right-to-Repair" legal frameworks for hardware and software. By encouraging durability and discouraging planned obsolescence, reinforce cultural values of investment, repair, and long-term stability.⁸

**3. Mitigating Psychological Entropy.** Mandate age-appropriate usage limits, restrict features interfering with sleep, and prohibit designs engineered to overstimulate developing brains. Prioritize community-based interventions emphasizing non-mediated social interaction.⁷
