# The Logos Thesis: Information as Ontologically Primitive and the Closure Condition of Reality

## Abstract

This article examines the proposition that information constitutes an ontologically primitive substrate of reality, with material phenomena emerging as information under constraint. The central thesis—designated the *Logos Thesis*—posits that the Logos functions as the closure condition of reality rather than as an ornamental or supplementary theological construct. The argument proceeds through four structural moves: (1) articulation of the grounding problem for information prior to material instantiation; (2) identification of the epistemological crisis that motivates the inquiry; (3) presentation of the Logos as the framework within which information achieves ontological priority; and (4) specification of criteria for empirical falsifiability. This treatment constitutes an interlude within a larger canonical argument; the complete derivation, including formal proofs, tabular data, and source structure, is preserved in the accompanying full-length treatment.

## 1. Introduction: The Grounding Problem for Information

The foundational question motivating this inquiry is: *What grounds information before matter appears?* If information is posited as ontologically primitive—that is, as existing prior to and independently of material instantiation—then material phenomena may be understood as information under constraint. This formulation inverts the conventional physicalist ontology, in which information is typically regarded as an emergent property of material configurations. The present thesis instead proposes that matter represents a constrained state of an underlying informational substrate.

This inversion raises a critical metaphysical question: if information is primitive, what provides the conditions under which information can exist, be structured, and undergo constraint? The Logos Thesis answers this question by identifying the Logos as the closure condition of reality—the necessary and sufficient ground that renders information intelligible, stable, and capable of constraint without requiring prior material instantiation.

## 3. The Logos as Closure Condition: Formal Statement

Let \(\mathcal{I}\) denote the set of all possible informational states, and let \(\mathcal{M}\) denote the set of all possible material configurations. The conventional physicalist ontology posits \(\mathcal{M}\) as primitive, with \(\mathcal{I}\) emergent: \(\mathcal{I} = f(\mathcal{M})\). The Logos Thesis inverts this relation:

\[
\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{I} \mid_{\mathcal{C}}
\]

where \(\mid_{\mathcal{C}}\) denotes the operation of constraint applied to the informational substrate \(\mathcal{I}\), and \(\mathcal{C}\) represents the set of constraint conditions that yield material phenomena. The Logos, denoted \(\Lambda\), is identified as the closure condition that makes \(\mathcal{I}\) both self-consistent and capable of generating \(\mathcal{M}\):

\[
\Lambda := \text{the closure condition of } \mathcal{I} \text{ such that } \mathcal{I} \mid_{\mathcal{C}} \rightarrow \mathcal{M}
\]

This formulation entails that \(\Lambda\) is not an additional entity within the ontology but rather the condition under which the ontology itself is coherent. The Logos is thus the ground of intelligibility for both information and matter, and it is in this sense that it is "not decoration on the theory."

## 4. Scriptural and Theological Context

The term *Logos* is employed in its Johannine sense (John 1:1–3, NA28: *Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ Λόγος*), wherein the Logos is identified as the principle through which all things were made and without which nothing was made that has been made. The present thesis does not reduce this theological claim to a physical principle but rather identifies a structural isomorphism between the Johannine Logos and the closure condition required by an information-ontological physics.

This isomorphism was identified through structural comparison of the logical requirements for informational closure (as derived from information theory and quantum mechanics) with the theological properties attributed to the Logos in patristic and scholastic traditions. The correspondence is not asserted as a proof of the theological claim but as a demonstration of coherence between the two domains.
