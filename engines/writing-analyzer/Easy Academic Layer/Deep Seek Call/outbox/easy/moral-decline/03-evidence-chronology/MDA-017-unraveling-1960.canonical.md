# The Unraveling (1960–1967)

## Executive Summary

The years 1960 to 1967 marked the end of the stable, high-trust moral agreement that had defined the post-World War II era. This wasn't a slow decay. It was a fast, structural collapse caused by new technology, mass education, and national crises of trust.

The decade started at the high point of traditionalism — the lowest average marriage age ever recorded and peak trust in institutions. But almost immediately, that foundation shattered. The birth control pill gave people control over reproduction. The high school graduation rate hit **69.5% in 1960**, creating a huge, educated group of young people ready to reject the rigid conformity of their parents.

At the same time, the Vietnam War and civil rights tensions caused a sharp drop in public confidence in government authority. The moral story of 1960–1967 is about **moral authority moving** from centralized institutions — Church, State, Nuclear Family — to the individual and emerging counter-cultural movements.

The 1960s didn't start with rebellion. It started at peak trust — and ran out of it in seven years.

## Part 2 · Expert Insights

### Family — Enovid and the End of the Marriage Economy

The most important development for family morality was the **immediate availability and adoption of Enovid (the Birth Control Pill)**. This moral technology fundamentally broke the structure of the early marriage model (median age at 20.3 for women).

The Pill separated sexual intimacy from the risk of pregnancy and immediate marriage obligation. This instantly made the old moral economy of marriage — where sex was often traded for security and legitimacy — conceptually obsolete. Like a parent holding a child's hand — loose grip when you're walking together, iron grip when you try to run into traffic. The closer you stay, the more freedom you feel. The Pill loosened the grip, and the old rules couldn't hold.

This technological freedom sped up the rising divorce rate (2.6 per 1,000 by 1967) and set up the practical foundation for the eventual decline of the nuclear family ideal.

### Sexuality — Kinsey and the Secularization of Desire

The critical development was the **widespread cultural spread of the Kinsey Reports**. Kinsey's work brought scientific measurement into a area previously controlled only by religious and social norms. The presentation of data — that up to 50% of women had had sex before marriage, and that sexuality existed on a spectrum — effectively **made sexual morality secular**.

By redefining "deviance" as just statistical difference, Kinsey created the intellectual basis for the era's sexual liberation. He gave the new educated youth group the justification to reject the public facade of purity.

### Education — The Mass-Educated Reckoning

The most important shift was the **high school graduation rate surging past 70%** for groups entering adulthood. This solidified the creation of the **mass-educated, critically aware citizen**.

A highly socialized, intellectually capable youth group had the analytical tools to examine and reject the moral and political contradictions of their parents' generation: the tension between democratic ideals and racial segregation, the credibility of the escalating Vietnam War. The moral result was the rise of organized protest movements that challenged institutional authority on grounds of morality and reason.

### Economic — The War on Poverty and Equality of Outcome

The most important moral development was the **passing of the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 (The War on Poverty)**. This act went beyond the New Deal's promise of providing security and wrote into law the federal government's definite moral commitment to **legislate economic and social equality of outcome**.

It established the belief that the government had a moral duty not just to prevent widespread poverty (already dropping rapidly from 22% in 1959), but to actively break down systemic inequality through federal programs. This commitment permanently linked the federal government to the pursuit of social justice as a central moral mission.

### Media — Television Saturation and the Death of Distance

The most important development was the **saturation of television ownership, reaching 90% of U.S. households by 1960**. This total technological takeover, combined with the rapid weakening of the Hays Production Code by 1967, meant that the political and social ruptures of the era — Civil Rights protests, Vietnam War images, counter-cultural music — were instantly broadcast as unified national spectacles.

Television served as the unavoidable channel for the new, skeptical reality. It destroyed the regional isolation of dissent and made the collapse of institutional legitimacy visible and immediate to nearly every American at the same time.

### Institutional — The Erosion of Trust

The most important institutional development was the **dramatic erosion of public trust in government, starting after 1964**. Having peaked at an all-time high of **77% in 1964**, trust began its decades-long free fall due to the escalating Vietnam War and subsequent civil unrest.

This collapse in legitimacy was the single most defining moral event of the era because it ended the post-war faith in centralized authority. When citizens stopped trusting the government's competence or moral honesty, the moral void was filled by individualized ethics and identity politics. This drove a deep cultural retreat from shared civic norms.

## Part 3 · Defining Characteristics

### The Technological Enfranchisement of Autonomy

The release of the birth control pill gave critical reproductive freedom. It made individual choice the new moral judge of sexual behavior.

### The Great Erosion of Institutional Trust

Public confidence in government began its sharp, long-term decline from a 77% peak, due to national crises of faith and integrity surrounding the Vietnam War.

### The Federal Mandate for Social Equality

Moral authority became permanently centered in Washington, D.C., as the federal government committed to legislating social justice and economic equality through Great Society programs.

### The Mass-Educated Moral Reckoning

A majority high school graduate population used newly quantified scientific frameworks (Kinsey's data) to challenge the moral and political legitimacy of Cold War conformity.

## Bridge to the Next Phase (1967–1973)

These tensions set up the 1967–1973 phase shifts: the rising crude divorce rate (from 2.6 in 1967 to 3.3 in 1969) sped up the collapse of family structure, even as rapidly declining government trust — the 1964 peak of 77% would fall by more than half within a decade — fueled political radicalism and counter-cultural challenges.

The unraveling did not break the rope. It found the seams. The next five years would pull them apart.
