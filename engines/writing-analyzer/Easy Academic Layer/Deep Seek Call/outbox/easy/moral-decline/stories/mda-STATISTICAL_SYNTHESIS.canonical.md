# The Arc of Decline: 125 Years in Charts

This article traces how American society changed through five generations of one family: Samuel (1900), Henry (1926), William (1950), Thomas (1974), Jacob (1998), and Jacob again (2025). The numbers tell a story that goes deeper than data alone, but the data makes the story impossible to ignore.

Each generation inherited a world shaped by the choices of those before them. Each generation faced limits their ancestors couldn't imagine losing, and freedoms their ancestors couldn't imagine wanting. The path is clear. Where it leads is not.

We organize the evidence into three areas: **Family** (the basic unit that passes values from one generation to the next), **Society** (the bigger patterns of trust, faith, and community), and **Economic** (the material conditions that help or hurt human flourishing).

## 1900: Samuel Lowe

### The Baseline - Before the Acceleration

In 1900, America was a nation of small circles. Seventy-six million people spread across 3.5 million square miles, but most would never travel more than fifty miles from where they were born. There was no radio, no telephone in rural Ohio, no moving pictures. The only voices Samuel heard were voices of people physically present. The only faces he saw were faces he could touch.

This isolation wasn't experienced as a lack of something. It was just the shape of human life before technology collapsed distance into nothing. The world was small, but it was whole. Complete in itself. There were no images of other lives to create dissatisfaction, no advertising to manufacture desire, no news cycle to generate fear.

The limits were absolute: you couldn't rush a harvest, hurry a horse, or speed a letter across the county. In this forced patience, people learned to sit with their thoughts, to wait, to endure. Marriage was for life because divorce meant social death. Faith was universal because the church was the only social infrastructure that existed.

- **Divorce Rate (per 1,000):** 0.7 (Baseline)
- **Marriage Age (M/F):** 26/22 (Baseline)
- **Cohabitation:** ~0% (Baseline)

### Society & Faith

- **Church Attendance:** ~95% (Near Universal)

### Technology & Connection

- **Households with Phone:** <1% (Baseline)
- **Households with Electricity:** <3% (Baseline)

## 1926: Henry Lowe

### The Builder - The First Intrusions

By 1926, America had grown to 117 million people. Cities had exploded: New York held six million, Chicago three. The Model T had put twenty million cars on American roads, turning what was once an expedition into a two-hour drive. Distance was starting to die.

But the deeper change was invisible: radio. The Lowe family bought a Radiola in 1924, and for the first time, outside voices entered the home. A man talking in New York. An orchestra playing in Chicago. The complete isolation of Samuel's world had been broken. Other voices were possible now. Other lives. Other ways of being.

The family still listened together, for an hour a night, gathered around the mahogany cabinet. It was still shared, still limited. But the door had opened. The silence that forced families together, that left no escape from the people you lived with or the thoughts in your own head, was starting to shrink.

### Family Structure

- **Divorce Rate (per 1,000):** 1.6 (+129% from 1900)
- **Marriage Age (M/F):** 24/21 (Stable)
- **Cohabitation:** ~0% (Still Rare)

### Society & Faith

- **Church Attendance (Rural):** ~90% (Still Universal)
- **Population:** 117M (+54% from 1900)

### Technology & Connection

- **Automobiles:** 20M (from 8,000)
- **Hours Media/Day:** ~1 (First Intrusion)

## 1950: William Lowe

### Peak Coherence - The Golden Moment

America in 1950 had never been richer, safer, or more confident. The war was over. The Depression was memory. Half the planet's manufacturing output came from American factories. The GI Bill had sent eight million veterans to college. The suburbs were blooming—instant communities on former farmland, each house with a yard, a garage, a television antenna reaching toward the sky.

This was the peak. Not of technology, not of wealth, but of coherence. Think of coherence like a tuning fork—when you strike it near other objects, they start vibrating at the same frequency. One source of perfect pitch that brings everything else into harmony. Church attendance hit 55% weekly—the highest in American history. The divorce rate, while higher than 1900, had stabilized. Families were marrying younger because they could afford to. The Baby Boom was underway: 3.7 children per woman by 1957. The middle class was expanding like never before.

Television had arrived—9% of households in 1950, 87% by 1960, the fastest adoption of any technology in history. But in 1950, it still felt manageable. The family gathered together to watch. It was shared. Limited. One screen, together. No one knew that this was the beginning of the end.

### Family Structure

- **Divorce Rate (per 1,000):** 2.6 (+271% from 1900)
- **Marriage Age (M/F):** 23/20 (Younger - Optimism)

### Society & Faith

- **Church Attendance:** 55% (PEAK - Historical High)
- **"No Religion":** 2% (Near Zero)
- **Median Income (2024$):** $42K (30% above 1940)

### Technology & Connection

## 1974: Thomas Lowe

### The Breaking - Everything Comes Apart

Everything Bill took for granted had come apart. It happened fast—so fast that people living through it couldn't quite see it. Like a building that looks solid until you notice the cracks, and then you can't see anything but cracks, and then one day it's rubble.

Vietnam was over—not with victory but with helicopters evacuating embassy roofs, with 58,000 American boys dead and nothing to show for it. Nixon resigned in August, the first president to do so. Watergate revealed what everyone suspected but no one wanted to know: the people in charge will lie to you. Trust in government collapsed from 77% to 36% in a decade.

The sexual revolution had arrived. Ten million women on the Pill. For the first time in human history, sex was separated from pregnancy. The biological limit that shaped every generation before—the simple, brutal fact that sex could mean a baby—was gone. Why marry if you don't have to? Why wait if there's no cost? California passed no-fault divorce in 1969. By 1974, most states had followed. You could now end a marriage because you wanted to.

Think of this like a shattered glass—you can't unshatter it, and you can't hide the pieces. Every shard is still there, accounted for. What broke stays broken until someone comes with glue from outside.

### Family Structure

- **Divorce Rate (per 1,000):** 4.6 (+557% from 1900)
- **Marriage Rate:** -49% from 1900, +117% from 1950

- **Violent Crime (per 100k):** 461 (+187% from 1960)
- **Murder Rate (per 100k):** 9.8 (+93% from 1960)

### Economic Reality

## 1998: Jacob Lowe

### The Aftermath - Rich, Comfortable, Untethered

America was rich, comfortable, and completely untethered. The Cold War was over. The Soviet Union had collapsed. The stock market was booming. Unemployment at 4.5%. The federal budget running a surplus. Bill Clinton was being impeached for lying about an affair, and it wouldn't meaningfully damage his approval ratings. The country had learned to separate public performance from private morality. Just another boundary erased.

The internet was emerging. AOL, Yahoo, early Amazon. By 1998, 26% of households had access. By 2024, the question would be meaningless—the internet as common as electricity. What it meant: information now infinite, any question answered instantly, any curiosity satisfied, any desire finding an outlet. And connection: you could talk to anyone, anywhere, at any time.

But infinite connection was not the same as depth. Infinite information was not wisdom. Think of information like a radio signal through static—the clearer the channel, the more of the message gets through. Sin is the static. Truth is the signal. The geographic limits that defined every previous generation were dissolving—but what would replace them? Jake didn't know where he was from. Not metaphorically—literally. His parents divorced when he was five. He shuttled between states, different schools, different neighborhoods. Home was wherever the screen was glowing.

- **Marriage Age (M/F):** 27/25 (Delayed/Skipped)
- **Cohabiting Couples:** 5.5M (10x from 1970)
- **Adults Living Alone:** 25M (from 4M in 1950)

### Society & Faith

- **"No Religion":** 14% (from 2% in 1950)
- **Violent Crime (per 100k):** 566 (+252% from 1960)
- **Median Income (2024$):** $70K (Prosperity)

### Economic Reality

## 2025: Jacob Lowe

### The Choice - Connected, Isolated, Searching

The loneliness epidemic is official. The Surgeon General declared it in 2023: loneliness is as deadly as smoking fifteen cigarettes a day. Social isolation increases premature death by 26%. Americans have fewer friends than ever. Fewer marriages. Fewer children. Fewer connections of any kind. The average American spends 7.5 hours daily looking at screens—more time with screens than with humans, more than with sleep.

And yet everyone is connected. Everyone is online. Everyone is reachable, all the time, everywhere. Connection without commitment. Presence without depth. The infinite scroll that never satisfies. AI writes your dating messages now. AI girlfriends are a billion-dollar industry. Dating apps have cratered—not because people found love, but because they gave up.

Birth rates have collapsed to 1.6 children per woman—the lowest in American history. South Korea is at 0.7. The future is not being born. Underneath it all, a quiet desperation: something is deeply wrong. This is not how life is supposed to feel. The promise of infinite connection has delivered infinite isolation.

Think of this like a flashlight in a dark room—it doesn't create what's there, it reveals what was always there. You can't have half-light. It either reaches the corner or it doesn't. The light of connection has revealed an empty room.

### Family Structure

- **Divorce Rate (per 1,000):** 2.3* (-54% from 1900 | HISTORIC LOW, but fewer marry)
- **Adults Living Alone:** 37M (from 4M in 1950)
- **Couples Met Online:** 65%+ (from 0% in 1990)
- **Marriage Rate:** -60% from 1900

### Society & Faith

- **Church Attendance:** 30% (-45% from Peak)
- **"No Religion":** 28% (from 2% in 1950)
- **Trust in Government:** <20% (-74% from Peak)

### Technology Saturation
