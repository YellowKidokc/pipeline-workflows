# The Bilateral Audit: Holding Both Science and Theology to the Same Standard

The John Templeton Foundation has spent fifty years asking how science and religion can talk to each other for real. The papers they've funded are good. But the conversation is shallow. The gap hasn't closed. **Here is why: every attempt starts from a broken foundation.**

## The 80/20 Split Nobody Voted For

Science owns eighty percent of the public space. Logic. Mathematics. Prediction. Technology. Medicine. Policy. The definition of what counts as knowledge. Christianity owns the other twenty percent. Sunday morning. Personal devotion. Private morality. The polite nod that faith matters to people who have it — as long as it stays in its own lane.

This arrangement looks earned. It looks like the result of four centuries of scientific progress beating religious dogma. It is not. It is a verdict handed down by one side after it quietly took the other side's foundation and never said thank you.

The 80% that science owns only works because the 20% is true. That is the structural contradiction nobody names.

Before science takes its first measurement, it must borrow ten things it cannot prove with its own method:
1. That the external world exists
2. That it is orderly and knowable
3. That logic works
4. That human thinking reliably tracks reality
5. That mathematics — an abstract invention — actually describes physical structure
6. That nature behaves consistently across time
7. That truth exists
8. That honesty matters
9. That simpler explanations are better
10. That the universe is understandable by minds at all

Ten assumptions. Zero of them can be proven by experiment. Every single one is required before the experiment can even be designed. These assumptions do not describe a neutral universe. They describe a universe that is rational, ordered, knowable, and accessible to minds. They describe a universe that theology has been describing for three thousand years. Science did not build that foundation. It borrowed it. Then it used the borrowed foundation to tell the lender it doesn't belong in the room.

That is not a fair settlement. That is a structural contradiction. And it is the reason fifty years of Templeton funding has produced conversation without resolution. You cannot have genuine conversation between two parties when one party is operating on premises it took from the other without acknowledgment.

## Hold Both Sides to the Same Standard

The standard move in science-religion conversation is to ask whether religious claims survive scientific testing. That is one direction. It is only half an audit. The full audit runs the other direction too.

Hold God accountable to science's own standards — falsifiability (can the claim be proven wrong?), predictive power (does it predict things we can check?), explanatory scope (how much does it explain?), internal consistency (does it contradict itself?), reproducibility (can we get the same result again?). Run the full pipeline. Do not protect theological claims from pressure.

Then hold science accountable to the same standards. Can it ground its own starting assumptions? Can it explain consciousness — not just map which brain areas light up, but explain why there is something it *feels like* to be a brain? Can it explain why abstract mathematics describes physical reality with precision that borders on the absurd? Can it account for the fine-tuning of cosmological constants (the exact settings of the universe's basic forces)? Can it explain where information comes from, not just how it is processed once it exists?
