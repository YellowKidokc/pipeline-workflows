## The Environment of 1400 BC: The Biological and Geopolitical Baseline

To understand the modern parallel, we need to establish what life was actually like in the ancient Near East using real archaeological and historical data. Evidence from bones and burial sites at places like Amarna (also called Akhetaten) shows a population under constant physical stress. Without germ theory (the understanding that tiny organisms cause disease) or antibiotics, death rates were so high that they shaped every religious and social decision. This was a world where surviving was a religious achievement, not something you could just expect.

### Biological and Demographic Constraints of the Bronze Age

In the 14th century BC, the idea of "clean" was spiritual, not biological. Ritual "uncleanness" worked as a kind of early quarantine, but it operated in a world where parasitic infections, malaria, and flesh-disfiguring diseases like leishmaniasis were everywhere. Studies of bones from Amarna's cemeteries show high rates of disease and unusual patterns — like multiple people buried in single graves — which suggests local epidemics or constant high-stress living conditions. The phrase "hand of Nergal" (a god of disease) appears in the Amarna Letters to describe sickness. This shows how people saw death as a divine or demonic attack, not a manageable biological event.

Infant death was the main driver of the Canaanite religious mindset. Historical estimates suggest that nearly fifty percent of all children did not survive to age fifteen. This biological reality created a "math of desperation" for ancient families. When survival isn't guaranteed by things like clean water and hospitals, you seek it through ritual. Burying dead infants in storage jars under the floors of family homes — a common practice in the region — shows a refusal to let the dead go outside into the chaos where jackals and decay ruled. The mother slept over her dead child every night, not out of a creepy impulse, but out of a desperate desire to protect the remains in a world where nothing outside the house was safe.
