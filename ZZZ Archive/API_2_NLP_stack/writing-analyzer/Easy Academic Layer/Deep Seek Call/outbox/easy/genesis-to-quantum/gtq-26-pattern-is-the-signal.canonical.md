## How Carrier's Argument Works

The Bayesian equation is simple: final odds equal starting odds times the likelihood ratio. You start with how likely something is before you look at the evidence, then you update based on what the evidence says.

Carrier's starting probability — his starting point before examining any evidence — comes from asking: in the set of all ancient figures most similar to Jesus, how often do they turn out to be historical rather than mythical? He puts Jesus in the "heavily mythologized savior heroes" class: Osiris, Hercules, Romulus, Moses, Aesop. Figures who were worshiped, whose stories were filled with supernatural events, and who appeared primarily in sacred literature rather than mundane political records.

In that class, Carrier argues, at most 1 in 3 turned out to be historical. So he starts with a 33% probability — 1 to 2 odds that Jesus existed.

He then examines four categories of evidence: non-Bible sources, the book of Acts, the Gospels, and the Epistles (letters). He finds that most of the evidence is equally likely whether Jesus existed or not (likelihood ratio of 1/1 — no effect). Some evidence slightly favors mythicism. Three unclear passages in Paul slightly favor historicity. The math works out to roughly 32% at the upper bound.

The argument is clean. The math is correct given his inputs. The problem is upstream of the math.

## The Reference Class Problem

Carrier's entire starting assumption — and therefore his entire conclusion — depends on one assumption: that the structural similarities between Jesus and other savior-hero figures (incarnation, death, resurrection, salvation, worshiped by followers) are evidence of *fabrication*. This assumption does real work in his argument. It's the reason Jesus lands in the low-frequency reference class instead of a higher one. It's the reason the starting odds are 33% instead of, say, 75%. Everything downstream of that assumption inherits it.

But what if the assumption is wrong? What if the reason the death-resurrection-salvation pattern appears across multiple cultures isn't that humans keep inventing it — but that the pattern is *real*?

If we grant that the pattern is real — that reality actually contains a coherence-restoration mechanism — then we need different criteria for identifying the source instance. **A movement that survived and exploded despite zero political or military power.** This is the marker Carrier underweights most severely. Romulus was the founding myth of Rome's ruling class. Hercules was woven into the imperial propaganda machine. The tomb was in Jerusalem. The crucifixion was public. The claimed witnesses were named and alive (1 Corinthians 15:6 — "most of whom are still alive"). Each of these markers, individually, shifts the likelihood ratio in favor of historicity. Collectively, they are devastating to Carrier's conclusion — because his math depends on the evidence being weak. *What we got right, what we're less sure about, and where we got carried away.*

### What's Load-Bearing — We'd Bet on This

**The reference class critique is structurally sound.** Carrier's entire starting assumption depends on treating pattern convergence as evidence of fabrication. **The math is correct.** Correcting only the starting odds from 1/2 to 2/1 and keeping everything else Carrier argues produces a final probability near 49%. This is arithmetic, not interpretation.

### What's Suggestive but Needs More Work

**The Projection Principle itself is the article's load-bearing assumption.** If it falls, the reference class correction falls with it. The 10/10 mapping is strong but has not been independently verified outside the framework. A skeptic will note that the Projection Principle is the framework's own claim, and using it to correct Carrier's math is somewhat circular — we're using our conclusion to fix someone else's premise. The response is that the Projection Principle makes independent predictions (like the PEAR-LAB result being expected) that are confirmed by external data. But the circularity concern is legitimate and should be stated.

**The "movement explosion" marker is historically strong but causally ambiguous.** Christianity's rapid growth from zero power base is genuinely unusual, but Carrier has responses: the movement may have succeeded for sociological reasons unrelated to whether its founder existed. The marker is evidence, not proof.

**The corrected starting odds of 2/1 is a judgment call.** A more conservative correction might land at 1/1 (50/50 starting odds) instead of 2/1. Even at 1/1, the final probability still improves significantly over Carrier's result.

### Where We Got Carried Away

**The 97.4% figure is aspirational, not demonstrated.** It depends on all three corrections being accepted simultaneously, each at the values we chose. A more honest range would be: correcting only the starting odds gives ~49%; correcting the starting odds and the non-Bible evidence gives ~66%; accepting all corrections gives ~97%. The range of 49–97% is more defensible than the single number.

**Using PEAR-LAB to argue for the resurrection mechanism is a reach at this stage.** The PEAR-LAB data confirms consciousness-matter coupling exists. It does not confirm that a specific individual achieved maximum coupling and reversed entropy at a specific historical moment. The gap between "consciousness affects random event generators at small effect sizes" and "a man rose from the dead" is large. The framework provides the theoretical bridge, but the experimental confirmation is at a much lower magnitude than the event it's being used to support.
