# The Technology of Human Will

## I. The Technology of Human Will

### A Conceptual and Critical Definition

To understand where American society is heading morally, we need a clear definition of technology. Technology is not just hardware (like phones) or software (like apps). It is a cultural force — an **exteriorization of human will**. That means technology is what happens when human intentions get built into objects and systems. It is how society organizes itself and holds together. So the way technology develops tells us what people value. Right now, the values being built in are: maximize, control, extract.

### A. Technology as Exteriorized Intent and Cultural Force

Technology shapes culture. That puts it in the territory of philosophy — the study of how things relate to each other. When we look at big, complex technological systems, we have to ask about their moral footprint. We stop asking "does this tool work well?" and start asking "what does this system do to people?"

Technology is not neutral. It carries values. Technical systems are built to be efficient and powerful. When humans interact with the world through technology, they are adopting a maximizing, controlling approach. When this approach is everywhere, it creates a "technological way of life." The decline we see in social connections and mental health may be the direct result of technology driven by these unchecked values.

### B. The Moral Status of the Artifact: Critical Theory and Controllability

A Critical Theory of technology gives us a way to analyze this decay. This view says two things at once: technology is controllable (like instrumentalism says), AND technology is value-laden (like substantivism says). This sounds contradictory, but it means: yes, technology embeds values like efficiency, but we can tame it by making the design process more democratic.

The key idea is this: the problem is not technology itself. The problem is that we have failed to build institutions that let humans control technological development. Think of the economy. People once thought the economy was an unstoppable force with its own laws. Then society said no, and created democratic institutions to influence the economy. We need to do the same thing with technology. If technology embodies the will to maximize and control, and we don't balance that with democratic structures, then the exteriorization of human will produces decay — broken trust, broken relationships, and broken minds.

### C. The Architectural Vectors of Technological Emergence (1950–2025)

We can divide the last 75 years into three phases.

#### Phase 1: Mass Mediation (1950–1985)

This was defined by one-way, passive, shared consumption. Think broadcast television, radio, landline phones. TV reached near-saturation. It reshaped how attention was managed and how families spent time at home. This period set up centralized cultural stories and demanded high passive attention. It laid the groundwork for everything that followed.

#### Phase 2: Individualized Access (1985–2005)

This was the shift to personal computing and active information retrieval. The Personal Computer (PC) and dial-up/early broadband internet defined this era. By early 2000, about half of U.S. adults were online. This decentralized information — anyone could find things — but it demanded new skills in filtering and digital literacy. Cell phone ownership also surged: two-thirds of Americans had one by 2005.

#### Phase 3: Perpetual Connectivity (2005–Present)

This is defined by mobile devices, high-speed internet everywhere, and social platforms woven into daily life. Today, **96% of U.S. adults use the internet**. That is near-complete saturation. The critical development was the explosion of social media and smartphones after 2005. Facebook grew 1,300% between 2005 and 2010. This technology shifts us to continuous, interactive, algorithm-driven engagement. **95% of youth ages 13–17** use social media, many "almost constantly." This high psychological saturation demands constant peer connection and algorithmic attention capture. It is fundamentally different from the earlier phases.

## II. The Aesthetic Transition

### Analog Durability to Digital Entropy

The **Aesthetic of Technological Entropy** is a way to see how the design philosophy of modern technology mirrors — and maybe causes — the social and psychological decay we measure. This aesthetic shift represents a fundamental change in what we expect to last.

### A. The Patina of Permanence (Analog Durability)

The earlier period of manufacturing (roughly Phase 1 and before) embraced analog durability. This design philosophy valued high-quality materials, products built to be repaired, and longevity. The physical object was meant to absorb time, gaining value through use — the "patina" of age. This encouraged long-term investment, repair, and passing things down through generations. Consumers developed "aesthetic durability" by choosing timeless designs and rejecting disposable trends. This embedded cultural value in physical goods. It naturally reinforced social stability, supporting behaviors like thrift and repair that are essential for strong relationships and trust.

### B. The Ascent of Planned and Perceived Obsolescence

The critical shift is the acceptance of an **expendable aesthetic** and **planned obsolescence**. This means products are designed to have a short life. They are "short-life toys" or replaceable parts in temporary frames. This idea was recognized early in design, especially in Metabolist architecture, which focused on the "joints" where fast and slow time meet. While some activists since the 1960s championed reuse as an ecological strategy, consumer technology moved toward disposability.

### C. Entropy as a Digital Design Principle

Thermodynamic entropy means systemic disorder and decay. We can apply this to technology. In modern consumer electronics, entropy is not a failure — it is an engineered feature. This is the **commodification of systemic decay**. Digital entropy shows up as closed hardware ecosystems, non-replaceable batteries, and — crucially — software incompatibility and unpatchable security flaws. A phone that works perfectly is rendered useless by digital decay. This ensures you keep buying new ones.

The structural implication is profound. If technology is the exteriorization of human intent, then the shift toward designed decay means our society and corporations prioritize maximum extraction (profit and power) over permanence and stability. This technological entropy creates a negative feedback loop: it undermines the value of durability, making transience and breakdown seem normal. This engineered instability models the moral decline we measure: decay in relationships (unstable social bonds), decay in information (rapid turnover of facts, misinformation), and the collapse into psychological entropy (internal systemic breakdown).

## III. Longitudinal Data Synthesis

### Technology and Decline Vectors (1950–2025)

Mapping the three categories of moral decline against the three technological phases shows that technology's impact was **differential and sequential** — not uniform.

### A. Relational Coherence: Fragmentation of Social Bonds

Relational Coherence measures the strength of community and family structures. The data shows that the foundational decay in this category happened before individualized and perpetual digital access.

#### Decline in Civic Participation

Citizens withdrew from voluntary associations and social connections **gradually and steadily after the 1950s and 1960s**. This affected many traditional organizations, including unions and the PTA. Social capital theory says this decline is mainly due to **generational replacement** (about half the decline) and the rise of **television-watching** (about 25% of the decline).

The critical finding: the most significant decay in Relational Coherence was correlated with Phase 1 (Mass Mediation). The shift from active group participation to passive, home-bound consumption fundamentally weakened the civic fabric. Further complicating this is the long-term trend toward **structural isolation**: the proportion of U.S. households with only one person rose from 7.7% in 1940 to 27.6% in 2020. This accelerating trend toward living alone spans all three phases.

#### Marital Coherence and Isolation

The U.S. divorce rate peaked around 1980 and has generally declined since. This peak happened during the transition from Phase 1 to Phase 2. The fact that marital instability peaked before the mass proliferation of PCs, the internet, and smartphones suggests that changes in family structure were driven by broader social and cultural forces — not the digital revolution.

### B. Informational Integrity: Erosion of Institutional Trust

Informational Integrity means public confidence in institutions that govern and provide information. The data shows a crisis that started long before the digital age. The Perpetual Connectivity era then fragmented what was already broken.

#### Trust in Government and Media

Public trust in the federal government has suffered a profound, decades-long collapse. In 1958, about three-quarters of Americans trusted the government to act rightly. But trust began eroding during the 1960s with the Vietnam War, and continued falling through the 1970s with Watergate and economic struggles. Since 2007, confidence in government has not risen above 30%. A similar trend is seen in media: confidence in news reporting fell from 68–72% in the 1970s to 53% by 1997.

#### Disillusionment Versus Fragmentation

The data shows that the crisis of Informational Integrity was initiated and cemented during Phase 1 (Mass Mediation) and the early part of Phase 2. This was driven by centralized communication of political failure and war. Phase 1 generated **disillusionment** — loss of faith in centralized authorities — decades before social media existed. Phase 3 (Perpetual Connectivity) then accelerated **fragmentation**, providing an unprecedented platform for decentralized, often dubious information. This thrives in an environment where foundational trust in centralized sources has already been destroyed.

### C. Psychological Entropy: Internal Systemic Breakdown

Psychological Entropy means internal systemic breakdown — acute mental distress. This shows a strong, time-specific correlation with the saturation of Perpetual Connectivity technology (Phase 3).

**16.0%** of adolescents ages 12–17 have diagnosed anxiety.
**3.5 hours** is the average daily social media use for teens.
**54%** of adults report feeling isolated often or some of the time.

#### The Acute Rise of Mental Distress

The most alarming trend is the surge in mental health issues, especially among youth. Depression prevalence increased significantly from **8.2% in 2013–2014 to 13.1% in 2021–2023**. Current data shows 16.0% of adolescents have diagnosed anxiety, and 8.7% have diagnosed depression. The U.S. Surgeon General has identified loneliness as a public health epidemic. A majority of adults report feeling isolated (54%) or lacking companionship (50%) often or some of the time.

#### Technological Causal Mechanisms

While suicide rates fluctuated over the second half of the 20th century, the contemporary crisis is marked by the temporal alignment of the mental distress spike with the saturation point of social media adoption. Experimental studies establish a **causal link** between limited use of platforms like Facebook, Snapchat, and Instagram and improved well-being.

The evidence points to a biological mechanism. Excessive and problematic social media use — defined by adolescents spending an average of 3.5 hours per day on these platforms — is consistently linked to poor sleep quality, reduced sleep duration, and depression. Furthermore, frequent social media use may be associated with distinct changes in the developing brain's amygdala (emotional learning) and prefrontal cortex (impulse control and emotional regulation). These changes increase sensitivity to social rewards and punishments. This essentially engineers a perpetually unstable internal state, susceptible to stress and feelings of exclusion. The technology consumes the cognitive resources needed for stability, creating systemic internal disorder.

## IV. Correlation Analysis

### Establishing Linkages Between Entropy and Decline

The analysis confirms that technological entropy is significantly correlated with moral decline. But the nature of that correlation depends heavily on the phase of technological adoption.

### Phase 1: Mass Mediation and the Foundation of Decay

The strong statistical correlation between the rise of passive broadcast media (Phase 1) and the start of large-scale social decay shows that the systemic vulnerabilities in American moral capital were laid decades ago. The mechanism is substitution: television functionally replaced active group participation with isolated, domestic leisure. This led to the gradual "unraveling" of social capital. At the same time, the broadcast nature of this medium centralized the dissemination of political failures (Vietnam, Watergate), generating pervasive and lasting disillusionment that destroyed Informational Integrity. This period initiated the cultural acceptance of technologically mediated, isolated consumption. It structurally weakened society before the internet arrived.

### Phase 2: Individualized Access and Structural Dispersion

Phase 2 (PC and early internet access) appears to function less as a primary cause of moral decline and more as a structural accelerant of existing trends. It provided tools for individualized retreat, contributing to the accelerating trend toward one-person households. While it introduced information dispersion, the mode of interaction remained active (retrieval-based). This kept the pressures of continuous social comparison and engineered addiction (the hallmarks of Psychological Entropy) relatively low. The decline in Informational Integrity continued, but the core crisis of trust was already cemented in Phase 1.

### Phase 3: Perpetual Connectivity and Acute Psychological Entropy

Phase 3 exhibits the highest and most acute correlation with decline, specifically regarding internal psychological stability. The mass adoption of smartphones and social media (post-2013) corresponds precisely with the sharp, measurable spike in youth anxiety and depression. The causal evidence confirms that the technology is not merely present alongside the decay — it actively diminishes well-being when used excessively.

The crucial dynamic identified is the **entropic feedback loop**: the aesthetic of digital entropy (designed instability and transience) feeds directly into psychological entropy. The algorithmic objective of maximizing attention capture and engagement leads to a technological environment that interferes with the biological mechanisms of emotional regulation and sleep. Users are driven toward perpetual cognitive and emotional instability (FOMO, comparison stress), mirroring the disposable and unmaintainable nature of the hardware and software itself. The efficiency goal of the technical system — maximum extraction of attention and data — directly causes the internal systemic breakdown that is Psychological Entropy.

## V. Falsification and Alternative Causal Pathways

To avoid the oversimplified conclusion that technology alone causes everything, we must test for other factors that may have accelerated or started the decline independently of technology. The evidence suggests that technology often reflects or accelerates existing societal weaknesses.

### A. The Non-Deterministic Counter-Thesis

Many critical analyses emphasize that technology primarily accelerates or provides a platform for pre-existing societal tensions and trends. For example, technology enables the gig economy, but it is pre-existing economic insecurity that makes workers reliant on insecure, technology-mediated labor. The societal harms observed are not necessarily the inevitable outcome of technological progress. They are the consequence of society failing to implement adequate civic or institutional structures to manage the pace of change.

### B. Socioeconomic and Political Drivers of Decline

Non-technological factors provide powerful alternative explanations for the erosion of moral capital.

#### Economic Inequality

Rising economic inequality has been shown to be a critical driver of social breakdown. Inequality fosters a competitive economic climate that directly erodes social cohesion. It causes fractured social environments and correlates strongly with increased moralization — the rise in using harsh moral language and judgments against others. If economic pressure structurally weakens social bonds, individuals may retreat into digital spheres for validation and connection. This makes them highly susceptible to the psychological harms inherent in constant social comparison and technologically engineered stress. Economic strain acts as a major confounding variable that both initiates and magnifies technological harm.

#### Political Polarization and Conflict

The widespread feeling of moral free fall is a point of agreement across political affiliations, despite deep polarization. Societal division is frequently cited as a significant source of stress among adults. These political and ideological conflicts, often rooted in historical, economic, and cultural divisions, substantially predate the mass adoption of Phase 3 technology. Technology, in this view, did not create the division. It provided a highly efficient, fragmented, and emotionally charged platform for existing moral conflict to be amplified and weaponized, accelerating the decay of Informational Integrity.

### C. Generational Replacement and Cultural Momentum

Robert Putnam's definitive analysis of civic decline places overwhelming emphasis on **generational replacement**. The replacement of the "long civic generation" (formed by shared experiences of the Great Depression and World War II) by generations with less inherent proclivity for group involvement accounts for roughly half of the decline in Relational Coherence. This perspective reinforces the finding that the decay of civic bonds was fundamentally a cultural shift — one facilitated by Phase 1 technology (TV) but driven primarily by demographic momentum. This confirms that the decline in Relational Coherence had deep, non-digital origins.

## VI. Conclusion and Policy Recommendations

The investigation into the Aesthetic of Technological Entropy confirms a profound, time-specific correlation between technological evolution and the degradation of American moral capital from 1950 to the present. The analysis rejects a simple, single-cause narrative. It establishes that the impact of technology has been **differential and sequential**.

The **Mass Mediation era (Phase 1)** initiated the fundamental structural decay. It established an isolating media consumption model that severely undermined **Relational Coherence** (civic bonds) and generated the systemic political and media failures that destroyed **Informational Integrity** (institutional trust). The **Perpetual Connectivity era (Phase 3)** subsequently exploited these pre-existing social and informational vacuums. It introduced an acute, potentially causal crisis of **Psychological Entropy**, driven by the biologically destabilizing effects of attention-capture design.

The core finding: the technological imperative of efficiency and maximization, when exteriorized without institutional control, creates an aesthetic of entropic transience (planned obsolescence) that structurally models and enables moral decay — culminating in the engineered instability of the human psyche.

### A. Recommendations: Towards Technological Democracy

#### 1. Democratizing Technology Design and Deployment

Technological systems must be submitted to a more democratic process to ensure that their embedded values extend beyond maximization and control. This requires mandating design transparency, allowing public scrutiny of algorithmic objectives, and holding technological corporations accountable for the measurable negative externalities (Psychological Entropy) produced by their systems. The objective is to establish institutions capable of directing technological development toward social stability, mirroring the historical democratization of economic control.

#### 2. Countering Digital Entropy and Planned Obsolescence

Policy intervention must prioritize permanence over engineered transience. Implementing stringent legal frameworks that enforce a "Right-to-Repair" for both hardware and software would directly resist the aesthetic of entropy. By encouraging durability and discouraging planned obsolescence, governance can reinforce cultural values of investment, repair, and long-term stability that underpin Relational Coherence.

#### 3. Mitigating the Crisis of Psychological Entropy

Given the acute, demonstrable correlation between excessive Phase 3 technology use and youth mental distress, immediate and targeted public health interventions are justified. These measures should include mandating default settings that impose age-appropriate usage limits, restrict features that interfere with sleep, and prohibit designs engineered to overstimulate the developing brain's reward centers. Addressing the public health epidemic of loneliness and disconnection requires not only limiting harmful exposure but also prioritizing and resourcing community-based interventions that emphasize non-mediated social interaction to rebuild the Relational Coherence eroded by preceding technological phases.
