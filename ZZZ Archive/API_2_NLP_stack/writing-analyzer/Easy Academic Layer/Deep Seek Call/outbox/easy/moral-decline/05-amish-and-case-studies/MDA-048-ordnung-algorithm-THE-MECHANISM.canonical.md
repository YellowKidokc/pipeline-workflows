# The Ordnung Algorithm: How the Amish Code of Conduct Works as a System-Saving Machine

## Executive Summary

This report takes a deep look at the Old Order Amish *Ordnung* — their unwritten set of rules. It shows that this code is not just about religion. It is a smart **System-Preservation Algorithm** (a set of rules that keeps a system alive and healthy). We use ideas from systems theory (how complex systems work), cybernetics (how systems control themselves), and information entropy (how information tends to get messy and lose meaning) to study Amish technology and society.

We find one main rule driving everything: the **Technological Axiom of Distance** (the idea that technology should make things harder to reach, not easier).

We look at real research data — from how air-powered tools work, to the physics of 12-volt batteries, to how Amish help each other, to the geography of the horse-drawn buggy. We strip away the religious words people usually use to describe Amish life — words like *Demut* (humility) or *Gelassenheit* (yieldedness) — and replace them with functional terms (terms that describe what things actually *do*). When we do this, we see the Amish community as a **"Coherence Factory"** — a system designed to build and keep high levels of social closeness by systematically filtering out "Information Entropy" (the noise and chaos that breaks communities apart).

The report proves there are seven **Structural Rules** (basic rules that shape the whole system) that come from the main Axiom:

**RULE 1 — The Proximity Limit**
A space rule that forces people to stay close, creating a strong local network.

**RULE 2 — The Friction Requirement**
A heat-and-energy rule that stops technology from becoming invisible and automatic.

**RULE 3 — The Anti-Bypass Rule**
A connection rule that forbids direct links to outside critical systems (like the power grid).

**RULE 4 — The Sanctuary Boundary**
A space rule that protects the home from the attention economy (the business of grabbing your attention).

**RULE 5 — The Mutual Aid Imperative**
An economic rule that rejects insurance in favor of depending on each other.

**RULE 6 — The Finite Input Rule**
A resource rule that prefers limited flows over unlimited ones.

**RULE 7 — The Ownership Distinction**
A boundary rule that separates using a tool from owning it.

By carefully studying these rules, this report argues that the Amish have successfully built a sociological **"membrane"** — a selective filter that lets matter and energy pass in and out of their community while blocking the "structural effects" (the hidden changes in how people think and act) that would dissolve their internal togetherness.

## Chapter 1 · Entropy, Coherence & The Technological Axiom

To understand the Amish *Ordnung* as a survival algorithm, we first need to understand the environment it fights against. The modern world, which sociologists call "time-space compression" (the feeling that time and space are shrinking because of technology), is a high-entropy environment. In systems theory, entropy is the tendency of a closed system to move toward disorder, sameness, and loss of structure. In a social setting, "Information Entropy" shows up as local culture dissolving into global "noise," social bonds breaking because too many things compete for your attention, and unique community identity fading away.

The outside world — what the Amish call "English" society — is driven by a need to go faster and faster. Information flows without limits. Connections are everywhere but weak. The line between "you" and the global "network" gets blurry. This state is defined by "velocity" (speed), a critical value in social mechanics. When the speed of information and physical movement passes a certain point, the "coherence" (togetherness) of the local social group falls apart. The individual becomes isolated — connected to everyone in theory but dependent on no one in practice.

For a high-coherence system like the Amish community to survive inside this high-entropy/high-velocity environment, it must keep a rigid **membrane** — a selective filter that controls the flow of energy, information, and influence. This report identifies the *Ordnung* as the software that runs this membrane.

### 1.1 The Technological Axiom of Distance

The core logic driving this membrane is the **Technological Axiom of Distance**. This axiom says that the health of a social system goes down as technology makes it easier for members to skip local interaction. Standard Western modernity designs technology to *eliminate* distance and friction (resistance or difficulty). The goal is to make the far near and the hard easy, effectively shrinking the world to the size of a screen.

The Amish algorithm flips this. It artificially *imposes* distance and friction to make sure interactions stay high-cost and therefore high-value. This is not Luddism (being against machines just because they're new). It is a calculated **Coherence Metric** (a way to measure togetherness). The Amish understand without being told that "frictionless" (effortless) interaction leads to "frictionless" abandonment. By keeping the cost of interaction high, the system makes sure social bonds are not treated as throwaway items.

Research into the "Substitution Principle" in economic geography gives us a key counter-model (a model that works the opposite way). In the secular (non-religious) economy, the principle says that companies replace labor (people) with capital (technology) to maximize efficiency. If a machine is cheaper than a person, the machine replaces the person. The Amish algorithm sees this principle as an existential threat (a threat to their very existence). If a technology substitutes a machine for a human relationship — insurance replacing mutual aid, a car replacing a neighbor who gives you a ride — it is rejected.

### 1.2 Coherence Metric: Inward vs. Outward Vectors

The algorithm optimizes for one specific variable: the **Coherence Metric**. This metric can be defined by the ratio of "Inward Attention Vectors" to "Outward Attention Vectors."

**Outward Vectors:** Forces that pull attention, loyalty, and dependence toward the outside world — the government, the global market, the media. Examples: public school, the electric grid, television, the internet.

**Inward Vectors:** Forces that direct attention toward the local group — family, church district, immediate area. Examples: horse-drawn buggy, Pennsylvania Dutch language, barn raising, local school.

The *Ordnung* works as a filter for Information Entropy by maximizing Inward Vectors and minimizing Outward Vectors. It is a system of "Attention Economics" (the study of how attention is a scarce resource) that was put in place long before Silicon Valley made the term popular. Every technology they reject — from the car to the smartphone — can be traced back to its potential to create an Outward Vector.

### 1.3 Velocity and the Governance of Time

A critical part of this framework is the regulation of **Time**. The research distinguishes between "Industrial Time" (linear, broken into segments, focused on efficiency) and "Amish Time" (rhythmic, based on farm work, focused on tasks). Industrial Time is high-velocity; it demands you sync up with the global clock. Amish Time is "slow" and "melismatic" (stretching out syllables), a quality you can even hear in their singing, where they stretch syllables over long periods.

This "slowness" is not a failure to modernize. It is a security feature. High-velocity systems are prone to rapid contagion (fast spread) — financial panics, viral trends, social crazes spread instantly. By imposing a "speed limit" on their entire civilization (through the Proximity Limit and rejecting the car), the Amish create a "latency" (a delay) that lets the community process change without being overwhelmed by it. They exist in a state of **Tempo-stasis** (time stability), resisting the acceleration that drives the social entropy of the outside world.

## Chapter 2 · Rule 1 — The Proximity Limit

The first and most visible structural rule proven by the research is the **Proximity Limit**. This rule controls the physical size of the community and acts as a hard limit on the network layout of the Amish church district.

### 2.1 Geometry of the Horse as a Governor

The main thing that enforces the Proximity Limit is the horse and buggy. The horse works structurally as a **governor of velocity and range** (a speed and distance limiter). Research shows that the average Amish-owned horse and buggy travels within a radius of about **20 miles**. This is not just a travel limit. It is a [boundary condition](/glossary/?term=boundary-condition "See glossary: boundary condition") (a rule that defines the edges of the system) for the community's social reality.

The horse imposes a biological limit on travel. It needs rest, water, and care. It travels at about 10 miles per hour. A member cannot simply "commute" 50 miles to a job, work an 8-hour shift, and return. The horse forces the economy to stay local. Amish labor crews and businesses operate within a strict 10-mile radius. This creates a **spatial fidelity** (a match between the map and real life) where the map of the community matches the territory of daily interaction.

### 2.2 Preserving Dunbar's Number Through Geography

Evolutionary psychology (the study of how evolution shaped our minds) suggests humans can maintain stable social relationships with about 150 people — this is called Dunbar's Number. Modern transportation lets individuals keep loose, weak connections spread over huge distances. This creates a "diffuse" (spread-out) social graph where the individual is connected to many but committed to few.

The Proximity Limit enforces a social density that matches Dunbar's Number. Because traveling beyond the 20-mile limit requires significant friction (hiring an "Amish Taxi"), casual interaction with the outside world is discouraged. The church district — the basic unit of Amish society — is geographically defined by the number of families that can travel to a single farmhouse for Sunday service using a horse. When the district grows too large for this geometry, it splits. This is biological reproduction (mitosis — cell division), not imperial expansion (taking over new territory).

### 2.3 The Structural Effect of "Slow"

The Proximity Limit imposes a low-velocity state. Amish singing is "slow and melismatic," often stretching a single song over twenty minutes. This slowness is a **cadence of cohesion** (a rhythm of togetherness). It forces participants to sync their breathing and attention for long periods, creating a deep harmony that is impossible in the soundbite culture of the English world.

By rejecting the car as a possession, the Amish reject the freedom of high-velocity travel. A car owner is a sovereign individual (a person with total independence) who can leave whenever they want. A buggy driver is a tethered member (someone tied to the group) of a local node. Geography must remain a binding constraint. The Amish do not "overcome" distance. They inhabit it (they live inside it, not fight it).

## Chapter 3 · Rule 2 — The Friction Requirement

The second structural rule says that labor and process must keep a certain level of physical difficulty or "friction" to make sure the human element is not made useless by the machine. This is most clearly shown in the Amish adoption of pneumatic (air-powered) tools over grid-electric tools — a phenomenon called **"Amish Electricity."**

### 3.1 The Pneumatic Compromise

To an outsider, an Amish workshop filled with the "ear-cracking racket" of power sanders and saws seems contradictory. If they reject electricity, why use tools that do the same thing?

The answer lies in the **source** and the **friction** of the energy.

**Grid Electricity:** Frictionless. Silent, invisible, infinite. Arrives instantly from a distant, unknown source. Connects the user to global infrastructure. Requires no effort to start.

**Pneumatic Power:** High-friction. Requires a large diesel engine to drive an air compressor. Loud, vibrates, requires physical fuel handling. Finite — limited by tank and generator capacity.

The diesel generator acts as a **local power plant**. The Amish shop owner controls the means of energy production but carries the burden of its maintenance, noise, and fueling. This prevents the seamless integration of technology into the background of life. You cannot ignore a diesel engine. You can easily ignore a wall socket.

### 3.2 Retrofitting as Hacker Culture

A whole cottage industry (a small, specialized business) exists for retrofitting (modifying) electrical tools to run on pneumatic power. Amish hackers buy heavy-duty appliances (blenders, saws, washing machines), strip out electric motors, and install air vanes. This serves a vital structural function: **The Modification Phase**.

Technology is not adopted out of the box. It must be taken apart, understood, and modified to fit community constraints. This strips technology of its "black box" nature (the idea that you don't know how it works inside). The Amish user is not a passive consumer of a magical device. They are an active master of a mechanical tool. The friction of retrofitting makes sure only tools that are truly necessary get adopted. A frivolous gadget (a silly, unnecessary device) is unlikely to go through the complex and expensive process of pneumatic conversion.

### 3.3 The Friction Tax on Consumption

The rejection of the electric grid is often framed as a rejection of "worldliness." Structurally, it is a rejection of **technological ease** (how easy technology makes things). Grid electricity makes power too easy. It allows for the spread of devices (TVs, radios, computers) that would otherwise require deliberate effort to power.

By mandating that power be generated pneumatically or hydraulically, the *Ordnung* imposes a Friction Tax on energy use. You will not run a television if you have to start a diesel generator to watch it. The Friction Requirement filters out low-value, high-entropy technological noise. Energy is spent only on productive labor — woodworking, farming — rather than consumptive leisure (wasting time on entertainment).

## Chapter 4 · Rule 3 — The Anti-Bypass Rule

The Anti-Bypass Rule deals with the *topology* (layout) of connection. No technology may be adopted if it lets a member bypass the community's regulatory structures or physical boundaries. This is the core logic behind rejecting the public utility grid (electricity) and the public communication grid (internet/phone lines in the home).

### 4.1 The Grid as a Vector of Influence

The electric grid is a physical tether to the outside world. It is an umbilical cord (a lifeline) that feeds the home with energy, media, and influence from a centralized, non-Amish authority. If the grid enters the home, the Sanctuary Boundary is breached.

**VOLTAGE FIREWALL**

110V AC vs. 12V DC
Standard of the outside world vs. Standard of the island

**110-volt AC (Grid Standard):** The currency of the English consumer economy. Powers high-wattage appliances — microwaves, hair dryers, televisions, computers.

**12-volt DC (Battery Standard):** The voltage of the island. Powers lights, basic fans, specific tools. Cannot easily power the distractions of modern life without inefficient inverters (devices that change DC to AC).

### 4.2 Inefficiency as a Feature

Converting 12V battery power to 110V AC is inefficient. It wastes energy and drains batteries quickly. In the secular world, this is a problem to be solved. In the Amish Coherence Factory, this inefficiency is a **feature** (a useful quality).

By standardizing on 12V DC, the Amish create a **technical incompatibility** (a built-in mismatch) with the mass consumer market. An Amish family cannot simply buy a standard toaster or TV at Walmart and plug it in. They are structurally blocked from plug-and-play consumer culture. To use a 110V appliance, they would need an inverter, which drains the finite battery, triggering the Finite Input Rule.

### 4.3 The Battery as Finite Container

The grid offers an illusion of infinity — the lights never go out. A battery is finite. It runs out. It must be recharged. This finiteness forces the user to be conscious of consumption. It prevents the "backgrounding" of technology (letting it fade into the background unnoticed). No input — energy or information — can flow into the Amish home without a conscious, deliberate act of acquisition. The home remains an autonomous node (a self-governing unit), dependent on the community for support but independent of the state for sustenance.

## Chapter 5 · Rule 4 — The Sanctuary Boundary

The Sanctuary Boundary is the spatial application of the *Ordnung* to the domestic sphere (the home). It designates the Home as a **zero-entropy zone** (a place with no disorder), protected from the intrusive vectors of the attention economy. The main artifact (object) that proves this rule is the **Telephone Shanty**.

### 5.1 The Logic of the Telephone Shanty

The phone itself is not evil. The *location* matters.

**Phone in Home:** Breaks the sanctuary. Lets the outside world interrupt the family dinner, the silence of the evening, or the flow of domestic life. Privileges the distant voice over the present face. Takes "visiting" out of its proper context.

**Phone in Shanty:** Preserves the sanctuary. The phone is a tool, not a member of the household. Located at a distance — often at the end of a lane or shared between families.

### 5.2 Distance as Filter for Triviality

The requirement to walk to the shanty imposes a transaction cost (a cost of doing business) on communication. As one Amish member noted, *"If you walk a quarter mile, you don't use it as much."* The distance filters out trivial (unimportant) communication. You walk to the shanty for business or an emergency (high signal), not for idle gossip (high noise).

The shanty physically separates the informational space from the relational space. The Home remains a space for face-to-face interaction (high coherence), while the Shanty contains digital/electronic interaction (potential entropy). This spatial segregation prevents the "colonization" (takeover) of the home by external information flows.

### 5.3 The Threat of Decontextualized Visiting

In Amish culture, "visiting" is described as **the national sport**. It is a ritual of social bonding that involves physical presence, travel, and shared time — high-bandwidth communication (lots of information exchanged) with non-verbal cues, shared meals, and the physical environment.

A phone call strips away the context — body, travel, environment — and leaves only voice. It is a low-bandwidth substitute. The Sanctuary Boundary prevents this substitution. By banning the phone from the living room, the *Ordnung* forces members to engage in physical visiting if they want to maintain relationships. Social bonding remains physical, embodied — preserving the high-coherence social fabric against the virtualization of relationships (turning real relationships into digital ones).

### 5.4 The "Fax in the Shanty" Anomaly

An Amish businessman operates a fax machine inside his phone shanty. When asked if the Bishop allows it, he replies, *"I AM the Bishop!"* The fax is permitted because it is contained within the shanty — a workspace boundary zone — not the home. It is a tool for economic interface (dealing with the economy), not domestic consumption.

The shanty acts as a **DMZ** (Demilitarized Zone — a neutral area) where the Amish can interact with the electronic world without letting it contaminate the Holy of Holies — the family table.

## Chapter 6 · Rule 5 — The Mutual Aid Imperative

The Mutual Aid Imperative is the economic cornerstone of the Coherence Factory. It says that **risk must be socialized, not commercialized** (shared by the group, not bought and sold). This rule is proven by the strict ban on commercial insurance.

### 6.1 Insurance as Disintegrating Force

Commercial insurance works on indemnity (making someone whole again after a loss): an individual pays a premium (a regular fee) to a distant corporation so that if disaster strikes, they are made financially whole without needing to rely on their neighbors. It buys *independence*.

To the Amish algorithm, this independence is a critical failure mode (a way the system can break). If a member does not need their neighbors to rebuild a barn after a fire, the bonds of community weaken. Insurance replaces the flesh-and-blood safety net with a contractual-financial safety net. It dissolves the glue that holds the community together.

### 6.2 The Barn Raising as Coherence Ritual

The alternative to insurance is **Mutual Aid** — Amish Aid Plans, barn raisings. When a disaster occurs, the community mobilizes. This is not just charity. It is a structural necessity. The disaster becomes a mechanism for reinforcing social solidarity (togetherness).

Amish liability aid certificates are explicitly *not* insurance contracts. They are promises of mutual assistance. They are not covered by state guaranty funds (government backup money). This lack of legal certainty forces the member to rely entirely on the moral certainty of the community.

### 6.3 The Ethos of Interdependence

The ban on commercial insurance makes sure every member remains existentially tethered (tied for survival) to the collective. You cannot afford to alienate (push away) your neighbors, because they are your fire insurance, your health insurance, and your social security.

Amish Hospital Aid pools resources to pay catastrophic medical bills, encouraging cost containment and community interdependence. The community negotiates with hospitals as a bloc (a single group), using their collective paying power. The system must reject any financial instrument that lets an individual buy their way out of mutual dependency. The *Ordnung* structurally engineers a state of vulnerability that can only be resolved through community cohesion. **The risk of the individual becomes the bond of the group.**

## Chapter 7 · Rule 6 — The Finite Input Rule

The Finite Input Rule controls the flow of resources into the system. It says that **unlimited flows create unlimited appetites**, leading to entropy. The system must impose artificial scarcity (fake shortage) or "metering" (measuring and limiting) on inputs like electricity, information, and speed.

### 7.1 Metered vs. Unmetered Existence

**Unmetered:** Encourages waste, passivity, and constant connection. The user forgets the cost of the resource. Leads to the hedonic treadmill (the cycle where you always need more to feel the same satisfaction) where the luxury of today becomes the necessity of tomorrow.

**Metered:** Enforces discipline. The user is always aware that the resource is depleting. The battery light is blinking. The propane tank is getting light.

The Amish rejection of "always-on" connection is a defense against the infinite scroll of modern culture. By relying on batteries and finite fuel supplies, the Amish maintain a psychological connection to the physical limits of the world. They live in a finite universe, which matches their theological view of creatureliness (being a created being with limits), whereas the English live in a techno-utopian illusion (a false belief that technology can solve everything) of infinite resources.

### 7.2 The Solar Power Dilemma

Solar panels are allowed in many districts because they are stand-alone (anti-bypass) and local. However, the storage of that power (batteries) remains finite.

Some conservative Amish groups worry that massive solar arrays combined with efficient inverters mimic the grid too closely, providing "too much" power and violating the Finite Input Rule. If a farmer has enough solar to run a freezer, a dryer, and a computer, he has effectively recreated the grid. Thus, the *Ordnung* often regulates the **capacity** of the system — limiting the number of panels or strictly enforcing the 12V limit — to make sure the input remains effectively finite. The goal is to prevent the energy surplus that leads to social surplus (leisure time spent on non-communal activities).

## Chapter 8 · Rule 7 — The Ownership Distinction

The Ownership Distinction creates a boundary condition regarding the relationship between user and tool. It allows **access** to a technology — using a taxi, using a neighbor's phone — while forbidding **ownership** (owning a car, owning a phone).

### 8.1 The Taxi Compromise

Critics often call the use of "Amish Taxis" hypocrisy ("If the car is evil, why ride in it?"). The algorithm reveals it as a brilliant filter.

**Ownership of Car:** Transforms identity. Restructures time (spontaneous travel), budget (insurance, payments), status. Creates an autonomy vector (a force that pulls toward independence). The owner becomes an individualist, capable of leaving the community on a whim.

**Access to Taxi:** Retains the utility of the car (distant funeral, hospital) but strips autonomy. Expensive, requires planning, involves a third party. A friction-heavy mode of transport.

### 8.2 Possession Changes the Habitus

*Possessing* a technology changes the human **habitus** — ingrained habits and dispositions (the way you naturally think and act). A car owner thinks like a motorist. A taxi passenger remains a buggy driver who is temporarily borrowing speed. This rule lets the Amish interact with the modern economy (buying supplies, visiting doctors) without becoming *of* the modern economy. They surf the wave of modernity without drowning in it.

### 8.3 Business Exception & Technological Dualism

Amish businesses may use higher levels of technology (computers, fax machines, hydraulic manufacturing systems) in the shop than are allowed in the home. The Shop is a border zone where the Amish interface with the World. The Home is the sanctuary. The Ownership Distinction allows for **technological dualism** (two different standards) — high-tech production, low-tech consumption.

The shop owner may "own" the pneumatic CNC router, but he does not "possess" it in his domestic life. The distinction prevents the logic of the market (efficiency) from bleeding into the logic of the home (discipleship — following Christ).

## Chapter 9 · Validating the Algorithm

The seven structural rules do not operate in isolation. They form a composite **Coherence Metric** — a set of variables that the Amish leadership intuitively monitor (watch without formal measurement) to assess the health of the community.

The primary metric being optimized is the **Attention Vector**. Outward Vectors (TV, Grid, Internet, Car, Public School) pull the individual's gaze toward the global market and the state, creating Social Diffusion that spreads the community thin. Inward Vectors (Buggy, Mutual Aid, Pennsylvania Dutch, Local School, Visiting) direct the gaze toward the neighbor and the church. The *Ordnung* functions to maximize the Inward Vector and minimize the Outward Vector. Every rule — from the shanty to the pneumatic drill — bends the attention vector back toward the community.

**Coherence Factory · Output Metrics**

~20 yrs — Population doubling time
85%+ — Youth retention
Held — Membrane integrity (internet age)

The Substitution Principle has been thwarted (blocked). The Amish have not substituted connection for efficiency — and have preserved the social coherence the rest of the industrialized world is rapidly losing.

## Conclusion · The Future of the Algorithm

The Amish *Ordnung* is a **system-preservation algorithm**. It is a rational, functional set of heuristics (rules of thumb) designed to filter Information Entropy and maintain high social coherence.

The **Technological Axiom of Distance** remains the central processing logic. By artificially maintaining distance — geographic, informational, energetic — the Amish preserve the distinctiveness of their system. They have effectively solved the Riddle of Amish Culture not by rejecting the world, but by filtering it through a high-friction membrane.

As the outside world becomes increasingly frictionless, unmetered, and high-velocity, the Amish algorithm offers a counter-model of friction-rich, metered, slow existence. It serves as a living laboratory for the value of **constraints** (limits) in an age of infinite excess. Their success is not due to isolation, but due to the rigorous application of these seven structural rules — which collectively manufacture the social coherence necessary for human flourishing in a high-entropy universe.

The Amish do not "overcome" distance. They inhabit it. And the membrane holds.
