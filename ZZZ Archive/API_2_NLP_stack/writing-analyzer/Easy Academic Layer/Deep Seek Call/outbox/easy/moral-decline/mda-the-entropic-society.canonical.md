# The Technology of Human Will: How Our Machines Are Reshaping Our Souls

## I. The Technology of Human Will

### A Conceptual and Critical Definition

To understand what's happening to American society, we need a clear definition of technology. Technology isn't just tools—phones, apps, or computers. It's something deeper. Technology is the **exteriorization of human will**—meaning, it's what happens when we take our inner intentions and build them into the world around us. It's the main way modern society organizes itself and holds together. So the direction technology takes shows us what we, as a culture, truly value. And right now, what we value most is maximizing, controlling, and extracting.

Technology is not merely hardware or software. It is the exteriorization of human will—and the will it externalizes is the will to maximize, to control, to extract.

### A. Technology as Exteriorized Intent and Cultural Force

Technology shapes culture. That's why we need to study it like we study philosophy or history. If philosophy tries to understand how everything relates to everything else, then we have to look at the moral footprint of our massive technological systems. The question isn't just "Does this gadget work?" but "What does this system do to people?"

This way of thinking means admitting that technology is never neutral. It always carries values. Technical systems are built to be efficient and powerful. When we use technology to interact with the world, we're automatically taking a maximizing, controlling approach. When that approach gets applied everywhere, it creates a "technological way of life." The collapse of social connections and the rise of mental health problems might be the direct result of technology driven by these unchecked values.

### B. The Moral Status of the Artifact: Critical Theory and Controllability

A **Critical Theory of technology** gives us the tools to investigate this decay. This view says two things at once: technology can be controlled (like the common-sense view says), AND technology carries values (like the critics say). This seems contradictory, but it's not. It means that while technology is built with values like efficiency, we can tame it by making the design process more democratic.

The real problem isn't technology itself. It's that we've failed to build systems and institutions that let humans control technological development. Think about it: people once thought the economy was an unstoppable force with its own laws. Then society said, "No, we can control this," and created democratic institutions to influence the economy. We need to do the same thing with technology. If technology embodies the will to maximize and control, and we don't balance that with democratic oversight, then the exteriorization of human will produces results that prioritize power over human flourishing—like the breakdown of trust and cooperation (Relational Coherence) and the rise of internal chaos (Psychological Entropy).

### C. The Architectural Vectors of Technological Emergence (1950–2025)

We can organize the last 75 years into three phases, defined by how people interacted with technology and how deeply it penetrated society.

**Phase 1: Mass Mediation (1950–1985)**

This era was defined by one-way, passive, shared consumption. The big technologies were broadcast TV, radio, and landline phones. TV adoption reached nearly every household. This changed how families spent time at home and how attention was managed. It set up centralized cultural stories and demanded high passive attention, preparing the ground for future changes in how people connected with each other.

**Phase 2: Individualized Access (1985–2005)**

This period shifted toward personal computing and active information searching. The Personal Computer (PC) and dial-up/early broadband internet defined this era. By early 2000, about half of U.S. adults were using the internet. This decentralized information—anyone could find things out—but it also demanded new skills for sorting through information. Cell phone use also took off, with two-thirds of Americans owning one by 2005.

**Phase 3: Perpetual Connectivity (2005–Present)**

This era is defined by mobile devices, fast internet, and social platforms woven into daily life. Today, **96% of U.S. adults use the internet**—near-complete saturation. The critical change was the explosive growth of social media and smartphones after 2005. Facebook grew 1,300% between 2005 and 2010. This technology shifts the mode to continuous, interactive, algorithm-driven engagement. **95% of youth ages 13–17** use social media, and many use it "almost constantly." This high psychological saturation demands constant peer connection and algorithmic attention capture—something completely different from earlier phases.

## II. The Aesthetic Transition

### Analog Durability to Digital Entropy

The **Aesthetic of Technological Entropy** is a framework for understanding how the design philosophy of modern technology mirrors—and maybe even causes—the social and psychological decay we're measuring. This aesthetic shift represents a fundamental change in what our culture expects to last.

### A. The Patina of Permanence (Analog Durability)

The earlier period of manufacturing (roughly Phase 1 and before) embraced the aesthetic of analog durability. This design philosophy valued high-quality materials, products built to be repaired, and longevity. The physical object was meant to absorb the effects of time, gaining value through use—the "patina" of age. This encouraged long-term investment, repair, and passing things down through generations. Consumers developed "aesthetic durability" by choosing timeless designs and rejecting disposable trends. This embedded cultural value in physical goods naturally reinforced social stability, supporting behaviors like thrift and repair that are essential for strong Relational Coherence.

### B. The Ascent of Planned and Perceived Obsolescence

The critical shift involves accepting an **expendable aesthetic** and **planned obsolescence**. This principle says products should be designed with a short lifespan—treated as "short-life toys" or replaceable parts. This idea was recognized early in design, especially in Metabolist architecture, which focused on the "joints" where fast and slow timeframes met, highlighting the concept of structural obsolescence. While some activist efforts since the 1960s championed reuse as an ecological strategy, consumer technology moved steadily toward disposability.

### C. Entropy as a Digital Design Principle

**Thermodynamic entropy** means systemic disorder and decay. We're applying this concept to technology aesthetics, moving from a physical idea about building materials to a functional feature of digital systems. In modern consumer electronics, entropy is not a failure—it's an engineered feature. This is the **commodification of systemic decay**—turning breakdown into something you can sell. Digital entropy shows up through closed hardware ecosystems, non-replaceable parts, and crucially, software incompatibility and security flaws that can't be patched. A device that works perfectly physically is rendered useless by digital decay, ensuring you have to keep buying new stuff.

The structural implication is huge: if technological artifacts are the exteriorization of human intent, then the shift toward designed decay shows a societal and corporate will that prioritizes maximum extraction (economic efficiency and power) over cultural permanence and stability. This technological entropy creates a negative feedback loop: it structurally undermines the value of durability, making society accept transience and systemic breakdown as normal. This engineered instability models the moral decline we're measuring: decay in Relational Coherence (unstable social bonds), decay in Informational Integrity (rapid turnover of facts, misinformation), and the collapse into Psychological Entropy (internal systemic breakdown).

In modern consumer electronics, entropy is not a failure—it is an engineered feature. The commodification of systemic decay ensures perpetual consumption, and structurally models the moral decline being measured.

## III. Longitudinal Data Synthesis

### Technology and Decline Vectors (1950–2025)

Mapping the timeline of three categories of moral decline against the three technological phases shows that technology's impact has been **differential and sequential**—different kinds of damage happened at different times, not all at once.

### A. Relational Coherence: Fragmentation of Social Bonds

Relational Coherence measures how strong and healthy our community and family structures are. The data suggests that the foundational decay in this area happened *before* individualized and perpetual digital access arrived.

**Decline in Civic Participation**

The withdrawal of citizens from voluntary groups and the unraveling of social connections happened **gradually and steadily after the 1950s and 1960s**. This decline affected many traditionally important organizations, including unions and groups like the PTA. **Social capital theory** (the idea that social networks have value) attributes this decline mainly to **generational replacement** (about half of the decline) and the rise of **television-watching** (about 25% of the decline).

The critical finding: the most significant decay in Relational Coherence was linked to Phase 1 (Mass Mediation). The shift from active group participation to passive, home-bound consumption fundamentally weakened the civic fabric. Adding to this is the long-term trend toward **structural isolation**: the percentage of U.S. households with only one person rose from 7.7% in 1940 to 27.6% in 2020—an accelerating, systemic trend toward living alone that spans all three phases.

**Marital Coherence and Isolation**

While divorce is often cited as a marker of moral decline, official statistics show that the U.S. divorce rate peaked around 1980 and has generally declined since then. This peak happened during the transition from Phase 1 to Phase 2. The fact that marital instability peaked *before* the mass spread of PCs, the internet, and smartphones suggests that changes in family structure were driven by broader social and cultural forces—not the digital revolution.

### B. Informational Integrity: Erosion of Institutional Trust

Informational Integrity means public confidence in government and information-providing institutions. The data shows a crisis that started long before the digital age, which then allowed for the fragmentation we see in the Perpetual Connectivity era.

**Trust in Government and Media**

Public trust in the federal government has suffered a profound, decades-long collapse. In 1958, about three-quarters of Americans trusted the government to act rightly. But trust began eroding during the 1960s, as the Vietnam War escalated, and kept falling through the 1970s with Watergate and economic struggles. Since 2007, confidence in government has not risen above 30%. A similar trend shows up in media: confidence in news reporting fell from 68–72% in the 1970s to 53% by 1997.

**Disillusionment Versus Fragmentation**

The data shows that the crisis of Informational Integrity was started and cemented during Phase 1 (Mass Mediation) and the early part of Phase 2, driven mainly by centralized communication of political failure and war. Phase 1 generated **disillusionment**—the loss of faith in centralized authorities—decades before social media existed. Phase 3 (Perpetual Connectivity) then accelerated **fragmentation**, providing an unprecedented platform for decentralized, often dubious information, which thrives in an environment where foundational trust in centralized sources has already been destroyed.

### C. Psychological Entropy: Internal Systemic Breakdown

Psychological Entropy is internal systemic breakdown—acute mental distress. This shows a strong, time-specific correlation with the saturation of Perpetual Connectivity technology (Phase 3).

**The Acute Rise of Mental Distress**

The most alarming trend is the surge in mental health issues, especially among youth. The prevalence of depression increased significantly from **8.2% in 2013–2014 to 13.1% in 2021–2023**. Current data shows that 16.0% of adolescents ages 12–17 have diagnosed anxiety, and 8.7% have diagnosed depression. The U.S. Surgeon General has identified loneliness as a public health epidemic. A majority of adults report feeling isolated (54%) or lacking companionship (50%) often or some of the time.

**Technological Causal Mechanisms**

While suicide rates fluctuated over the second half of the 20th century, the contemporary crisis is marked by the temporal alignment of the mental distress spike with the saturation point of social media adoption. Experimental studies establish a **causal link**—meaning, limited use of platforms like Facebook, Snapchat, and Instagram leads to improved well-being.

The evidence points to a biological mechanism behind Psychological Entropy. Excessive and problematic social media use—defined by adolescents spending an average of 3.5 hours per day on these platforms—is consistently linked to poor sleep quality, reduced sleep duration, and depression. Furthermore, frequent social media use may be associated with distinct changes in the developing brain's amygdala (which handles emotional learning) and the prefrontal cortex (which handles impulse control and emotional regulation). These changes increase sensitivity to social rewards and punishments, essentially engineering a perpetually unstable internal state that's vulnerable to stress and feelings of exclusion. The technology consumes the cognitive resources needed for stability, creating systemic internal disorder.

## IV. Correlation Analysis

### Establishing Linkages Between Entropy and Decline

The analysis confirms that technological entropy is significantly correlated with moral decline, but the nature of that correlation depends heavily on which phase of technology adoption we're looking at.

**Phase 1: Mass Mediation and the Foundation of Decay**

The strong statistical link between the rise of passive, broadcast media (Phase 1) and the start of large-scale social decay shows that the systemic vulnerabilities in American moral capital were laid decades ago. The mechanism here is substitution: television functionally replaced active group participation with isolated, home-based leisure, leading to the gradual "unraveling" of social capital. At the same time, the broadcast nature of this medium centralized the spread of political failures (Vietnam, Watergate), creating widespread and lasting disillusionment that destroyed Informational Integrity. This period started the cultural acceptance of technologically mediated, isolated consumption, structurally weakening society before the internet arrived.

**Phase 2: Individualized Access and Structural Dispersion**

Phase 2 (PC and early internet access) seems to function less as a primary cause of moral decline and more as a structural accelerant of existing trends. It provided tools for individualized retreat, contributing to the accelerating trend toward one-person households. While it introduced information dispersion, the mode of interaction remained active (you had to go find things), keeping the pressures of constant social comparison and engineered addiction (the hallmarks of Psychological Entropy) relatively low. The decline in Informational Integrity continued, but the core crisis of trust was already cemented in Phase 1.

**Phase 3: Perpetual Connectivity and Acute Psychological Entropy**

Phase 3 shows the highest and most acute correlation with decline, specifically regarding internal psychological stability. The mass adoption of smartphones and social media (post-2013) corresponds precisely with the sharp, measurable spike in youth anxiety and depression. The causal evidence confirms that the technology is not merely present alongside the decay but actively diminishes well-being when used excessively.

The crucial dynamic identified is the **entropic feedback loop**: the aesthetic of digital entropy (designed instability and transience) feeds directly into psychological entropy. The algorithmic goal of maximizing attention capture and engagement creates a technological environment that interferes with the biological mechanisms of emotional regulation and sleep. Users are driven toward perpetual cognitive and emotional instability (FOMO, comparison stress), mirroring the disposable and unmaintainable nature of the hardware and software itself. The efficiency goal of the technical system (maximum extraction of attention/data) directly causes the internal systemic breakdown (Psychological Entropy).

The efficiency goal of the technical system—maximum extraction of attention and data—directly causes the internal systemic breakdown that is Psychological Entropy. The machine optimizes. The human disintegrates.

## V. Falsification and Alternative Causal Pathways

To avoid the oversimplified conclusion that technology alone causes everything, we need to test for other factors that may have accelerated or started the decline independently of technology. The evidence suggests that technology often reflects or speeds up existing societal weaknesses.

### A. The Non-Deterministic Counter-Thesis

Many critical analyses emphasize that technology mainly accelerates or provides a platform for pre-existing societal tensions and trends. For example, technology enables the gig economy, but it's the pre-existing economic insecurity that makes workers rely on insecure, technology-mediated labor. The societal harms we see are not necessarily the inevitable outcome of technological progress, but rather the result of society failing to put adequate civic or institutional structures in place to manage the pace of change.

### B. Socioeconomic and Political Drivers of Decline

Non-technological factors provide powerful alternative explanations for the erosion of moral capital:

**Economic Inequality**

Rising economic inequality has been shown to be a critical driver of social breakdown. Inequality creates a competitive economic climate that directly erodes social cohesion. It causes fractured social environments and correlates strongly with increased moralization—the rise in using harsh moral language and judgments against others. If economic pressure structurally weakens social bonds, individuals may retreat into digital spaces for validation and connection, making them highly vulnerable to the psychological harms of constant social comparison and technologically engineered stress. Economic strain acts as a major confounding variable that both starts and magnifies technological harm.

**Political Polarization and Conflict**

The widespread feeling that we're in moral free fall is a point of agreement across political affiliations, despite deep polarization. Societal division is frequently cited as a significant source of stress among adults. These political and ideological conflicts, often rooted in historical, economic, and cultural divisions, substantially predate the mass adoption of Phase 3 technology. Technology, in this view, didn't create the division but provided a highly efficient, fragmented, and emotionally charged platform for existing moral conflict to be amplified and weaponized, accelerating the decay of Informational Integrity.

### C. Generational Replacement and Cultural Momentum

Robert Putnam's definitive analysis of civic decline places overwhelming emphasis on **generational replacement**. The replacement of the "long civic generation" (formed by shared experiences of the Great Depression and World War II) by generations with less inherent tendency toward group involvement accounts for roughly half of the decline in Relational Coherence. This perspective reinforces the finding that the decay of civic bonds was fundamentally a cultural shift—one facilitated by Phase 1 technology (TV) but driven primarily by demographic momentum, confirming that the decline in Relational Coherence had deep, non-digital origins.

## VI. Conclusion and Policy Recommendations

The investigation into the Aesthetic of Technological Entropy confirms a profound, time-specific link between technological evolution and the degradation of American moral capital from 1950 to the present. The analysis rejects a simple, single-cause story and establishes that technology's impact has been **differential and sequential**.

The **Mass Mediation era (Phase 1)** started the fundamental structural decay, establishing an isolating media consumption model that severely undermined **Relational Coherence** (civic bonds) and generating the systemic political and media failures that destroyed **Informational Integrity** (institutional trust). The **Perpetual Connectivity era (Phase 3)** then exploited these pre-existing social and informational vacuums, introducing an acute, potentially causal crisis of **Psychological Entropy** driven by the biologically destabilizing effects of attention-capture design.

The core finding: the technological imperative of efficiency and maximization, when exteriorized without institutional control, creates an aesthetic of entropic transience (planned obsolescence) that structurally models and enables moral decay, culminating in the engineered instability of the human psyche.

### A. Recommendations: Towards Technological Democracy

**1. Democratizing Technology Design and Deployment**

Technological systems must be submitted to a more democratic process to ensure that their embedded values go beyond maximization and control. This requires mandating design transparency, allowing public scrutiny of algorithmic objectives, and holding technological corporations accountable for the measurable negative externalities (Psychological Entropy) produced by their systems. The goal is to establish institutions capable of directing technological development toward social stability, mirroring the historical democratization of economic control.

**2. Countering Digital Entropy and Planned Obsolescence**

Policy intervention must prioritize permanence over engineered transience. Implementing strong legal frameworks that enforce a "Right-to-Repair" for both hardware and software would directly resist the aesthetic of entropy. By encouraging durability and discouraging planned obsolescence, governance can reinforce cultural values of investment, repair, and long-term stability that underpin Relational Coherence.

**3. Mitigating the Crisis of Psychological Entropy**

Given the acute, demonstrable link between excessive Phase 3 technology use and youth mental distress, immediate and targeted public health interventions are justified. These measures should include mandating default settings that impose age-appropriate usage limits, restrict features that interfere with sleep, and prohibit designs engineered to overstimulate the developing brain's reward centers. Addressing the public health epidemic of loneliness and disconnection requires not only limiting harmful exposure but also prioritizing and funding community-based interventions that emphasize non-mediated social interaction to rebuild the Relational Coherence eroded by preceding technological phases.
