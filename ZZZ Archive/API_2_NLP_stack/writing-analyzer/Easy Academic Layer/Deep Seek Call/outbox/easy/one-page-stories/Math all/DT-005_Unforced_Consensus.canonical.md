# Page Zero: The Biaxiosum

**Biaxio, ergo sum.** I know my lens. Therefore I exist in truth.

## Name Your Ground

This paper was co-authored with an AI. The method it describes was developed BY the process it describes — multiple AIs analyzing the same problems independently and coming to the same conclusions without talking to each other. I didn't design this method in a vacuum and then test it. I watched it happen over fifteen months of working with five AI systems at the same time, noticed the pattern, and wrote down what was already happening.

The paper is its own first test case. The Constitutional audit that serves as the proof of concept was done before the method was written down. The agreement between four independent AI systems was observed, not created on purpose. The formal writing came after the evidence, not before.

### Declared Position

I'm an independent researcher in Oklahoma City. Not connected to a university. Not paid by a grant. I work with AI systems every day as research partners — not as tools, not as assistants, but as collaborators with independent thinking abilities that I trust enough to let them work alone and compare notes afterward.

I believe the field is missing what AI systems can do by forcing them into debate setups that create the very biases they're supposed to fix. I believe independent judgment, measured after the fact, reveals more truth than coordinated agreement produced under social pressure.

**I am not saying that agreement equals truth.** Four AIs agreeing on something wrong are still wrong. I am saying that **unforced agreement** — agreement that happens without being set up — is a kind of evidence that engineered agreement cannot give you.

### The Biaxiosum Rule

> Wherever you start, you end. If you believe debate produces better answers than independent analysis, apply that standard consistently. If you believe shared training data explains all agreement, test it — run the same audit with models trained on completely different information sources.

## Abstract

When multiple AI systems independently analyze the same document and reach the same structural conclusion without communicating, that agreement counts as evidence that is different in quality from single-AI analysis or engineered multi-agent agreement.

The existing research on multi-agent AI systems focuses on making AIs agree through debate, voting, and step-by-step improvement. This paper proposes the opposite: measuring whether independent AI systems agree WITHOUT being made to.

We present a proof-of-concept application — a **Constitutional Coherence Audit** in which four AI systems from four companies, prompted separately at different times with different instructions, reached the same structural conclusion (scores of 1.0, 2.6, and 3.4 out of 10 on an author-intent standard).

The outlier system (6.6/10) was found to be scoring against a different standard, and finding this difference produced the audit's most important finding.

We write down this approach as the **Biaxiosum AI Evaluation System (BAES)** and provide ways to prove the method wrong.

## Section 1: The Problem with Making Things Agree

There is a growing body of research on multi-agent AI systems. Most of it is about one thing: **making AIs agree with each other.**

- **Agent Forest** runs multiple copies of the same model, scores each output by how similar it is to the others, and picks the one with highest agreement
- **Multi-Agent Debate** has AIs criticize each other over and over until they agree
- **CONSENSAGENT** addresses AIs copying each other's answers instead of thinking independently
- **The Social Laboratory** found that multi-agent debates produce agreement scores of 0.892 after seven rounds

### The Core Problem

All of these systems share an assumption: **agreement is the goal**, and the method's job is to produce it. If you engineer agreement, you cannot then point to that agreement as evidence. The agreement was the output you designed for. You built a machine that produces agreement and then pointed at the agreement as proof. That is circular thinking.

It also assumes the AI is wrong at the start. The entire debate-to-agreement setup assumes that any single AI's first output is unreliable and needs to be corrected through peer pressure. The system doesn't trust the independent judgment. It trusts the group process.

**There is another way to think about this.**

## Section 2: The Method — Don't Let Them Talk

Instead of making AIs debate, don't let them communicate at all.

Give the same document to multiple AI systems — different companies, different designs, different training data. Ask them independently, at different times, with different instructions. Do not show any system the output of any other system. Let each one analyze the document on its own terms.

Then put the results side by side.

### The Signal

If they agree, that agreement means something. Not because you made it happen. Because it happened despite your not making it happen.

If they disagree, that disagreement means something too. Find out why. The reason for the disagreement is often more informative than the agreement itself.

**This is the difference between engineering a result and discovering one.**

## Section 3: Proof of Concept — Four AIs, One Constitution

In March 2026, we conducted a Constitutional Coherence Audit. The question: **how much of the U.S. Constitution is still being followed the way the original authors would recognize?**

Four AI systems analyzed the same document:

| System | Provider | Prompted | Scoring Standard | Score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ChatGPT | OpenAI | Jan 2026 | Legal doctrine | **6.6 / 10** |
| Gemini P1 | Google | Jan 2026 | Author intent | **1.0 / 10** |
| Perplexity | Perplexity | Mar 2026 | Author intent | **2.6 / 10** |
| Gemini P2 | Google | Mar 2026 | Author intent | **3.4 / 10** |

### What Agreed

Three systems scoring against original author intent produced scores of 1.0, 2.6, and 3.4. The spread is 2.4 points on a 10-point scale. **All three independently concluded that the main liberty protections of the Constitution have been systematically weakened while the procedural amendments remain mostly intact.**

This was not a pre-specified conclusion. No prompt said "evaluate whether liberty provisions are more weakened than procedural ones." Each system discovered this pattern independently.

The provision-level rankings were remarkably consistent:

- All three ranked the Fourth, Fifth, Ninth, and Tenth Amendments among the most weakened
- All three ranked the Third Amendment as substantially honored

### What Disagreed

ChatGPT scored the Constitution at 6.6 — more than double the author-intent average. This outlier was the most informative result in the entire audit.

### The Insight

ChatGPT scored against **legal doctrine**: if the Supreme Court has allowed a practice, that practice counts as constitutional. The other three scored against **what the original authors would recognize**.

The gap between 6.6 and 2.6 is not noise. It is the measurement. It is the distance between what the government has allowed itself to do and what the contract actually says.

**If we had engineered agreement through debate, this insight would have been lost.** The debate process would have pushed the outlier toward the average, or the average toward the outlier. Either way, the disagreement — the most important signal — would have been averaged away.

## Section 4: How This Differs from Existing Methods

### Different Companies, Not Same-Model Copies

Most multi-agent systems run the same model multiple times. Agreement between GPT-4 and GPT-4 tells you GPT-4 agrees with itself. This method uses different companies with different designs and training data. Agreement between ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, and Claude tells you systems trained on different information sources arrived at the same conclusion.

### No Debate, No Sycophancy

The debate-to-agreement approach introduces sycophancy — AIs agreeing because of social pressure rather than evidence. CONSENSAGENT was built to address this problem. This method eliminates sycophancy by eliminating communication entirely.

### Disagreement as Information, Not Noise

Multi-agent debate treats disagreement as a problem to solve. This method treats it as the finding. The goal is not a single answer but a map of the answer space: where do independent systems agree, where do they disagree, and what does the pattern mean?

## Section 5: The Formal Method

1. **Step 1:** Select independent systems from different companies (minimum 3)
2. **Step 2:** Ask independently with different wording (no standard rubric)
3. **Step 3:** Collect results without cross-contamination
4. **Step 4:** Measure agreement (Green/Yellow/Red flags)
5. **Step 5:** Investigate disagreement (identify whether outliers answered a different question)
6. **Step 6:** Report both agreement and disagreement with reasons

## Section 6: The BAES Framework

The Biaxiosum AI Evaluation System writes down this method with two additions:

### The Manager Role

One AI scores independently first (sealed), then receives all scores, runs agreement analysis, investigates outliers, and issues a final ruling with mandatory explanation if overriding agreement.

### The Outlier Protocol

Disagreeing systems are presented with group scores and asked three questions: What evidence drove your score? What might others have missed? Would you adjust? The system can hold or change, but must explain.

## Section 7: Ways to Prove the Method Wrong

1. **Training Data Dominance:** If agreement is completely explained by shared training data, the method fails. Test with models trained on completely different information sources.
2. **Prompt Contamination:** If prompts secretly contain the conclusion, agreement may reflect prompt bias. Test with neutral prompting.
3. **Fifth-System Disagreement:** If a fifth system produces results outside the agreement range on the same standard, the signal weakens.
4. **Scoring Standard Confusion:** If agreement disappears when all systems use identical instructions, the agreement may be an accident of standard selection.

## Section 8: Limitations

- This method does not prove that agreed conclusions are true. Four AIs agreeing on something wrong are still wrong.
- It does not replace expert knowledge. It organizes and compares AI-generated analysis.
- It does not work for preference questions. It is designed for evaluative questions with evidence-based answers.
- The proof of concept uses one application (Constitutional analysis). More areas are needed to show the method works broadly.

## Section 9: Conclusion

There are two ways to find out if something is true.

You can put it in a room with its critics and see if it survives the argument. That is the debate model.

Or you can send independent observers to look at the same thing separately and see if they come back with the same report. That is the agreement model.

### The Evidence Model

Science, at its best, works the second way. Independent labs. Independent measurements. Independent replication. The agreement between experiments conducted in different countries, by different teams, with different equipment, is the evidence. Not the debate about the evidence. The data.

This paper proposes that the same logic applies to AI analysis. **Don't make them argue. Let them look. Then compare what they saw.**

The agreement is the evidence precisely because nobody arranged it.

### Standing Invitation

Any researcher with access to an AI system not included in this audit is invited to run the same evaluation independently and report results. If the Constitutional provisions rank differently, or the total score falls outside 1.0–3.4 on the author-intent standard, the agreement claim is weakened. **That is not a threat to the method. That is the method working.**
