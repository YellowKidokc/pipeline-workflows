# The Peak Window: 1940–1958

The data all points to one thing: there was a peak time of coherence (things working together smoothly) between about 1940 and 1958. This doesn't mean the era was morally perfect — it wasn't. Jim Crow laws were still in place. Women had limited freedom. Mental illness was hidden and shamed. These are real, serious moral failures.

But coherence and justice are two different measurements. Coherence measures how well the parts of a system fit together and support each other. A system can be coherent and still be unjust. What it cannot be is incoherent (falling apart) and stable.

Here's what peak coherence looked like in the numbers:

### Family

- Divorce rate: 2.2 per thousand people (1960), down from earlier spikes
- Only 28% of adults were unmarried
- Non-marital birth rate for white babies: 3.1% (1965)
- Living together without marriage: basically didn't happen
- Children raised by both biological parents: the overwhelming normal situation

### Religious

- Weekly church attendance peaked at 49% in 1958 (Gallup poll)
- About 87% of Americans identified as Christian
- "Nones" (people with no religious affiliation) were in the single digits
- The church was the center of community life, not just one option among many

### Institutional Trust

In 1958, 73% of Americans told the National Election Study they trusted the federal government to do the right thing "most of the time" or "just about always." Seventy-three percent. Think about that number. Today it's under 20%.

### Education

- SAT verbal scores were at 478
- High school graduation rates were climbing
- The education system passed along a shared cultural story — flawed and incomplete, but _shared_

### Media

- The Hays Code (movie censorship rules) was fully enforced
- Television was new and had to follow FCC rules that treated broadcasting as a public trust
- Profanity was absent from public media
- Pornography was illegal and underground
- The Pornography Access Index (a combined measure of legal status, technology, and cultural reach) sat at zero

### Economic

- Union membership: 34.8% of workers
- The gold standard tied money to physical reality
- Personal savings rates were high
- Home ownership grew through programs (VA loans, FHA) that built wealth rather than extracting it

**Composite Coherence Index: 89–91 out of 100 — Peak Window**

## What Made It Work (The Physics)

Let me pause and explain what was actually happening using the language of the equation. This isn't nostalgia — it's thermodynamics (the physics of how energy and order work).

In any coherent system, you need three things working at the same time:

### First: A Strong Negentropic Source (G)

This is something that continuously pumps order into the system, fighting against the natural drift toward disorder. In America's peak window, that source came from multiple institutions that reinforced each other. The church provided moral order. The family provided relational order. The legal system provided behavioral order. The economic system provided material order. These weren't separate — they were _coupled_ (connected and strengthening each other). The church supported the family. The family supported being part of the community. Being part of the community supported trust in institutions. Trust supported economic stability. It was a positive feedback loop of coherence.

### Second: High Observer Receptivity (O)

The population was tuned in to receive the signal. Church attendance wasn't just a social habit — it was a weekly reset. Families ate dinner together. Neighbors knew each other's names. The observer function was high because the way society was built _required_ people to engage with the sources of coherence.

### Third: Managed Entropy (S)

Entropy (disorder) never goes to zero — that's the second law of thermodynamics. But in a well-functioning system, entropy gets absorbed, processed, and spread out before it builds up past the danger point. Social problems existed in the 1940s and 1950s. But the system had ways to handle them — not perfectly, not always fairly, but structurally. The system had mechanisms for processing disorder without being destroyed by it.

The equation says this setup produces rising coherence: dC/dt > 0 (the rate of change of coherence over time is positive). And that's what the data shows. From 1900 to 1958, in every measurable area, the trend lines moved toward greater integration.

## What Was Already Fragile

Here's where I need to push back against the "good old days" way of thinking. Because the equation also reveals something that nostalgia misses: the system was _brittle_ (easily shattered).

High coherence that's maintained by outside pressure — rather than by people's inner beliefs — is structurally weak. Think of it like a crystal: highly ordered, but one sharp hit in the right place shatters the whole thing.

Three specific weaknesses were already present in the peak window:

### 1. Conformity as a Substitute for Conviction

A lot of the behavioral coherence in the 1940s and 1950s came from social pressure, not from genuine moral growth. People went to church because that's what you did. People stayed married because divorce carried shame. When the outside pressure was removed — as it would be, catastrophically, in 1968-1973 — the behavioral coherence collapsed almost overnight. That speed of collapse tells you the internal coherence was lower than the outside measurements suggested.

In equation terms: the observed C (coherence you could measure) was artificially inflated by institutional enforcement. The _actual_ C — the coherence held by individual conviction — was lower. The gap between observed and actual coherence is the measure of the system's fragility.

### 2. Justice Deficits as Entropy Seeds

Jim Crow. Treating women as less than equal. Systematically excluding entire groups of people from the system didn't just harm those people — it planted seeds of disorder in the system itself. A moral system that says "all men are created equal" while enforcing racial hierarchy has an internal contradiction. That contradiction is stored entropy, waiting for something to trigger its release.

The civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s was, in this framework, not the cause of moral decline but the _exposure_ of an entropy reservoir that had been building for generations. The system had to deal with this entropy. The question was whether it could do so without falling apart. The answer, as we'll see, was no — because the entropy release from fixing injustice happened at the same time as entropy release from many other sources.

### 3. The Single-Signal Problem

Almost all of the order-creating input came through one cultural channel: Protestant Christianity filtered through American civic religion. There was no backup signal. No backup plan. No second source of coherence that could keep the system going if the main source weakened. When church attendance started falling, there was nothing to catch the system.

In information theory terms: the system had no error correction (a way to fix mistakes in the signal). A single-source signal with no backup is maximally vulnerable to noise (interference). When the noise came — and it came from every direction at once — the signal was overwhelmed.
