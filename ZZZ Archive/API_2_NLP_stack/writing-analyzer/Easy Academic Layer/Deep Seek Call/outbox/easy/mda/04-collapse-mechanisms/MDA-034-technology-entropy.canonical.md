# THE ENTROPIC SOCIETY

Technology, Obsolescence, and the Devaluation of American Moral Capital

1950 — 2025

Phase 1: Mass Mediation (1950–1985)
Phase 2: Individual Access (1985–2005)
Phase 3: Perpetual Connect (2005–Present)

Entropy is not a failure — it is an engineered feature

Section I

## The Technology of Human Will

A Big-Picture Definition

To understand how American morals have changed, we need a deeper definition of technology. Technology is not just tools and gadgets. It is a cultural force — an **exteriorization of human will** (meaning: it takes what humans want and puts it out into the world as objects and systems).¹ Technology is how modern society organizes itself and "hangs together."² So the way technology develops shows us what people value, especially the drive for more control and maximum results.

### A. Technology as What We Want, Made Real

Technology shapes culture, so we have to study it like we study philosophy and history.² If philosophy asks how everything in the world relates to everything else, then technology's huge reach forces us to ask about its moral impact.

Here's the key idea: technology is **value-laden** (it carries values inside it).¹ Technical systems are built for efficiency and power. When humans use technology to interact with the world, they are automatically taking a maximizing, controlling approach. When this approach is everywhere, it creates a "technological way of life."

### B. Can We Control Technology? A Critical View

A **Critical Theory** of technology gives us the lens to see the decay happening. This view agrees with one side (instrumentalism) that technology can be controlled, but also agrees with the other side (substantivism) that technology carries values.¹ This sounds like a contradiction, but here's what it means: technology does embed values like efficiency, but we can tame it by letting more people have a say in how it's designed and used.

The main point: the problem isn't technology itself. The problem is that **our institutions failed to build systems that let humans control technological development**.¹

### C. The Three Phases of Technology

Phase 1: 1950–1985

#### Mass Mediation

One-way, passive, shared consumption. Broadcast TV, radio, landline phones. TV reached almost everyone, reshaping attention and how people spent time at home.³ It created centralized cultural stories and demanded high passive attention.⁴

Phase 2: 1985–2005

#### Individualized Access

Personal computers and active information searching. PC and early internet. By 2000, about half of U.S. adults were online.⁵ This decentralized information and gave individuals power to find things. Two-thirds owned cell phones by 2005.⁶

Phase 3: 2005–Present

#### Perpetual Connectivity

Mobile phones, fast internet, social media platforms. 96% of U.S. adults are online.⁵ Facebook grew 1,300% between 2005 and 2010.⁶ 95% of teens ages 13–17 use social media; many are on it "almost constantly."⁷

Section II

## The Aesthetic Transition

From Things That Last to Things That Break on Purpose

### A. The Beauty of Permanence (Analog Durability)

Earlier manufacturing had an aesthetic of analog durability (things built to last). This design philosophy used high-quality materials, products made to be repaired, and long lifespans.⁸ The physical object was meant to absorb the effects of time, gaining value as it aged — the "patina" of use. This encouraged people to invest in things, repair them, and pass them down to the next generation. This embedded cultural value in physical goods and naturally reinforced social stability.

### B. The Rise of Planned and Perceived Obsolescence

The big shift was accepting an **expendable aesthetic** (things meant to be thrown away) and **planned obsolescence** (things designed to break or become useless quickly).⁹ This principle says products should have a short life on purpose. Objects are treated as "short-life toys" or replaceable parts in a temporary system.

### C. Entropy as a Digital Design Principle

In modern consumer electronics, entropy (the natural tendency toward disorder and decay) is not a failure — it is an engineered feature. This is the **commodification of systemic decay** (turning decay into something you can sell). Digital entropy shows up in closed hardware systems (you can't open them), non-replaceable batteries, and software that stops working with old devices. A phone that works perfectly fine physically becomes useless because of digital decay. This guarantees you keep buying new stuff.

If technology is what humans want made real, then the shift toward designed decay shows that society and corporations want maximum extraction (taking as much money as possible) over cultural permanence and stability.

#### The Entropic Feedback Loop

- **Relational Coherence** → Unstable social bonds
- **Informational Integrity** → Rapid turnover of facts, misinformation
- **Psychological Entropy** → Internal systemic breakdown

Section III

## Long-Term Data

Technology and Decline (1950–2025)

### A. Relational Coherence: Social Bonds Falling Apart

#### 1. Decline in Civic Participation

People stopped joining clubs, churches, and volunteer groups **gradually and steadily after the 1950s and 1960s**.⁴ Social capital theory (the study of how social networks have value) says this decline is mostly caused by **generational replacement** (older generations who were joiners dying off and being replaced by less-join-y generations) — that accounts for about 50% of the decline. The rise of **TV watching** accounts for about 25% of the decline.⁴

One-person households rose from 7.7% (1940) to 27.6% (2020). This is a long-term trend toward structural isolation (living alone by design) that spans all three technological phases.¹¹

#### 2. Marriage and Isolation

The U.S. divorce rate peaked around 1980 and has generally declined since.¹² This peak happened during the transition from Phase 1 to Phase 2. The fact that marriage instability peaked **before** PCs, the internet, and smartphones became widespread suggests that changes in family structure were driven by big social and cultural forces **separate from and before the digital revolution**.

### B. Informational Integrity: Trust Eroding

75% of Americans trusted the government in 1958. Less than 30% have trusted it since 2007.¹³ Trust started falling during the 1960s with the Vietnam War and kept falling through the 1970s with Watergate.¹³ Confidence in the media fell from 68–72% in the 1970s to 53% by 1997.¹⁵

Phase 1 created **disillusionment** — people lost faith in centralized authorities. Phase 3 then accelerated **fragmentation** — it gave a platform for decentralized, often fake information that thrives where trust has already been destroyed.¹⁶

### C. Psychological Entropy: Internal Breakdown

From 2013 to 2023, teens spent an average of 3.5 hours per day on social media. The current crisis is marked by the fact that the spike in mental distress lines up exactly with when social media reached saturation (almost everyone had it).¹⁷ Experimental studies have established a **causal link** (not just a correlation, but proof that one causes the other) between limited social media use and improved well-being.²³

Frequent social media use may be linked to changes in the developing brain's amygdala (which handles emotional learning) and the prefrontal cortex (which handles impulse control and emotional regulation).⁷ These changes make people more sensitive to social rewards and punishments. This engineers a perpetually unstable internal state — like a machine designed to keep you off-balance.

Section IV

## Correlation Analysis

How Entropy and Decline Are Connected

Phase 1

#### Foundation of Decay

TV functionally replaced active group participation with isolated, at-home leisure. This led to the gradual "unraveling" of social capital.⁴ At the same time, broadcast media centralized the spread of political failures, creating widespread disillusionment.¹³

Phase 2

#### Structural Dispersion

This phase functions less as a primary cause and more as a structural accelerant (something that speeds up existing trends). It provided tools for individualized retreat, contributing to the accelerating trend of one-person households.¹¹ The mode of interaction was still active (you had to go find information).

Phase 3

#### Acute Psychological Entropy

This phase has the highest and most acute correlation with decline, specifically in internal psychological stability. Mass smartphone and social media adoption (after 2013) lines up exactly with the youth anxiety and depression spike.¹⁷ Causal evidence has been confirmed.²³

The algorithmic goal of maximizing attention capture creates a technological environment that interferes with the biological mechanisms of emotional regulation and sleep. The efficiency goal of the technical system directly causes internal systemic breakdown.

Section V

## Falsification & Alternative Explanations

### A. The Non-Deterministic Counter-Argument

Technology mainly accelerates or provides a platform for pre-existing societal tensions and trends.²⁴ The harms we see are not necessarily the inevitable result of technological progress. They are the result of society failing to put adequate civic or institutional structures in place to manage the pace of change.

### B. Socioeconomic and Political Drivers

#### Economic Inequality

Rising inequality creates a competitive economic climate that directly erodes social cohesion.²⁶ It correlates with increased moralization (people using harsher moral language and judgments). Economic strain is a major confounding variable (a third factor that affects both things you're studying) that both starts and magnifies technological harm.

#### Political Polarization

The feeling that morals are in free fall is something both political sides agree on.²⁷ Political and ideological conflicts substantially predate Phase 3 technology. Technology did not create the division. It provided a highly efficient platform for existing moral conflict to be amplified.

### C. Generational Replacement and Cultural Momentum

Robert Putnam's definitive analysis places overwhelming emphasis on **generational replacement**.⁴ The replacement of the "long civic generation" (people who grew up during the Great Depression and WWII and were very join-y) by generations with less natural tendency for group involvement accounts for roughly half of the decline in Relational Coherence. The decay of civic bonds was fundamentally a cultural shift — made possible by Phase 1 technology (TV) but driven mainly by demographic momentum (the simple fact that older generations died and younger ones took their place).

Section VI

## Conclusion & Policy Recommendations

This investigation confirms a deep, time-specific connection between technological evolution and the degradation of American moral capital from 1950 to the present. The analysis rejects a simple, single-cause story. It establishes that technology's impact has been **different at different times and in different ways**.

#### Core Finding

The technological drive for efficiency and maximum results, when put into the world without institutional control, creates an aesthetic of entropic transience (things designed to fall apart). This structurally models and enables moral decay. It culminates in the **engineered instability of the human psyche**.

### A. Recommendations for Restoring Moral Capital

1

#### Democratizing Technology Design

Require design transparency. Let the public see what algorithms are trying to do. Hold corporations accountable for negative side effects. Create institutions that direct technological development toward social stability.¹

2

#### Countering Digital Entropy

Implement strong "Right-to-Repair" laws for both hardware and software. By encouraging durability and discouraging planned obsolescence, we reinforce cultural values of investment, repair, and long-term stability.⁸

3

#### Mitigating Psychological Entropy

Require age-appropriate usage limits. Restrict features that interfere with sleep. Ban designs engineered to overstimulate developing brains. Prioritize community-based interventions that emphasize face-to-face social interaction.⁷
