# The Control Group

We've traced the decline. From 1900 to 2024. From a coherence score (χ, pronounced "kai") of 0.85 down to 0.27.

But here's the question that matters: **Was it inevitable?**

Was there something about modern life that forced this collapse? Some iron law of progress that said we had to sacrifice social health for wealth?

The answer is no.

We know because we have a control group (a group we can compare against).

"The Amish don't reject technology. They reject the breaking of boundaries that technology allows."

Two populations. Same country. Same century. Same legal rights.

**Group A (Mainstream America):** Removed rules starting around 1968. Embraced "freedom."

**Group B (Amish):** Kept rules. Rejected "progress."

If the Theophysics idea is correct (rules → order, freedom → disorder), Group B should do better than Group A on every measure of health and stability — even though they have less technology, less education, and less money.

Result: They do. On everything.

## The Seven Mechanisms They Kept

The Amish aren't magic. They're strategic. They figured out what destroys social health and blocked it.

### The Proximity Limit

**Principle:** Community works best when people are physically together.

Church districts of 20-40 families meet in each other's homes. When a district gets too big, it *splits*. Not because of fighting, but because the system demands it. They respect Dunbar's number (~150 meaningful relationships). You can't have real connection beyond that limit.

**What we lost:** Mega-churches of 10,000 strangers. Social media "friends" numbering thousands. Communities based on ideas rather than presence.

### The Friction Requirement

**Principle:** Easy is the enemy of meaningful.

The Amish don't ban cars — they ban *owning* cars. You can hire a driver. But the hassle of arranging a ride serves a purpose. No home telephones (you must walk to the phone shanty). Friction forces you to think before acting. Inconvenience creates space for community. Working together builds bonds.

**What we lost:** One-click buying. Instant messaging. Endless entertainment at your fingertips.

When everything is easy, nothing is meaningful. Friction is a feature, not a bug.

### The Anti-Bypass Rule

**Principle:** Technology that lets you skip community is rejected.

**Accepted:** Pneumatic tools (need shared air compressor). Shared community phone. **Rejected:** Personal smartphones (let you isolate yourself). Individual home phones.

**What we lost:** Streaming replaced watching together. Food delivery replaced family dinner. Remote work replaced the office as a social space.

Judge technology by what it does to relationships, not what it does for you as an individual.

### The Sanctuary Boundary

**Principle:** The home is protected from information invasion.

No television. No internet. No social media. No algorithm-driven feeds inside the house. This isn't about the content — it's about the *way* it enters. Parents control what information comes in. Children's attention isn't stolen by outside forces. The family doesn't compete with anything for time together.

**What we lost:** Average American: 7+ hours daily screen time. Average teen: 4.8 hours social media daily. The home became another place for ads.

Whoever controls the information flow controls how minds are shaped. Protect the sanctuary.

### The Mutual Aid Imperative

**Principle:** Depending on each other is built into the system, not avoided.

No health insurance. No retirement accounts. No government benefits. Instead: barn raisings (the whole community builds your barn in one day), medical bills paid from a shared community fund, elderly care provided by family (never hired out). This creates real obligation. It makes leaving costly. It turns charity into relationship, not just a transaction.

**What we lost:** Insurance spreads risk among strangers. Social Security replaces family duty. GoFundMe takes the place of community.

A community where no one needs anyone is a community that doesn't exist.

### The Finite Input Rule

**Principle:** Limit information to what the community can actually talk about.

They read: the Bible, the *Budget* (Amish community newspaper), practical farming and trade publications. They don't read: national news, political commentary, celebrity gossip, endless scrolling feeds. Shared information creates shared reality. The community can actually discuss what everyone knows.

**What we lost:** Infinite content streams. Algorithms that show everyone different "news." No common information base.

A community needs a shared knowledge base. Infinite information breaks reality into pieces.

### The Ownership Distinction

**Principle:** Know the difference between tools you control and systems that control you.

The Amish own: horse and buggy (they control movement), hand tools (they control labor), their land (they control production). The Amish avoid owning: cars (insurance, registration, debt, highways — outside systems), grid electricity (dependence on utility companies), smartphones (platforms own your attention).

**What we lost:** We don't own our social connections (platforms do). We don't own our content (terms of service do). We don't own our attention (algorithms do).

If you can't turn it off without penalty, you don't own it — it owns you.

## The Structural Interpretation

The Amish are not better people. They are not smarter, kinder, or more virtuous than other Americans. They are a population living inside a **structural system that keeps its binding force** (the force that holds things together).

**America after 1968:** The external field (h) was set to zero. Authority was taken apart — in law (no-fault divorce), in money (paper money not backed by gold), in truth (post-truth media), in identity (expressive individualism). The system went through a phase transition (a sudden change in state) from ordered to disordered. Disorder (entropy) was maximized.

**The Amish (always):** The external field (h) was kept in place. The Ordnung (the unwritten code of rules) is the field. It is not a law forced from outside — it is an unwritten code maintained by agreement within each church district of 25-35 families. Technologies are judged by whether they strengthen or weaken the field. Those that weaken it are rejected. Those that strengthen or are neutral are adopted.

The key insight is not that the Amish reject modern life. **They do not.** They use diesel generators, pneumatic tools, LED lighting, solar panels, GPS for farming, and increasingly, cell phones. What they reject is **any technology that shifts attention from the community to the individual**.

The Amish are engineering their own coherence field (the force that keeps them together). They are doing it with the same physics that rules every other system: strong binding forces (mutual aid, living close together, shared language), well-defined boundaries (Ordnung, plain dress, careful technology choices), and active rejection of disorder sources (media saturation, financial debt, individual freedom as the highest value).

The data says this works. Not perfectly. Not without cost. **But it works.**

## The Meta-Rule: Deliberate Adoption

The Amish don't reject technology automatically. They **evaluate** it.

When a new technology appears:

1. The bishop and community discuss it
2. They ask: "What will this do to our community?"
3. They test it in limited settings
4. They decide together to allow or forbid it

This process can take **years**.

#### Contrast with mainstream adoption:

- New iPhone released → bought within weeks
- New social platform → adopted without discussion
- No group thinking about consequences

The speed of adoption determines how deeply you think about it. Slow down.

## What This Means For Us

We cannot become Amish. Their system requires a shared faith, multi-generational commitment, living close together, and economic self-sufficiency.

But we can take the principles:

- **Scale limits:** Keep communities small enough for real relationships
- **Introduce friction:** Make some things deliberately inconvenient
- **Protect the home:** Control what enters your information sanctuary
- **Engineer interdependence:** Create structures where people need each other
- **Shared information:** Build common knowledge bases
- **Evaluate technology together:** Ask "what will this do to us?" before adopting
- **Own your tools:** Avoid systems that own you

**Final Dictum:**

The Amish aren't relics. They're a control group.

While we optimized for individual freedom, they optimized for group health.

**Their population is doubling. Ours is fracturing.**

Maybe friction isn't the enemy. Maybe having no friction is.

### The Lesson

Social health didn't collapse because modern life made it impossible.

Social health collapsed because we made choices — each one made sense for the individual, but together they were disastrous — that destroyed it.

Each technology adopted without evaluation. Each information source allowed to compete. Each ritual abandoned as "outdated." Each boundary dissolved as "discriminatory." Each institution centralized for "efficiency." Each intergenerational bond cut for "independence." Each community scaled up for "opportunity."

The Amish made different choices. Same environment. Different outcomes.

The decline was not inevitable. We chose it.
