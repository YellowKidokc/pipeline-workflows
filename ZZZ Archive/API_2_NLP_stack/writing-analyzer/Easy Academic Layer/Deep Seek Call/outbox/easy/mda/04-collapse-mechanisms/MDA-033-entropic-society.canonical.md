# I. The Technology of Human Will

## A Clear Definition

To understand what's happening to American society, we need a definition of technology that goes beyond just "tools." Technology, in this paper, is not just hardware or software. It is a cultural force—an **exteriorization of human will** (the outward expression of what humans want). It's the main way modern society organizes itself and holds together. Because of this, the direction technology takes reflects big changes in what people value, especially the urge to maximize and control everything.

Technology is not merely hardware or software. It is the exteriorization of human will—and the will it externalizes is the will to maximize, to control, to extract.

### A. Technology as What We Want, Made Real

Technology shapes culture, so we have to study it like we study philosophy or history. If philosophy asks how things relate to each other, then the huge size and complexity of technology systems force us to look at their moral impact. We stop asking "Does this tool work well?" and start asking "What does building and using this system do to people?"

This way of thinking requires us to admit that technology is never neutral—it always carries values. Technical systems are built around goals like efficiency and power. When humans use technology to interact with the world, they are automatically taking a maximizing, controlling approach. When this approach is applied everywhere, it creates a "technological way of life." The drop in social connection and mental health that we see may be the disastrous result of technology driven by these unchecked values.

### B. Can We Control Technology?

A **Critical Theory of technology** (a way of analyzing technology that asks who benefits and who loses) gives us the lens to look at this decay. This view agrees with one side that says technology can be controlled, but also agrees with another side that says technology always carries values. This sounds like a contradiction, but it means: yes, technology is built with values like efficiency, but we can tame it by letting more people have a say in how it's designed and used.

The main idea here is that the problem isn't technology itself. The problem is that our institutions have failed to design systems that let humans control technological development. In the past, people thought the economy was an unstoppable force with its own laws. Then society said "no" and created democratic institutions to influence the economy. We need to do the same thing with technology. If technology expresses the will to "maximize and control," and we don't balance that with democratic oversight, then the exteriorization of human will produces results—like the breakdown of **Relational Coherence** (cooperation and trust between people) and the rise of **Psychological Entropy** (internal mental breakdown)—that put system power above human well-being.

### C. Three Phases of Technology (1950–2025)

We can organize the story of moral decline around three phases of technology in America, defined by how people used it and how widespread it was.

**Phase 1: Mass Mediation (1950–1985)**

This phase was defined by one-way, passive, shared technology use. The main technologies were broadcast TV, radio, and landline phones. TV reached almost every home, completely changing how attention was managed and how families spent time together. This period created centralized stories that everyone heard and introduced media that demanded high passive attention, setting the stage for later changes in how people connected.

**Phase 2: Individualized Access (1985–2005)**

This phase moved toward personal computers and active information searching. The key developments were the Personal Computer (PC) and dial-up/early broadband internet. By early 2000, about half of U.S. adults were using the internet. This decentralized information, giving individuals the power to find things themselves but also demanding new skills for sorting through information. Cell phone use also took off, with two-thirds of Americans owning one by 2005.

**Phase 3: Perpetual Connectivity (2005–Present)**

This phase is defined by mobile devices, fast internet, and social platforms woven into daily life. Today, **96% of U.S. adults use the internet**—almost everyone. The critical change in this era was the explosion of social media and smartphones after 2005. Facebook grew 1,300% between 2005 and 2010. This technology shifts the mode to continuous, interactive, algorithm-driven engagement. **95% of youth ages 13–17** use social media, and many use it "almost constantly." This high psychological saturation demands constant peer connection and algorithmic attention capture, making it completely different from earlier phases.

# II. The Aesthetic Transition

## From Things That Last to Things That Break

The **Aesthetic of Technological Entropy** (the look and feel of technology that's designed to fall apart) gives us a way to understand how the design philosophy of modern technology mirrors—and maybe even causes—the social and psychological decay we're measuring. This shift represents a fundamental change in what our culture expects from things: do they last, or do they break?

### A. The Beauty of Things That Last (Analog Durability)

The earlier period of manufacturing, roughly matching Phase 1 and the years before it, embraced the look and feel of analog durability. This design philosophy valued high-quality materials, products built to be repaired, and long life. The physical object was meant to absorb the effects of time, gaining value as it aged—the "patina" of use. This encouraged people to invest long-term, repair things, and pass them down through generations. Consumers developed "aesthetic durability" by choosing timeless designs and rejecting fast, disposable trends. This embedded cultural value in physical goods naturally reinforced social stability, supporting behaviors like thrift and repair that are essential for strong Relational Coherence.

### B. The Rise of Things Made to Fail

The critical shift involves accepting an **expendable aesthetic** (things designed to be thrown away) and **planned obsolescence** (products designed with a short life on purpose). This principle says products should have an intentionally short lifespan, treating objects as "short-life toys" or replaceable parts. This shift was recognized early in design, especially in Metabolist architecture, which focused on the "joints" where fast and slow time periods met, highlighting the idea of structural obsolescence. While earlier activist efforts championed reuse as an ecological strategy since the 1960s, consumer technology moved steadily toward disposability.

### C. Decay as a Feature, Not a Bug

**Thermodynamic entropy** (the natural tendency of systems to move toward disorder and decay) is applied here to technology's look and feel. It moves from being a concept about building materials to a functional characteristic of digital systems. In modern, highly specialized consumer electronics, entropy is not a failure—it is an engineered feature. This is the **commodification of systemic decay** (turning breakdown into something you can sell). Digital entropy shows up through closed hardware ecosystems (you can't open the device), non-replaceable parts, and—crucially—software incompatibility and security flaws that can never be patched. A device that physically works fine is made useless by digital decay, ensuring you keep buying new ones.

The structural implication is deep: if technological artifacts are the exteriorization of human intent, then the shift toward designed decay shows a societal and corporate will that values maximum extraction (economic efficiency and power) over cultural permanence and stability. This technological entropy creates a negative feedback loop: it undermines the value of durability, making society accept transience and systemic breakdown as normal. This engineered instability models the moral decline we're measuring: decay in Relational Coherence (unstable social bonds), decay in Informational Integrity (rapid turnover of facts, misinformation), and the collapse into Psychological Entropy (internal systemic breakdown).

In modern consumer electronics, entropy is not a failure—it is an engineered feature. The commodification of systemic decay ensures perpetual consumption, and structurally models the moral decline being measured.

# III. Longitudinal Data Synthesis

## Technology and Decline (1950–2025)

Mapping the timeline of three categories of moral decline against the three technology phases shows that technology's impact has been **different at different times and in different ways**—not the same across the board.

### A. Relational Coherence: The Breaking of Social Bonds

Relational Coherence measures how strong and healthy our communities and families are. The data suggests that the foundational decay in this area happened *before* individualized and always-on digital access arrived.

**Decline in Civic Participation**

The withdrawal of citizens from voluntary groups and the unraveling of social connections happened **gradually and steadily after the 1950s and 1960s**. This decline hit many traditionally important organizations, including unions and groups like the PTA. **Social capital theory** (the idea that social networks have value) says this decline is mainly due to **generational replacement** (older generations who were joiners being replaced by younger ones who aren't)—which accounts for about half the decline—and the rise of **television-watching**, which accounts for about 25% of the decline in American social capital.

The critical finding: the most significant decay in Relational Coherence was linked to Phase 1 (Mass Mediation). The shift from active group participation to passive, home-bound consumption fundamentally weakened the civic fabric. Adding to this is the long-term trend toward **structural isolation**: the percentage of U.S. households with only one person rose from 7.7% in 1940 to 27.6% in 2020, showing an accelerating, systemic trend toward living alone that spans all three technology phases.

**Marriage and Isolation**

While divorce rates are often cited as a sign of moral decline, official statistics show that the U.S. divorce rate peaked around 1980 and has generally declined since then. This peak happened during the transition from Phase 1 to Phase 2. The fact that marital instability peaked *before* the mass spread of PCs, the internet, and smartphones suggests that changes in family structure were driven by big social and cultural forces separate from—and earlier than—the digital revolution.

### B. Informational Integrity: The Erosion of Trust

Informational Integrity means public confidence in the institutions that govern us and give us information. It shows a crisis that started long before the digital age, which then allowed the fragmentation of the Perpetual Connectivity era.

**Trust in Government and Media**

Public trust in the federal government has suffered a deep, decades-long collapse. In 1958, about three-quarters of Americans trusted the government to do what's right. But trust began eroding during the 1960s, as the Vietnam War escalated, and kept falling through the 1970s with the Watergate scandal and economic problems. Since 2007, confidence in the government has not risen above 30%. A similar trend shows up in media: confidence in news reporting fell from 68–72% in the 1970s to 53% by 1997.

**Disillusionment vs. Fragmentation**

The data shows that the crisis of Informational Integrity was started and locked in during Phase 1 (Mass Mediation) and the early part of Phase 2, driven mainly by centralized communication of political failure and war. Phase 1 generated **disillusionment**—the loss of faith in centralized authorities—decades before social media existed. Phase 3 (Perpetual Connectivity) then accelerated **fragmentation**, providing an unprecedented platform for decentralized, often dubious information, which thrives in an environment where foundational trust in centralized sources has already been destroyed.

### C. Psychological Entropy: Internal Breakdown

Psychological Entropy is the internal systemic breakdown shown by acute mental distress. It shows a strong, time-specific link to the saturation of Perpetual Connectivity technology (Phase 3).

**The Sharp Rise of Mental Distress**

The most alarming long-term trend is the surge in mental health problems, especially among youth. The prevalence of depression increased significantly from **8.2% in 2013–2014 to 13.1% in 2021–2023**. Current data shows that 16.0% of adolescents ages 12–17 have diagnosed anxiety, and 8.7% have diagnosed depression. Loneliness has been identified by the U.S. Surgeon General as a public health epidemic. A majority of adults report feeling isolated (54%) or lacking companionship (50%) often or some of the time.

**How Technology Causes This**

While suicide rates went up and down over the second half of the 20th century, the current crisis is marked by the timing: the mental distress spike lines up exactly with the saturation point of social media adoption. Experimental studies establish a **causal link** (not just a correlation, but a proven cause-and-effect relationship) between limited use of platforms like Facebook, Snapchat, and Instagram and improved well-being.

The evidence points to a biological mechanism behind Psychological Entropy. Excessive and problematic social media use—defined by adolescents spending an average of 3.5 hours per day on these platforms—is consistently linked to poor sleep quality, reduced sleep duration, and depression. Furthermore, frequent social media use may be linked to distinct changes in the developing brain's amygdala (which handles emotional learning) and the prefrontal cortex (which handles impulse control and emotional regulation). These changes increase sensitivity to social rewards and punishments, essentially engineering a perpetually unstable internal state that's vulnerable to stress and feelings of exclusion. The technology consumes the cognitive resources needed for stability, creating systemic internal disorder.

# IV. Correlation Analysis

## Linking Entropy and Decline

The analysis confirms that technological entropy is significantly linked to moral decline, but the nature of that link depends heavily on which phase of technology adoption we're looking at.

**Phase 1: Mass Mediation and the Foundation of Decay**

The strong statistical link between the rise of passive broadcast media (Phase 1) and the start of large-scale social decay shows that the systemic weaknesses in American moral capital were laid decades ago. The mechanism here is substitution: television functionally replaced active group participation with isolated, home-based leisure, leading to the gradual "unraveling" of social capital. At the same time, the broadcast nature of this medium centralized the spread of political failures (Vietnam, Watergate), creating widespread and lasting disillusionment that destroyed Informational Integrity. This period started the cultural acceptance of technology-mediated, isolated consumption, structurally weakening society before the internet arrived.

**Phase 2: Individualized Access and Structural Dispersion**

Phase 2 (PC and early internet access) seems to function less as a primary cause of moral decline and more as a structural accelerant of existing trends. It provided tools for individualized retreat, contributing to the accelerating trend toward one-person households. While it introduced information dispersion, the mode of interaction remained active (you had to go find things), keeping the pressures of constant social comparison and engineered addiction (the hallmarks of Psychological Entropy) relatively low. The decline in Informational Integrity continued, but the core crisis of trust was already locked in during Phase 1.

**Phase 3: Perpetual Connectivity and Acute Psychological Entropy**

Phase 3 shows the highest and most acute link to decline, specifically regarding internal psychological stability. The mass adoption of smartphones and social media (post-2013) corresponds precisely with the sharp, measurable spike in youth anxiety and depression. The causal evidence confirms that the technology is not merely present alongside the decay but actively diminishes well-being when used excessively.

The crucial dynamic identified is the **entropic feedback loop**: the aesthetic of digital entropy (designed instability and transience) feeds directly into psychological entropy. The algorithmic goal of maximizing attention capture and engagement leads to a technological environment that interferes with the biological mechanisms of emotional regulation and sleep. Users are driven toward perpetual cognitive and emotional instability (FOMO, comparison stress), mirroring the disposable and unmaintainable nature of the hardware and software itself. The efficiency goal of the technical system (maximum extraction of attention/data) directly causes the internal systemic breakdown (Psychological Entropy).

The efficiency goal of the technical system—maximum extraction of attention and data—directly causes the internal systemic breakdown that is Psychological Entropy. The machine optimizes. The human disintegrates.

# V. Falsification and Alternative Causal Pathways

To avoid the oversimplified conclusion that technology alone causes everything, we need to test for other factors that may have sped up or started the decline independently of technology. The evidence suggests that technology often reflects or speeds up existing societal weaknesses.

### A. The Non-Deterministic Counter-Thesis

Many critical analyses emphasize that technology mainly speeds up or provides a platform for pre-existing societal tensions and trends. For example, technology enables the gig economy, but it's the pre-existing economic insecurity that makes workers rely on insecure, technology-mediated labor. The societal harms we see are not necessarily the inevitable outcome of technological progress, but rather the result of society failing to put adequate civic or institutional structures in place to manage the pace of change.

### B. Socioeconomic and Political Drivers of Decline

Non-technological factors provide powerful alternative explanations for the erosion of moral capital:

**Economic Inequality**

Rising economic inequality has been shown to be a critical driver of social breakdown. Inequality creates a competitive economic climate that directly erodes social cohesion. It causes fractured social environments and correlates strongly with increased moralization—the rise in using harsh moral language and judgments against others. If economic pressure structurally weakens social bonds, individuals may retreat into digital spaces for validation and connection, making them highly vulnerable to the psychological harms of constant social comparison and technologically engineered stress. Economic strain acts as a major confounding variable that both starts and magnifies technological harm.

**Political Polarization and Conflict**

The widespread feeling that we're in moral free fall is a point of agreement across political affiliations, despite deep polarization. Societal division is frequently cited as a significant source of stress among adults. These political and ideological conflicts, often rooted in historical, economic, and cultural divisions, substantially predate the mass adoption of Phase 3 technology. Technology, in this view, did not create the division but provided a highly efficient, fragmented, and emotionally charged platform for existing moral conflict to be amplified and weaponized, speeding up the decay of Informational Integrity.

### C. Generational Replacement and Cultural Momentum

Robert Putnam's definitive analysis of civic decline places overwhelming emphasis on **generational replacement**. The replacement of the "long civic generation" (formed by shared experiences of the Great Depression and World War II) by generations with less natural tendency for group involvement accounts for roughly half of the decline in Relational Coherence. This perspective reinforces the finding that the decay of civic bonds was fundamentally a cultural shift—one helped along by Phase 1 technology (TV) but driven mainly by demographic momentum, confirming that the decline in Relational Coherence had deep, non-digital origins.

# VI. Conclusion and Policy Recommendations

The investigation into the Aesthetic of Technological Entropy confirms a deep, time-specific link between technological evolution and the degradation of American moral capital from 1950 to the present. The analysis rejects a simple, single-cause story and establishes that technology's impact has been **different at different times and in different ways**.

The **Mass Mediation era (Phase 1)** started the fundamental structural decay, establishing an isolating media consumption model that severely undermined **Relational Coherence** (civic bonds) and generated the systemic political and media failures that destroyed **Informational Integrity** (institutional trust). The **Perpetual Connectivity era (Phase 3)** then exploited these pre-existing social and informational vacuums, introducing an acute, potentially causal crisis of **Psychological Entropy** driven by the biologically destabilizing effects of attention-capture design.

The core finding: the technological imperative of efficiency and maximization, when exteriorized without institutional control, creates an aesthetic of entropic transience (planned obsolescence) that structurally models and enables moral decay, culminating in the engineered instability of the human psyche.

The technological imperative of efficiency and maximization, when exteriorized without institutional control, creates an aesthetic of entropic transience that structurally models and enables moral decay—culminating in the engineered instability of the human psyche.

### A. Recommendations: Toward Technological Democracy

**1. Democratizing Technology Design and Deployment**

Technological systems must be submitted to a more democratic process to make sure their embedded values go beyond maximization and control. This requires mandating design transparency, allowing public scrutiny of algorithmic objectives, and holding technological corporations accountable for the measurable negative side effects (Psychological Entropy) produced by their systems. The goal is to establish institutions capable of directing technological development toward social stability, mirroring the historical democratization of economic control.

**2. Countering Digital Entropy and Planned Obsolescence**

Policy intervention must prioritize permanence over engineered transience. Implementing strict legal frameworks that enforce a "Right-to-Repair" for both hardware and software would directly resist the aesthetic of entropy. By encouraging durability and discouraging planned obsolescence, governance can reinforce cultural values of investment, repair, and long-term stability that underpin Relational Coherence.

**3. Mitigating the Crisis of Psychological Entropy**

Given the acute, demonstrable link between excessive Phase 3 technology use and youth mental distress, immediate and targeted public health interventions are justified. These measures should include mandating default settings that impose age-appropriate usage limits, restrict features that interfere with sleep, and prohibit designs engineered to overstimulate the developing brain's reward centers. Addressing the public health epidemic of loneliness and disconnection requires not only limiting harmful exposure but also prioritizing and funding community-based interventions that emphasize non-mediated social interaction to rebuild the Relational Coherence eroded by preceding technological phases.
