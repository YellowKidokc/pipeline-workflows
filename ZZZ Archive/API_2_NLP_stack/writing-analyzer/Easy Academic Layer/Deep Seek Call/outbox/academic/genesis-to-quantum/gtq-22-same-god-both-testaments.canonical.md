# The Same God in Both Testaments: A Unified Framework for Justice, Mercy, and Free Will Across the Canonical Narrative

## Abstract

The apparent theological discontinuity between the Old and New Testaments—manifest in divergent portrayals of divine character, moral expectation, and judicial severity—has generated four principal explanatory models within Christian apologetics: the atheist critique, the hermeneutical reinterpretation, the sovereignty appeal, and the progressive revelation thesis. Each captures partial truth while failing to hold simultaneously the three constraints that any satisfactory resolution must satisfy: perfect justice, perfect mercy, and the preservation of human free will. This article proposes that these three constraints correspond formally to the three terms of the Coherence Equation, previously developed in this series: justice as the decay term \(S \cdot C\), mercy as the grace input \(G\), and free will as the openness variable \(O\). The equation \(\frac{dC}{dt} = O \cdot G(1-C) - S \cdot C\) is demonstrated to operate across both testaments without relaxation of any term, with the diagnostic phase (Old Testament) emphasizing the \(S \cdot C\) term and the treatment phase (New Testament) foregrounding the \(G\) term, while \(O\) remains nonzero throughout. The framework is tested against the hardest canonical cases—slavery, capital punishment, the conquest of Canaan, and the status of women—and converges on the Cross as the singular event wherein all three constraints are simultaneously visible and operative. The pace of revelation is argued to be constrained not by divine caprice but by the cognitive and cultural receptivity of the human receivers, analogized to pedagogical scaffolding across a fifteen-century curriculum.

## 1. Introduction: The Problem of Perceived Discontinuity

The claim that the God of the Old Testament is identical to the God revealed in Jesus Christ constitutes a foundational tenet of Christian orthodoxy, attested explicitly in both testaments: "For I the Lord do not change" (Malachi 3:6, NRSV) and "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever" (Hebrews 13:8, NRSV). Despite this textual affirmation, the reader who moves from Numbers 31 to John 8 encounters a dissonance that has generated sustained theological crisis since the second-century Marcionite controversy. The honest believer, as the present author has elsewhere noted, does not lose faith but rather loses the capacity to *explain* that faith to the thoughtful inquirer.

The four standard responses to this problem may be characterized as follows:

1. **The atheist critique**: The God of the Old Testament is not morally good; this position is logically consistent but epistemically self-undermining, as it borrows a moral framework that atheistic naturalism cannot ground (cf. Nietzsche, 1887; Mackie, 1977).

2. **The hermeneutical reinterpretation**: The problematic passages are misread; this approach captures the importance of context but establishes an interpretive principle that can be deployed selectively, potentially undermining the passages one wishes to retain.

3. **The sovereignty appeal**: God operates on a moral scale inaccessible to human judgment; this contains genuine insight regarding scale differences (cf. Job 38–41) but, pressed to its limit, evacuates the term "good" of semantic content by making it coextensive with "whatever God does."

4. **The progressive revelation thesis**: God entered a violent world and initiated a trajectory toward moral improvement; this captures the observable canonical trajectory but cannot explain its pace—why fifteen centuries rather than fifteen years?

Each of these responses attempts to resolve the underlying tension by relaxing one of three constraints that must, for a fully satisfactory account, be held simultaneously: perfect justice, perfect mercy, and the preservation of human free will. The present article argues that these three constraints correspond to the three terms of a dynamical equation previously derived in this series, and that the equation holds across both testaments without relaxation of any term.

## 2. The Coherence Equation: Formal Statement and Theological Interpretation

### 2.1 Definition of Variables

The Coherence Equation, introduced in Article 14 of this series (see also Article 21 for empirical validation), is given by:

\[
\frac{dC}{dt} = O \cdot G(1-C) - S \cdot C
\]

where:

- \(C\) represents **coherence**—the degree of alignment between a human soul and the divine nature, normalized to the unit interval \([0,1]\), where \(C=1\) denotes perfect alignment and \(C=0\) denotes total decoherence. \(C\) is dimensionless.
- \(t\) denotes **temporal progression**, measured in units of subjective or narrative time (dimension: time).
- \(O\) represents **openness**—the human volitional variable, dimensionless and bounded below by zero, representing the willingness to receive divine input. \(O\) is a free parameter set by the human agent.
- \(G\) represents **grace**—the negentropic input from the divine source, with dimensions of inverse time \([T^{-1}]\), representing the rate at which God supplies restorative energy to the system.
- \(S\) represents **entropy**—the decay coefficient, also with dimensions of inverse time \([T^{-1}]\), representing the rate at which coherence is lost due to the structural consequences of sin in a fallen universe.

### 2.2 Theological Mapping of Terms

The three terms of the right-hand side map to the three theological constraints as follows:

**The decay term \(S \cdot C\) corresponds to justice.** This is not punitive in the sense of divine retributive emotion but structural in the sense that coherence loss has consequences intrinsic to the system that loses it. The prophetic language of divine "wrath" (Hebrew: *ʾaph*, *ḥēmāh*; Greek: *orgē*) describes the operation of this term in real time on populations that have accumulated decoherence across generations. The justice term is proportional to the current coherence state: the more aligned a system is, the more severely it is affected by decoherence, analogous to the relationship between a precision instrument and the damage caused by its misalignment.

**The grace term \(G\) corresponds to mercy.** Grace is the negentropic input without which the equation reduces to pure exponential decay:

\[
\frac{dC}{dt} = -S \cdot C \quad \Rightarrow \quad C(t) = C_0 e^{-St}
\]

The system, left to itself, asymptotically approaches zero coherence. The presence of \(G\) is therefore necessary for any positive trajectory. The Old Testament witness to survival—Noah and his family (Genesis 6–9), Rahab and her household (Joshua 2, 6), the remnant after the exile (Ezra 9:8–15), the preservation of the Abrahamic line through famine, slavery, and apostasy—constitutes evidence that the \(G\) term was never withdrawn. The New Testament does not introduce grace but unveils what had been operative throughout.

**The openness term \(O\) corresponds to free will.** The growth term is multiplicative: \(O \cdot G \cdot (1-C)\). Both \(O\) and \(G\) must be nonzero for growth to occur. God supplies \(G\); the human agent supplies \(O\). Neither alone is sufficient. Setting \(O = 0\) reduces salvation to divine coercion (puppetry); setting \(G = 0\) reduces it to a self-help project that the spiritually dead cannot initiate (cf. Ephesians 2:1–5).

### 2.3 Simultaneity Constraint

The central claim of this article is that all three terms must be nonzero simultaneously for the equation to describe the divine-human relationship as revealed in Scripture. The four standard answers each relax one term:

- The atheist critique effectively denies the \(G\) term (mercy is illusory).
- The hermeneutical reinterpretation relaxes the \(S \cdot C\) term (justice is reinterpreted away).
- The sovereignty appeal relaxes the constraint that all three terms be simultaneously intelligible to human reason.
- The progressive revelation thesis, while preserving the trajectory, cannot account for the pace without an account of the \(O\) term's constraints on the rate of \(G\)'s introduction.

## 3. Historical Context: The World of 1400 BCE

Before testing the framework against the hardest canonical cases, it is necessary to establish the historical conditions into which the Mosaic covenant was introduced. The following reconstruction is based on archaeological and textual evidence from the Late Bronze Age southern Levant (ca. 1550–1200 BCE).

### 3.1 Demographic and Health Conditions

Infant and child mortality rates in pre-modern agrarian societies are estimated at 30–50% before age five (Volk & Atkinson, 2013). In the Late Bronze Age Levant, the absence of germ theory, antibiotics, anesthesia, and surgical hygiene meant that infectious disease accounted for the majority of deaths. Paleopathological evidence from sites such as Tell el-Dab'a and Megiddo indicates endemic malaria, leishmaniasis, and intestinal parasites (Zias, 1991; Mitchell, 2017). The average life expectancy at birth was approximately 25–30 years (Angel, 1972).

### 3.2 Religious and Judicial Context

The religious environment of Canaan was characterized by polytheistic systems in which divine beings were understood as capricious, localized, and requiring propitiation through sacrifice—including, in the case of the god Molech (or Milcom), child sacrifice (2 Kings 23:10; Jeremiah 32:35). Archaeological excavations at Carthage have recovered approximately 20,000 urns containing cremated infant remains from the tophet (Stager & Wolff, 1984). Plutarch (*De Superstitione* 171C–D) describes the bronze statue of Cronus (interpreted as Ba'al Hammon) with sloped hands onto which infants were placed, rolling into a fire pit below. The Hebrew term *toph* (drum) and the site name *tophet* are etymologically linked to the practice of drumming to drown out the screams of the victims (cf. Isaiah 30:33).

The default justice system of the region was blood feud and clan retaliation, as attested in the Amarna letters (EA 287–290) and the Code of Hammurabi (§§196–214). There was no centralized judicial authority, no police force, and no concept of *mens rea* (criminal intent) as distinct from act.

### 3.3 The Pedagogical Challenge

The author's thought experiment—transporting a modern Western observer to a Late Bronze Age village—serves to make vivid the following claim: the moral and conceptual vocabulary required to receive the Sermon on the Mount did not exist in 1400 BCE. The curriculum of the Old Testament is therefore not arbitrary in its pace but constrained by the cognitive and cultural receptivity of its audience. This is the \(O\) term operating at the population level: the openness of the receivers sets the maximum rate at which \(G\) can be introduced without causing cognitive or cultural collapse.

## 4. Testing the Framework: The Hardest Cases

### 4.1 Slavery

The Mosaic law regulates slavery; it does not abolish it. From the perspective of the Late Bronze Age, however, "slavery" (Hebrew: *ʿebed*) functioned as a form of debt servitude and social safety net—the only institution that prevented starvation for the destitute (Exodus 21:2–6; Leviticus 25:39–55). The framework's claim is that the regulation itself constitutes the \(G\) term operating under the constraint that the \(O\) term cannot be set to zero (i.e., God cannot coerce the entire economic system into abolition overnight without overriding human freedom at a structural level).

The Mosaic provisions represent a floor raised above the surrounding norms:

- Mandatory release in the seventh year (Exodus 21:2; Deuteronomy 15:12)
- Freed servants to be sent away "furnished liberally" from the flock, threshing floor, and wine press (Deuteronomy 15:13–14)
- Prohibition on returning runaway slaves (Deuteronomy 23:15–16)—a provision with no parallel in ancient Near Eastern law
- Manumission for a single lost tooth (Exodus 21:26–27)—an unprecedented protection
- Universal Sabbath rest extending to slaves and resident aliens (Exodus 20:10; Deuteronomy 5:14)
- Female inheritance rights established through Zelophehad's daughters (Numbers 27:1–11)

The trajectory is then raised across the canonical timeline until Galatians 3:28 declares that "there is no longer slave or free" in Christ. The floor was set, then raised, then the theological basis for the institution itself was removed. No regression below an established floor occurs; the trajectory is monotonic.

### 4.2 Capital Punishment (Stoning)

The Mosaic law prescribes death by stoning for certain offenses (e.g., Leviticus 20:2, 10, 27; Deuteronomy 13:6–11; 21:18–21). However, the prescription and the execution are distinct. The procedural safeguards introduced by Mosaic law were unprecedented:

- Capital cases required two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 17:6; 19:15)
- Circumstantial evidence was inadmissible
- False testimony in a capital case carried the same penalty as the alleged crime (Deuteronomy 19:16–21)
- The concept of *mens rea* was introduced through the distinction between murder and manslaughter, with Cities of Refuge established for the latter (Numbers 35:9–34; Deuteronomy 19:1–13)

The Mishnah (*Makkot* 1:10) records that a Sanhedrin that executed even one person in seventy years was called a "bloody court." This indicates that the procedural safeguards effectively functioned to prevent execution in most cases. The \(S \cdot C\) term (justice) is present in the prescription; the \(G\) term (mercy) is present in the procedural barriers; the \(O\) term (free will) is preserved in the requirement for witnesses who must voluntarily come forward.

### 4.3 The Conquest of Canaan

This is the hardest case. The conquest narrative (Joshua 6–12) describes the divinely commanded destruction of Canaanite cities, including the *ḥērem* (ban) that involved the killing of entire populations. The framework's analysis proceeds through four considerations:

**Justice (\(S \cdot C\)):** The Canaanite religious practices, including child sacrifice and cultic prostitution, represent accumulated decoherence across centuries. The \(S \cdot C\) term operates: coherence loss has consequences.

**Mercy (\(G\)):** Genesis 15:16 explicitly states that the conquest would wait until "the iniquity of the Amorites is complete"—a period of four hundred years. This constitutes the \(G\) term operating as divine patience, providing time for repentance that the text indicates was not taken.

**Free will (\(O\)):** The Canaanite populations chose their religious and moral practices across four centuries. The \(O\) term is preserved: God does not override their freedom to choose, even when the choice leads to destruction.

**Signal chain preservation:** There is a fourth consideration that emerges from the framework's logic. If Israel adopted Canaanite religious practices, the covenant signal—the vehicle through which the knowledge of the true God would be transmitted to the entire species—would be corrupted and lost. The conquest, on this account, is not God's preferred option but the option that remained after four centuries of mercy had been exhausted and the signal chain was at risk of extinction.

The author acknowledges that this analysis does not make the suffering smaller. It makes it *legible*—that is, it provides a framework within which the action can be understood as holding all three constraints simultaneously rather than relaxing any of them. The confidence level for this particular application is rated MEDIUM, as it represents the most contestable move in the article (see §7, Kill Conditions).

### 4.4 The Status of Women

Mosaic protections for women exceeded every surrounding culture by a wide margin. The following provisions are attested:

- Female inheritance rights (Numbers 27:1–11)—no parallel in ancient Near Eastern law
- Divorce protections (Deuteronomy 24:1–4)
- Rape laws punishing the perpetrator rather than the victim (Deuteronomy 22:25–29)
- Protections for female captives taken as wives (Deuteronomy 21:10–14)
- The inclusion of women in covenant renewal ceremonies (Deuteronomy 29:10–12)
- The requirement that female slaves be released in the seventh year (Deuteronomy 15:12)

The floor was set above the surrounding norm and raised across millennia until Galatians 3:28 declares the abolition of gender hierarchy in Christ. The trajectory is monotonic; no regression below an established floor is attested.

## 5. The Pace of Revelation: A Pedagogical Account

The progressive revelation thesis correctly identifies a trajectory but cannot explain its pace. The Coherence Equation provides the missing explanation: the pace is constrained by the \(O\) term at the population level.

### 5.1 The Analogy of Mathematical Pedagogy

The author employs the following analogy: teaching calculus to a four-year-old requires beginning with counting, then addition, subtraction, multiplication, fractions, variables, and functions, before derivatives can be introduced. The pace is set not by the teacher's knowledge but by the student's receptivity.

The curriculum of the Old Testament is analogously structured. Before the concept of sacrificial, covenantal, enemy-loving love can be taught, the student must understand:

- What *good* and *evil* are (Genesis 3—the fall and the knowledge of good and evil)
- What *neighbor* means (Leviticus 19:18, 34)
- What *self* means (the identity-forming narratives of the patriarchs)
- What *God* is (the long curriculum on divine uniqueness: "I am the Lord, and there is no other"—Isaiah 45:5)

The world of 1400 BCE was populated by small, capricious, demanding deities who behaved nothing like the God who was introducing Himself. Before love could be taught, centuries had to be spent on a single curriculum item: "I am NOT like those other gods." This is the function of the Old Testament—the curriculum on what God is *not*.

### 5.2 The Graduation Speech

When Jesus summarizes the entire law in two commandments—love God and love neighbor (Matthew 22:37–40; Mark 12:29–31)—He is delivering the graduation speech for a curriculum that required fifteen centuries. The vocabulary had finally been built. The conceptual scaffolding was in place. The species was ready for the treatment phase.

## 6. The Convergence Point: The Cross

There is one event in all of history wherein the three constraints are simultaneously visible in a single act: the crucifixion of Jesus Christ.

**Justice (\(S \cdot C\)):** The accumulated decoherence of the species must be addressed. The cost is not symbolic; the thermodynamic equivalent of erasing every sin ever committed is finite, large, and real. It is paid by the only Person whose "energy budget" is infinite. The \(S \cdot C\) term is balanced to zero by an external input that absorbs the entire accumulated load.

**Mercy (\(G\)):** The Person who pays is the Person who was offended. The grace term is not separate from the justice term in this event—they are the same act, performed by the same Person, simultaneously. God absorbs the cost of His own justice rather than charging it to the offenders (cf. 2 Corinthians 5:19; Romans 3:25–26).

**Free will (\(O\)):** The Cross does not impose itself on anyone. The door opens from inside. The \(O\) variable remains nonzero because if it were set to zero, the entire equation would collapse into divine coercion. The offer must be received (John 1:12; Revelation 3:20).

Formally, the convergence may be expressed as:

\[
\sum_{i} (S \cdot C_i)_{\text{species}} \xrightarrow{\text{Cross}} 0, \quad O \geq \epsilon > 0, \quad G \to \max
\]

where the summation is over all human agents across history, and \(\epsilon\) represents a minimal positive threshold for free will preservation.

## 7. Falsification Criteria

The framework is held to explicit falsification standards, as follows:

### 7.1 Load-Bearing Claims

**Claim 1:** The framework can show justice, mercy, and free will operating simultaneously on any single hard passage without relaxing any of them.

**Kill condition:** If any one term must be sacrificed to make the others fit, the unified-author claim collapses to one of the four standard answers.

**Status:** Tested on slavery, stoning, conquest, and the status of women. Last checked: 2026-05-05.

**Claim 2:** The trajectory of moral progress in the canon never reverses.

**Kill condition:** If there is a passage where God endorses a regression below an already-established floor (e.g., re-permitting slavery after raising the floor), the curriculum claim fails.

**Status:** No counter-example identified. Last checked: 2026-05-05.

### 7.2 Suggestive Claim (Needs Further Scrutiny)

**Claim 3:** The conquest of Canaan fits the three-constraint framework.

**Kill condition:** If the action can be shown to violate the framework rather than resolve it—if it does not fit any combination of \(S\), \(G\), \(O\) that holds all three terms.

**Status:** Pending external scrutiny. Confidence: MEDIUM.

### 7.3 Destructive Test

**Claim 4:** The Cross shows all three constraints simultaneously.

**Kill condition:** If any one of justice, mercy, or free will is absent or compromised at the convergence event, the framework's central thesis collapses at framework level.

**Status:** Open. Severity: FRAMEWORK-LEVEL.

## 8. Conclusion

The apparent discontinuity between the Old and New Testaments dissolves when justice (\(S \cdot C\)), mercy (\(G\)), and free will (\(O\)) are recognized as three terms in one equation rather than three values to be traded off against each other. The Person behind both testaments is one Person, parenting a species across the longest curriculum in recorded history, on the only timescale that did not break the receivers. The pace was set by the world. The framework holds all three constraints simultaneously and converges on the Cross, where they become visible as one act performed by one Person.

The Old Testament and the New Testament are not two religions stitched together. They are the diagnostic phase and the treatment phase of one continuous course of care. In the diagnostic phase, the \(S \cdot C\) term is loud in the narrative because \(S\) must be named before it can be addressed. In the treatment phase, the \(G\) term comes forward because \(G\) is what the diagnosed patient needs to receive. A patient who has not been told they have cancer cannot meaningfully accept chemotherapy. The diagnosis must land before the treatment can be received.

Every hard Old Testament passage is the logical consequence of a God who refuses to drop justice, refuses to withhold mercy, and refuses to override freedom—all at the same time, for fifteen centuries, while parenting a species that could not yet understand the word *love*.
