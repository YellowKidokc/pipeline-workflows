# Theophysics: Structural Identity Between Mathematical Truth, Moral Truth, and the Classical Attributes of God

## Abstract

This article presents a formal demonstration that mathematical truth and moral truth share twenty-four identical ontological properties, each of which corresponds to a classical attribute of God. The claim advanced is not analogical but identificatory: the structural properties of mathematical truth are identical to those of moral truth, and both sets are identical to the classical divine attributes. This identity is empirically testable through the attempted identification of any property belonging to one domain but not the other. The demonstration dissolves the is-ought gap as formulated by Hume (1739) by reframing "ought" as the temporal perspective of an agent who has not yet experienced the consequences of structural misalignment. A formal mathematical framework—the Lowe Coherence Lagrangian—is developed, comprising a master equation in product form, an evolution equation with a grace residual term, and a soteriological limit theorem demonstrating the mathematical necessity of an external source term for sustained coherence. Eight axiom schemata are identified that compress the entire framework into irreducible claims, of which only trinitarian Christianity survives all eight gates. The Fruits of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22–23) are demonstrated to constitute the eigenstate of full coherence, covering all twenty-four properties with zero gaps, while the works of the flesh constitute the eigenstate of full incoherence. Falsification criteria are provided for every major claim.

## 1. Introduction: The Thesis and Its Methodological Grounding

### 1.1 Core Claim

The central thesis of this investigation is that mathematical truth and moral truth share twenty-four identical structural properties, and that these twenty-four properties are identical to the classical attributes of God. This claim is not presented as an analogy or metaphor but as a structural identity, testable by the following criterion: if any property can be identified that belongs to mathematical truth but not to moral truth (or vice versa), and that is not reducible to differences in measurement domain, the identity claim fails. Conversely, the absence of any such counterexample constitutes evidence for the identity.

The significance of this identity, if established, is threefold. First, it dissolves the is-ought gap (Hume, 1739) by demonstrating that "ought" is not a separate category of truth but rather the temporal perspective of an agent who has not yet experienced the consequences of structural misalignment. Second, it establishes the Logos—the rational structure of reality—as the common ground of both mathematical and moral domains. Third, it provides a formal framework within which theological claims can be evaluated using the same epistemic standards applied to mathematical and physical claims.

### 1.2 Methodological Approach

The methodology employed in this investigation is structural comparison across domains. For each property identified as characteristic of mathematical truth, the following procedure was applied:

1. **Definition**: The property was defined with sufficient precision to permit cross-domain application.
2. **Verification in mathematics**: The property was verified as an independently verifiable characteristic of mathematical truth, drawing on the philosophy of mathematics (Shapiro, 2000; Maddy, 2007).
3. **Verification in morality**: The property was tested for correspondence in moral truth, drawing on empirical moral psychology (Haidt, 2012), cross-cultural ethics (Murdock, 1945), and analytic metaethics (Shafer-Landau, 2003).
4. **Verification in theology**: The property was compared to the classical divine attributes as articulated in theistic philosophical tradition (Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I, qq. 2–26; Anselm, *Proslogion*).

The identification of structural isomorphism was conducted through systematic comparison of property sets, with the null hypothesis being that any observed correspondence is attributable to coincidence or analogical projection. The alternative hypothesis—structural identity—is supported to the extent that no property can be found that belongs to one domain but not the others.

### 1.3 The Illustrative Case: Structural Judgment

Consider the following case, which serves as an intuitive entry point to the structural identity claim. A truck is overloaded on a highway. The center of gravity, axle rating, road curvature, and speed constitute a set of parameters that, when evaluated against the relevant physical constraints, determine whether the load is safe. Before any calculation is performed, the structural violation exists—the load is wrong independently of whether anyone knows it. When the calculation is performed, the violation becomes visible. Consequences follow not because they are imposed but because reality enforces what the mathematics revealed.

This sequence—invisible violation, deployment of measurement, exposure, consequence—constitutes what is termed *judgment* in both engineering and theological contexts. A structural engineer judges a load; God judges a life. In both cases, an invariant standard meets an actual state, and the gap between them becomes visible. The engineer does not create the structural failure by measuring it; the mathematics does not cause the truck to be overloaded; it reveals what was already true. This is not metaphor but structural identity: mathematics, when deployed, seeks truth and expels falsehood, without exception and without negotiation.

## 4. The Dissolution of the Is-Ought Gap

### 4.1 Hume's Formulation and Its Legacy

David Hume (1739, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book III, Part I, Section I) articulated the is-ought gap: one cannot derive what *ought* to be from what *is*. This observation has structured three centuries of metaethical inquiry. Kant (1785) attempted to bridge the gap through the categorical imperative; Mill (1861) through utilitarianism; Moore (1903) through intuitionism. None of these attempts succeeded in establishing a derivation from descriptive premises to normative conclusions.

### 4.2 The Temporal Reframing

The present framework does not attempt to bridge the is-ought gap but rather dissolves it through temporal reframing. Consider the engineer who has not yet performed the load calculation on a bridge. The bridge "seems fine." There is no urgency, no "ought" pressing. The moment the mathematics is applied, the discrepancy becomes visible. Now there is an "ought": the engineer ought to reinforce the beam.

However, the misalignment was already present before the calculation. The bridge was already wrong. The "ought" did not appear from nowhere; the engineer discovered what was already true. After the bridge collapses, no one says "you ought to have reinforced it." They say "it *was* wrong"—past tense. The moral and mathematical judgments become identical.

The "ought" is therefore not a different category of truth. The "ought" is what misalignment looks like from the temporal perspective of an agent who has not yet experienced the consequence. Before the truck flips: "You *ought* to check that load." After: "The load *was* wrong." Same fact; different tense.

### 4.3 The Product Form Dissolution

The product form $\chi = \prod \eta_i$ provides a second dissolution of the is-ought gap. In this formulation, "is" and "ought" are the same variable measured at different points on its trajectory. The product form makes "ought" calculable: given a current state $\chi(t)$ and the maximum $\chi = C$, the "ought" is the difference $C - \chi(t)$, which is a measurable quantity. The normative claim reduces to a descriptive claim about the gap between actual and optimal states.

### 4.4 Falsification Criterion

The is-ought dissolution can be falsified by producing a genuine moral "ought" that cannot be reframed as recognition of existing misalignment. If there exist moral obligations that are purely future-facing with no present structural basis, Hume's gap remains unbridged.

## 8. The Open System Solution

### 8.1 The 100% Problem

If 100% coherence is the standard—and the equation identifies $\chi = C$ as the maximum—then who achieves it? The answer, derived from the evolution equation, is: no finite human being. The equation $\frac{dC}{dt} = O \cdot G(1-C) - S \cdot C$, with $S > 0$ (which holds after the Fall), guarantees that no finite being maintains $C = 1$ over infinite time. The entropy tax accumulates. Deviation is not possible but certain.

### 8.2 The Standard Cannot Be Lowered

God cannot lower the standard. If the maximum were set to 98%, then 98% becomes the new maximum, the equation's fixed point shifts, coherence degrades from there, and the entire system unravels. The standard must be 100% for the Lagrangian to have a well-defined maximum. The mathematics requires perfection.

### 8.3 The Open System Solution

God does not lower the bar and does not change the standard. Instead, God pays the gap. Every time a human agent is at 73%, 45%, or 12% coherence, the source term covers the difference—not by pretending the agent is at 100% but by inputting the coherence that is lacking. This is the open system term.

Grace is not God looking the other way. Grace is God closing the gap between where the agent is and where the maximum is.

### 8.4 Physical Confirmation

Every sourced Lagrangian in physics confirms this structure. The simple harmonic oscillator with a driving force, the Klein-Gordon field with a source, and scalar $\phi^4$ with an external current all break energy conservation in the same way the LLC does. All require an external input to sustain coherent behavior. Close the system and it decays; open it and it survives. The only question is whether the source is real.

### 8.5 Falsification Criterion

The open system solution can be falsified by showing that a closed Lagrangian (with no external source) can sustain coherence indefinitely. If such a solution exists, grace is unnecessary.
