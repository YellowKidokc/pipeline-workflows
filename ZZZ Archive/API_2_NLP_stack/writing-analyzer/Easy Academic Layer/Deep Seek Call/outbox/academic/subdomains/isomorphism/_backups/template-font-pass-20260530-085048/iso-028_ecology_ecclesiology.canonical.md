# ISO-028: Structural Isomorphism Between Ecological Systems and Pauline Ecclesiology

## Abstract

This paper presents a formal structural isomorphism between ecological community dynamics and Pauline ecclesiology as articulated in the New Testament corpus. Through systematic comparison of ten correspondence points, we demonstrate that the functional architecture of diverse, interdependent communities—whether biological or ecclesial—exhibits mathematically analogous structural properties. The Shannon Diversity Index, species-area relationships, trophic cascade dynamics, and resilience-diversity correlations from ecology find direct analogues in gift diversity distributions, congregation-size functional relationships, leadership failure propagation, and community resilience patterns within Pauline ecclesiology. This isomorphism operates at the level of community topology rather than material substrate, establishing that the Apostle Paul's body metaphor (1 Corinthians 12; Romans 12; Ephesians 4) describes structural requirements for community function that parallel formal ecological principles developed approximately nineteen centuries later. The mapping yields testable predictions for both domains, including the fragility of gift-monoculture congregations, cascade effects following keystone leadership removal, and predictable maturation sequences in church development. Eight of ten correspondences are assessed as robust, with two acknowledged as suggestive rather than definitive. Bidirectional implications are identified: ecological principles constrain viable ecclesiological models, while Pauline interdependence predictions anticipate ecological discoveries regarding keystone species effects.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Methodological Framework

The present investigation employs structural isomorphism analysis—a method that identifies formal correspondences between the relational architectures of distinct domains without asserting material identity between them. This approach, developed within the broader Theophysics research program (see Axiom Dependencies, §7), proceeds through systematic comparison of mathematical structures, dynamical principles, and empirical predictions across domains.

The isomorphism presented herein was identified through structural comparison of ecological community theory—as formalized in biodiversity science, trophic ecology, and resilience theory—with Pauline ecclesiology as articulated in the New Testament epistles. The method does not presuppose that the Apostle Paul possessed formal ecological knowledge; rather, it demonstrates that the structural dynamics he described through organic metaphor (the body, growth, maturation) exhibit formal properties that map onto later ecological formalisms.

### 1.2 Thesis Statement

We advance the following thesis: The functional architecture of diverse, interdependent communities—whether biological ecosystems or Pauline ecclesial bodies—is governed by isomorphic structural principles. Specifically, (1) functional diversity determines community resilience in both domains; (2) keystone entities (species or leadership functions) exert disproportionate influence on community stability; (3) perturbation propagation follows cascade structures through dependency networks; (4) invasive elements (species or teachings) exhibit characteristic displacement dynamics; (5) community development follows predictable successional sequences; (6) carrying capacity constraints limit community size without structural adaptation; (7) inter-entity relationships range along a mutualism-parasitism continuum; (8) resource cycling (nutrient or knowledge) requires closed-loop processes; (9) boundary zones exhibit distinctive properties; and (10) resilience functions as an integrated measure of community health.

## 2. Domain Definitions and Mathematical Formalisms

### 2.1 Domain A: Ecological Systems

An ecosystem is defined as a community of interacting organisms and their abiotic environment, characterized by functional diversity (multiple species occupying distinct niches), interdependence (species removal effects propagate through trophic networks), and resilience proportional to diversity (Tilman et al., 2006). The mathematical formalisms employed include:

**Shannon Diversity Index (Shannon, 1948):**

$$H' = -\sum_{i=1}^{S} p_i \ln(p_i)$$

where $p_i$ represents the proportional abundance of species $i$ within the community, and $S$ denotes species richness. The index attains maximum value when all species are equally represented ($p_i = 1/S$ for all $i$). $H'$ decreases monotonically under two conditions: (a) when any single species achieves numerical dominance, or (b) when species are lost from the community (reduction in $S$). The index is dimensionless, with units of nats (natural logarithmic units) per individual.

**Species-Area Relationship (MacArthur & Wilson, 1967):**

$$S = cA^z$$

where $S$ = number of species, $A$ = habitat area (units: km²), $c$ is a taxon- and biome-specific constant (units: species·km⁻²ᶻ), and $z$ is a dimensionless scaling exponent. For continental ecosystems, $z \approx 0.25$ (range: 0.15–0.35; Lomolino, 2000). This relationship constitutes one of the most robust empirical patterns in ecology, confirmed across thousands of studies spanning terrestrial, marine, and freshwater biomes.

**Resilience-Diversity Relationship:**

$$R \propto H'$$

where $R$ denotes community resilience—defined as the capacity to absorb disturbance and maintain essential function (Holling, 1973). More diverse systems recover from perturbation more rapidly and more completely than less diverse systems, a relationship confirmed across agricultural, forest, and marine ecosystems (Tilman et al., 2006).

### 2.2 Domain B: Pauline Ecclesiology

A church (ἐκκλησία) is defined, following Pauline usage, as a community of interacting members possessing diverse spiritual gifts (χαρίσματα) within their cultural environment, characterized by functional diversity (multiple gifts filling distinct roles), interdependence (removal of one function cascades through the community), and resilience proportional to diversity. The primary textual foundations are:

- **1 Corinthians 12:14–20:** "For the body does not consist of one member but of many. If the foot should say, 'Because I am not a hand, I do not belong to the body,' that would not make it any less a part of the body... If the whole body were an eye, where would be the sense of hearing?" (NA28: καὶ γὰρ τὸ σῶμα οὐκ ἔστιν ἓν μέλος ἀλλὰ πολλά... εἰ ἦν τὸ σῶμα ὅλον ὀφθαλμός, ποῦ ἡ ἀκοή;)
- **Ephesians 4:11–12:** "And he gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the shepherds and teachers, to equip the saints for the work of ministry, for building up the body of Christ" (NA28: Καὶ αὐτὸς ἔδωκεν τοὺς μὲν ἀποστόλους, τοὺς δὲ προφήτας, τοὺς δὲ εὐαγγελιστάς, τοὺς δὲ ποιμένας καὶ διδασκάλους, πρὸς τὸν καταρτισμὸν τῶν ἁγίων εἰς ἔργον διακονίας, εἰς οἰκοδομὴν τοῦ σώματος τοῦ Χριστοῦ)

The mathematical formalisms developed for Domain B are structurally isomorphic to those of Domain A:

**Gift Diversity Index:**

$$H'_g = -\sum_{i=1}^{G} p_i \ln(p_i)$$

where $p_i$ represents the proportion of members exercising gift $i$ within the congregation, and $G$ denotes the number of distinct functioning gifts. Maximum $H'_g$ occurs when all gifts are equally exercised. $H'_g$ decreases under two conditions: (a) when any single gift achieves functional dominance (e.g., a congregation where only pastoral teaching occurs and all other gifts remain latent), or (b) when gifts are suppressed or unrecognized (reduction in $G$). The index is dimensionless.

**Gift-Size Relationship:**

$$G = cN^z$$

where $G$ = number of distinct functioning gifts, $N$ = congregation size (dimensionless count of members), $c$ is a context-specific constant, and $z$ is a scaling exponent. This predicts a power-law relationship between congregation size and functional gift diversity—an empirically testable proposition. Larger congregations are predicted to support more distinct functional roles, consistent with the ecological species-area relationship.

**Resilience-Gift Diversity Relationship:**

$$R \propto H'_g$$

where $R$ denotes congregational resilience—the capacity to absorb disturbance (leadership transition, doctrinal controversy, cultural pressure, persecution) and maintain essential function. Churches with more diverse functioning gifts are predicted to recover from crisis more rapidly and more completely than gift-monoculture congregations.

## 3. The Isomorphism: Ten Correspondence Points

### 3.1 Biodiversity and Gift Diversity

**Correspondence 1** establishes the foundational parallel between species diversity in ecosystems and gift diversity in ecclesial communities. The Pauline argument in 1 Corinthians 12:14–20 is not merely motivational or hortatory; it describes a structural requirement for community function. The logical structure of Paul's argument—"if the whole body were an eye, where would be the sense of hearing?"—constitutes an implicit recognition that functional monocultures cannot sustain community operations. An ecosystem with one species is not, properly speaking, an ecosystem; analogously, a church with one functioning gift is not, in the Pauline framework, a church.

This correspondence is supported by the mathematical isomorphism between the Shannon Diversity Index applied to species distributions ($H'$) and the Gift Diversity Index applied to charism distributions ($H'_g$). Both indices measure the same structural property: the evenness and richness of functional diversity within a community.

### 3.2 Keystone Species and Apostolic/Pastoral Function

**Correspondence 2** maps the ecological concept of keystone species—organisms whose removal causes disproportionate ecosystem collapse relative to their biomass—onto the Pauline leadership functions identified in Ephesians 4:11–12. The keystone species concept, formalized by Paine (1969) through experimental removal of the starfish *Pisaster ochraceus* from intertidal communities, demonstrates that certain species exert regulatory effects far exceeding their proportional representation.

The Pauline text identifies five specific roles (ἀποστόλους, προφήτας, εὐαγγελιστάς, ποιμένας καὶ διδασκάλους) whose function is "to equip the saints for the work of ministry" (πρὸς τὸν καταρτισμὸν τῶν ἁγίων εἰς ἔργον διακονίας). The term καταρτισμός (equipping/perfecting) carries the connotation of restoring something to its proper condition—analogous to the regulatory function of keystone species in maintaining ecosystem structure.

The prophetic warning in Ezekiel 34 ("Woe to the shepherds of Israel who feed themselves"; LXX: Ὢ οἱ ποιμένες Ἰσραήλ, μὴ βόσκουσιν ἑαυτούς;) describes the ecological consequence of keystone species failure: when shepherds (keystone leaders) prioritize self-feeding over flock care, the entire community structure collapses. This constitutes an explicit recognition, within the biblical text, of cascade effects following leadership failure.

### 3.3 Trophic Cascade and Leadership Failure Propagation

**Correspondence 3** identifies the structural parallel between trophic cascades in ecology and leadership failure propagation in ecclesiology. A trophic cascade occurs when removal of a top predator restructures the entire ecosystem through successive trophic levels. The canonical example is wolf removal from Yellowstone National Park (prior to reintroduction): elk overpopulation led to riparian vegetation destruction, stream erosion, and fundamental ecosystem transformation (Ripple & Beschta, 2012).

The cascade structure is identical in ecclesial contexts: when pastoral leadership fails or is removed, the effect propagates downward through dependency layers. Leadership failure → unchecked false teaching → doctrinal erosion → community fragmentation. The cascade mechanism is the same: removal of a regulatory entity releases lower-level entities from constraint, producing systemic reorganization.

### 3.4 Invasive Species and Heresy

**Correspondence 4** maps the ecological dynamics of invasive species onto the theological dynamics of heresy. Invasive species enter an ecosystem, outcompete native species for resources, and transform the ecosystem's character. Heresy enters a church, displaces sound teaching, and transforms the community's character.

The Pauline text in Galatians 1:6–7 describes this dynamic explicitly: "I am astonished that you are so quickly deserting him who called you in the grace of Christ and are turning to a different gospel—not that there is another one, but there are some who trouble you and want to distort the gospel of Christ" (NA28: Θαυμάζω ὅτι οὕτως ταχέως μετατίθεσθε ἀπὸ τοῦ καλέσαντος ὑμᾶς ἐν χάριτι Χριστοῦ εἰς ἕτερον εὐαγγέλιον, ὃ οὐκ ἔστιν ἄλλο· εἰ μή τινές εἰσιν οἱ ταράσσοντες ὑμᾶς καὶ θέλοντες μεταστρέψαι τὸ εὐαγγέλιον τοῦ Χριστοῦ).

The invasive species dynamic is characterized by: (a) rapid initial growth (invasive advantage), (b) resource competition that displaces native species, and (c) resistance to self-correction—both invasive species and heresies require active removal; neither self-corrects through internal community dynamics.

### 3.5 Ecological Succession and Church Maturation

**Correspondence 5** identifies the parallel between ecological succession—the predictable developmental sequence from pioneer species to climax community—and church maturation from evangelistic planting to established congregation.

Primary succession follows a characteristic trajectory: bare rock → lichens → mosses → grasses → shrubs → forest. Church planting follows an analogous sequence: evangelist → small group → organized congregation → established church with diverse ministries. Pioneer species (lichens, mosses) colonize hostile environments and create conditions for later species. Pioneer leaders (evangelists, church planters) similarly colonize spiritually hostile environments and create conditions for subsequent ministry development.

This correspondence yields a testable prediction: new church plants will exhibit low functional diversity and high vulnerability, while mature churches will exhibit high functional diversity and high resilience. This prediction is consistent with both ecological observation and pastoral experience.

### 3.6 Carrying Capacity and Community Size Limits

**Correspondence 6** maps ecological carrying capacity ($K$)—the maximum population an environment can sustain—onto cognitive and relational constraints on congregation size.

Dunbar's number (~150; Dunbar, 1993) represents a cognitive carrying capacity for meaningful social relationships, derived from neocortical size constraints in primates. The early church pattern of house churches (Acts 2:46: "And day by day, attending the temple together and breaking bread in their homes"; NA28: καθ' ἡμέραν τε προσκαρτεροῦντες ὁμοθυμαδὸν ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ καὶ κλῶντες κατ' οἶκον ἄρτον) and the modern cell-church model both reflect carrying capacity constraints.

When a community exceeds $K$, it must either fragment (cell division) or lose relational density. The species-area relationship ($S = cA^z$) predicts that larger communities support more gift diversity but at the cost of reduced per-capita interaction—precisely the trade-off observed in megachurch structures.

### 3.7 Mutualism/Parasitism and Ministry Partnerships/Exploitative Leadership

**Correspondence 7** maps the ecological mutualism-parasitism continuum onto the theological distinction between servant leadership and exploitative leadership.

Mutualistic relationships benefit both organisms (e.g., clownfish and anemone). Ministry partnerships that benefit both parties and the wider community are exemplified by Priscilla and Aquila with Paul (Acts 18:2–3, 18–26). Parasitic relationships benefit one organism at the other's expense. Exploitative leadership benefits the leader at the congregation's expense, exemplified by Diotrephes in 3 John 9–10: "Diotrephes, who likes to put himself first" (NA28: ὁ φιλοπρωτεύων αὐτῶν Διοτρεφής).

The Ezekiel 34 shepherds "who feed themselves" (LXX: οἱ ποιμένες Ἰσραήλ, μὴ βόσκουσιν ἑαυτούς;) represent the parasitic end of this continuum. The distinction between mutualism and parasitism in ecology maps directly onto the distinction between servant leadership (διακονία) and exploitative leadership (πλεονεξία) in ecclesiology.

### 3.8 Nutrient Cycling and Teaching/Discipleship Cycles

**Correspondence 8** identifies the parallel between nutrient cycling in ecosystems and teaching/discipleship cycles in churches. In ecosystems, nutrients cycle through producers, consumers, and decomposers; dead matter is broken down and returned to the soil to nourish new growth. In churches, knowledge cycles through teachers, learners, and mature believers who process and return teaching to the community.

Hebrews 5:12 describes the expected cycle: "For though by this time you ought to be teachers, you need someone to teach you again the basic principles of the oracles of God" (NA28: καὶ γὰρ ὀφείλοντες εἶναι διδάσκαλοι διὰ τὸν χρόνον, πάλιν χρείαν ἔχετε τοῦ διδάσκειν ὑμᾶς τινὰ τὰ στοιχεῖα τῆς ἀρχῆς τῶν λογίων τοῦ θεοῦ). Receivers of teaching should become providers of teaching, completing the nutrient cycle.

Communities where teaching flows unidirectionally (pastor → congregation, never recycled) suffer the equivalent of nutrient depletion. This correspondence is acknowledged as partial: nutrient cycling in ecosystems involves decomposition of dead matter, whereas discipleship cycles do not obviously involve an analogous process. The parallel is in the cycling structure (output becomes input), not in all features of the process.

### 3.9 Edge Effects (Ecotone) and Missional Boundary

**Correspondence 9** maps the ecological concept of ecotones—boundary zones where two ecosystems meet (forest/grassland, land/water)—onto the church-culture boundary where believers interact with non-believers.

Ecotones exhibit the highest biodiversity because organisms from both adjacent ecosystems are present, plus edge specialists. The church-culture boundary is predicted to be the zone of highest "spiritual biodiversity"—the most diverse encounters, the most conversion, the most cultural innovation. This predicts that the most vigorous church growth occurs at the edge, not at the center—consistent with missiology's emphasis on boundary engagement and church history's pattern of vitality at cultural margins.

This correspondence is acknowledged as the weakest of the ten. In ecology, edge effects can be negative (habitat fragmentation, edge predation). The mapping assumes the positive interpretation of ecotones (higher biodiversity) but acknowledges that not all boundary conditions are beneficial for either ecosystems or churches. Missionary engagement at the church-culture boundary can produce syncretism (a negative edge effect) as easily as conversion (a positive edge effect). This correspondence is suggestive rather than robust.

### 3.10 Resilience and Spiritual Health

**Correspondence 10** establishes the parallel between ecological resilience—the capacity to absorb disturbance and maintain essential function—and spiritual health in churches.

Ecological resilience is a function of three factors: (a) diversity (more diverse systems have more functional redundancy), (b) connectivity (well-connected systems redistribute resources), and (c) adaptive capacity (the ability to reorganize rather than collapse). Spiritually healthy churches absorb crisis (leadership transition, scandal, cultural pressure, persecution) and maintain essential function through the same three factors.

A monoculture church (one leader, one gift, one demographic) is fragile for exactly the same structural reasons as a monoculture farm: both lack the functional redundancy necessary to absorb perturbation. The diversity-resilience relationship ($R \propto H'$ and $R \propto H'_g$) provides the mathematical foundation for this correspondence.

## 4. Tests and Predictions

### 4.1 Swap Test

To assess whether the mapping is specific to ecology-ecclesiology or generic to all systems sciences, a swap test was conducted. Economics shares some features (market diversity, monopoly fragility, creative destruction) but lacks the organic growth/maturation structure (ecological succession) and the specific resilience-through-diversity mechanism (economic systems can be resilient through efficiency rather than diversity—the Toyota production model versus the Amazon rainforest model). Network theory shares the connectivity-resilience relationship but lacks the biodiversity-specific dynamics (trophic levels, nutrient cycling, succession).

The mapping is specific to ecology because Paul's own metaphor is organic (body, not machine; growth, not construction). The ecclesiology under examination is explicitly biological in its self-description. **Swap test result: PASSED.**

### 4.2 Domain A Predictions (Ecology)

The following predictions are empirically confirmed within ecological science:

1. **Monocultures are fragile.** Confirmed across agriculture (Irish potato famine, *Phytophthora infestans*), forestry (mountain pine beetle infestations in monoculture plantations), and marine biology (coral reef collapse following key species loss).

2. **Keystone species removal causes cascade failure.** Confirmed: wolf removal in Yellowstone (Ripple & Beschta, 2012), sea otter removal in Pacific kelp forests (Estes & Palmisano, 1974), large predator removal in African savannas.

3. **Invasive species displace native species.** Confirmed across thousands of documented cases worldwide (Global Invasive Species Database).

4. **Succession follows predictable stages.** Confirmed: primary and secondary succession patterns are well-documented and predictable across biomes (Clements, 1916; Connell & Slatyer, 1977).

5. **Larger areas support more species.** The species-area relationship is confirmed across thousands of studies (Lomolino, 2000).

6. **Edge habitats have higher biodiversity.** The ecotone effect is well-documented, though the mechanism is debated (see §3.9 for honest weakness).

### 4.3 Domain B Predictions (Theology)

The following predictions are empirically testable through ecclesiological research:

1. **Gift-monoculture churches should be fragile.** Churches where one gift dominates (e.g., only teaching, no pastoral care, no mercy ministry, no administration) should be more vulnerable to crisis. Conversely, churches with diverse, functioning gifts should show greater resilience. Testable through longitudinal studies of church survival rates correlated with gift diversity.

2. **Removal of keystone leaders should trigger cascade effects.** When a founding pastor leaves or a key leader falls, the effects should propagate through dependency layers, not remain localized. This is overwhelmingly confirmed by pastoral experience: founding-pastor transition is the most dangerous moment in a church's life. Churches that survive it do so by having distributed leadership (functional redundancy—multiple species filling related niches).

3. **Heresy should function like an invasive species: fast-growing, resource-consuming, displacing native teaching.** This predicts that heresies will initially grow faster than orthodox teaching (invasive species advantage) because they are not constrained by the ecological checks that limit native species. Church history confirms: every major heresy (Arianism, Pelagianism, Gnosticism) had a rapid growth phase before containment.

4. **Church plants should follow a predictable maturation sequence.** Early-stage churches should have low diversity, high dependence on pioneer leaders, and high vulnerability. Mature churches should have high diversity, distributed leadership, and high resilience. This matches church-planting literature and pastoral observation.

5. **There should be an optimal church size range for relational health.** Above carrying capacity (~150 for primary relationships), the community must either subdivide (cell model) or accept reduced relational density (megachurch model). This predicts a tension between size and relational depth that churches at all sizes navigate—confirmed by the consistent pattern of cell groups, small groups, and house churches within larger structures.

6. **The most vigorous evangelism and conversion should occur at the church-culture boundary (edge effect), not in the interior.** Churches that are entirely insulated from culture (no ecotone) should show less growth. Churches with active boundary engagement should show more. Missiological data generally supports this.

### 4.4 Bidirectional Implications

**Ecology → Theology:** Ecological principles constrain which ecclesiological models are structurally viable. A church model that suppresses gift diversity (only clergy minister, laity passive) violates the diversity-resilience principle and predicts fragility. A model that concentrates all function in one leader (no distributed leadership) violates the keystone-cascade principle and predicts catastrophic failure upon leader removal. Ecology gives ecclesiology a structural reason for gift diversity beyond mere politeness or ethical exhortation.

**Theology → Ecology:** The Pauline body metaphor's emphasis on mutual interdependence ("the eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of you'"; 1 Cor 12:21, NA28: οὐ δύναται δὲ ὁ ὀφθαλμὸς εἰπεῖν τῇ χειρί· χρείαν σου οὐκ ἔχω) predicts that ecological models should show strong negative effects from species removal even when the removed species seems minor. This is confirmed by the frequent discovery of "unexpected" keystone species—organisms whose importance was not recognized until their removal triggered cascades (e.g., Paine, 1969).

### 4.5 Falsification Criteria

The isomorphism is falsifiable through the following empirical tests:

1. **Break diversity-resilience:** Demonstrate that monoculture ecosystems (or churches) are equally or more resilient than diverse ones. If diversity is not load-bearing for resilience in either domain, the core structural parallel fails.

2. **Break the cascade structure:** Demonstrate that removing keystone species (or keystone leaders) produces only localized effects with no downstream cascade. If removal effects do not propagate through dependency networks, the trophic cascade mapping fails.

3. **Break the succession pattern:** Demonstrate that church development does NOT follow a predictable maturation sequence—that mature churches and new plants have the same structural characteristics. If maturation is not a developmental process, the succession mapping fails.

4. **Break the carrying capacity constraint:** Demonstrate that communities of any size maintain equal relational density—that Dunbar's number (or any cognitive constraint on relationship maintenance) does not apply. If community size has no structural consequences for relational function, the carrying capacity mapping fails.

5. **Break the invasive species parallel:** Demonstrate that heretical movements do NOT outcompete orthodox teaching for attention/adherents in the short term—that their growth dynamics are identical to orthodox movements. If heresy has no "invasive advantage," the invasive species mapping weakens significantly.

## 5. Honest Weaknesses

### 5.1 Edge Effect Correspondence (Correspondence 9)

The edge effect prediction is the weakest of the ten correspondences. In ecology, edge effects can be negative (habitat fragmentation, edge predation, nest parasitism). The mapping assumes the positive interpretation of ecotones (higher biodiversity) but acknowledges that not all boundary conditions are beneficial for either ecosystems or churches. Missionary engagement at the church-culture boundary can produce syncretism (a negative edge effect) as easily as conversion (a positive edge effect). This correspondence is suggestive rather than robust.

### 5.2 Nutrient Cycling Correspondence (Correspondence 8)

The nutrient cycling parallel is also somewhat weak. Nutrient cycling in ecosystems involves decomposition of dead matter; discipleship cycles do not obviously involve an analogous decomposition process. The parallel is in the cycling structure (output becomes input), not in all features of the process. This is noted as a partial correspondence.

### 5.3 Quantitative Precision

This mapping is not as quantitatively precise as the epidemiology mapping (ISO-027). Ecological systems are notoriously complex and multi-causal; the mapping is strongest at the qualitative-structural level. The specific scaling exponent $z$ in the gift-size relationship ($G = cN^z$) may differ from the ecological species-area exponent ($z \approx 0.25$), and empirical determination of $z$ for ecclesial communities remains a task for future research.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper has presented a formal structural isomorphism between ecological community dynamics and Pauline ecclesiology, identifying ten correspondence points that span diversity metrics, cascade dynamics, succession patterns, carrying capacity constraints, relational continua, cycling processes, boundary effects, and resilience measures. The isomorphism operates at the level of community topology rather than material substrate, establishing that the Apostle Paul's body metaphor describes structural requirements for community function that parallel formal ecological principles.

The mapping yields testable predictions for both domains and carries bidirectional implications: ecological principles constrain viable ecclesiological models, while Pauline interdependence predictions anticipate ecological discoveries regarding keystone species effects. Eight of ten correspondences are assessed as robust, with two acknowledged as suggestive rather than definitive.

Future research should focus on empirical determination of the gift-size scaling exponent $z$ for ecclesial communities, longitudinal studies of church survival rates correlated with gift diversity, and quantitative analysis of heresy growth dynamics relative to orthodox teaching propagation.
