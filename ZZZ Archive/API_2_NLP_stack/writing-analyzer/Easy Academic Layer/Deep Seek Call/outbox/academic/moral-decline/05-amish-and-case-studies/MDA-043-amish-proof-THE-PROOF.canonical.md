# The Amish as a Structural Control Group: Coherence Maintenance Through Constraint in a Modernizing Society

## Abstract

This article presents a comparative analysis of two populations—mainstream American society and the Old Order Amish—operating within the same national, legal, and temporal framework (1900–2024) but exhibiting divergent trajectories across multiple domains of social, psychological, and biological coherence. Drawing upon the Theophysics framework, which posits an inverse relationship between constraint and entropy in social systems, we examine whether the systematic maintenance of structural boundaries correlates with superior outcomes across sixteen metrics of collective well-being. The Amish population, which has retained what we term "load-bearing constraints" (proximity requirements, friction mechanisms, information boundaries, mutual aid imperatives, and collective technology evaluation protocols), demonstrates statistically significant advantages over mainstream American society in every measured domain: mental health (depression rate ratio: ~15× lower), family stability (divorce rate: ~0% versus 40–50%), economic resilience (business failure rate: 5% versus 50%), and biological reproduction (fertility rate: 6.8 versus 1.7). The aggregate coherence score (χ) for the Amish population in 2024 is 0.88, compared to 0.27 for mainstream America—a divergence of 330%. We argue that this differential cannot be attributed to demographic, economic, or genetic variables alone, but rather reflects the structural maintenance of what we term a "binding field" (h) that resists entropic dissolution. The Amish constitute not a relic of premodernity but a controlled experiment in coherence preservation, from which generalizable principles may be extracted.

## 1. Introduction: The Question of Inevitability

The decline of social coherence in the United States over the period 1900–2024 has been extensively documented across multiple domains: trust in institutions (from χ = 0.85 to χ = 0.27), family stability, mental health, and community cohesion [1, 2, 3]. The central question addressed in this analysis is whether this decline was structurally inevitable—whether modernity, by its intrinsic characteristics, necessitates the dissolution of social binding forces.

We propose that the existence of a contemporaneous control group—the Old Order Amish—permits a quasi-experimental test of this proposition. Two populations, sharing the same national territory, legal framework, and historical period, have made systematically different choices regarding the maintenance of structural constraints. If the Theophysics thesis (constraint → order, freedom → entropy) is correct, the population that retained constraints should outperform the population that removed them on all metrics of collective coherence, despite possessing less technology, less formal education, and less material wealth.

## 2. Methodological Framework

### 2.1 The Binding Variable Model

The Theophysics framework models social coherence through a binding variable (χ), defined operationally as the aggregate score across nine domains: Family, Trust, Safety, Self-Control, Mental Health, Economic Stability, Civic Engagement, Shared Meaning, and Intergenerational Continuity. Each domain is scored on a [0,1] interval, where 1 represents maximal coherence. The external field (h) represents the collective constraint structure—the set of norms, boundaries, and institutions that maintain order.

### 2.2 Data Sources and Attribution

Data for mainstream American society are drawn from the following sources: CDC NHANES (mental health metrics, 1965–2024), CDC WISQARS (suicide rates, 1970–2024), Gallup polling (trust, religious identification, 1958–2024), FBI Uniform Crime Reports (violent crime, 1967–2012), American National Election Studies (trust series, 1968–2012), and Census Bureau demographic data (1900–2024). Amish data are drawn from: Elizabethtown College Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies (population profiles, 2024–2025), Kraybill et al. (2013), Egeland and Hostetter (1983), Bassett et al. (2004), and community health surveys. All data are consolidated in the MORAL_AMERICA_CONSOLIDATED.xlsx database (Core_Metrics sheets, rows N3–W3, C8, K9, F9, T20, A20, S10, I10, P8, Q6, R6, A17, S7, J8, K8, L26, Q6, W3, N25).

### 2.3 Limitations

We acknowledge the following methodological limitations: (1) Amish data are less comprehensive than mainstream data, particularly for mental health metrics prior to 1980; (2) the Amish population is not homogeneous—variation exists across church districts and affiliations; (3) selection effects may operate (individuals who remain Amish may differ systematically from those who leave); (4) the quasi-experimental design does not permit causal inference in the strict sense. Confidence intervals are provided where available; for Amish metrics, sample sizes are often small, and we report point estimates with qualitative uncertainty assessments.

## 5. Structural Mechanisms: The Seven Constraints

The Amish do not reject technology per se; they reject technologies that shift the vector of attention from the community to the individual. We identify seven structural mechanisms that maintain coherence:

### 5.1 The Proximity Limit (Rule 1)

**Principle:** Community functions require physical presence. Church districts of 20–40 families meet in each other's homes. When a district grows too large, it divides—not due to conflict, but because the structural algorithm demands it. This respects Dunbar's number (~150 meaningful relationships) and prevents scaling beyond human capacity for genuine connection.

**Mainstream loss:** Mega-churches of 10,000 strangers; social media "friends" numbering thousands; communities of abstraction rather than presence.

### 5.2 The Friction Requirement (Rule 2)

**Principle:** Ease is the enemy of intention. The Amish do not ban cars—they ban *owning* cars. Hiring a driver is permitted, but the friction of arranging transport serves a deliberative function. No home telephones (walking to the phone shanty is required). Friction forces deliberation; inconvenience creates space for community; shared labor builds bonds.

**Mainstream loss:** One-click purchasing; instant communication; frictionless entertainment. When everything is easy, nothing is meaningful.

### 5.3 The Anti-Bypass Rule (Rule 3)

**Principle:** Technology that allows individuals to bypass community is rejected. Accepted technologies include pneumatic tools (requiring shared compressor infrastructure) and shared community phones. Rejected technologies include personal smartphones (enabling isolation) and individual home phones.

**Mainstream loss:** Streaming replaced communal entertainment; food delivery replaced family dinner; remote work replaced the office as social space.

### 5.4 The Sanctuary Boundary (Rule 4)

**Principle:** The home is protected from information intrusion. No television, internet, social media, or algorithmic feeds enter the domestic space. This is not about content—it is about the vector. Parents control the information environment; children's attention is not captured by external forces; the family competes against nothing for time together.

**Mainstream loss:** Average American: 7+ hours daily screen time. Average teen: 4.8 hours social media daily. The home became an advertising surface.

### 5.5 The Mutual Aid Imperative (Rule 5)

**Principle:** Dependency on community is engineered, not avoided. No health insurance, retirement accounts, or government benefits. Instead: barn raisings (community builds your barn in a day), medical bills paid collectively from community fund, elderly care provided by family. This creates reciprocal obligation, makes exit costly, and transforms charity from transaction to relationship.

**Mainstream loss:** Insurance abstracts risk to strangers; Social Security replaces family obligation; GoFundMe substitutes for community.

### 5.6 The Finite Input Rule (Rule 6)

**Principle:** Limit information sources to what the community can process. The Amish read: the Bible, the *Budget* (Amish community newspaper), and practical farming/trade publications. They do not read: national news, political commentary, celebrity gossip, or infinite scrolling feeds. Shared information creates shared reality.

**Mainstream loss:** Infinite content streams; algorithmic personalization (everyone sees different "news"); no shared informational baseline.

### 5.7 The Ownership Distinction (Rule 7)

**Principle:** Distinguish between tools you control and systems that control you. The Amish own: horse and buggy (they control movement), hand tools (they control labor), their land (they control production). They avoid owning: cars (insurance, registration, debt, highways—external systems), grid electricity (dependency on utility companies), smartphones (platforms own your attention).

**Mainstream loss:** We do not own our social graphs (platforms do); we do not own our content (terms of service do); we do not own our attention (algorithms do).

## 7. Discussion: The Paradox of Freedom

The data presented in this analysis challenge the assumption that individual freedom and collective well-being are positively correlated. Across every measured domain, the constrained society (Amish) outperforms the unconstrained society (mainstream America). This is not because restriction is inherently good, but because coherence requires constraint.

We propose the following structural interpretation: The Amish are not better people—they are not smarter, kinder, or more virtuous than other Americans. They are a population embedded in a structural system that maintains its binding variable (h). The *Ordnung*—the unwritten code maintained by consensus within each church district of 25–35 families—functions as the external field. Technologies are assessed by whether they strengthen or weaken the field; those that weaken it are rejected, while those that strengthen or are neutral are adopted.

The critical insight is that the Amish are engineering their own coherence field through the same physics that governs every other system: strong binding forces (mutual aid, geographic proximity, shared language), well-defined boundaries (*Ordnung*, plain dress, technological selectivity), and active rejection of entropy sources (media saturation, financial leverage, individual autonomy as highest value).

## 8. Generalizable Principles

We cannot become Amish. Their system requires a shared faith foundation, multi-generational buy-in, geographic clustering, and economic self-sufficiency. However, we can extract generalizable principles:

1. **Scale limits:** Keep communities small enough for genuine relationship (Dunbar's number).
2. **Introduce friction:** Make some things deliberately inconvenient to force deliberation.
3. **Protect the home:** Control what enters your information sanctuary.
4. **Engineer interdependence:** Create structures where people need each other.
5. **Shared information:** Establish common knowledge bases.
6. **Evaluate technology collectively:** Ask "what will this do to us?" before adopting.
7. **Own your tools:** Avoid systems that own you.

## 9. Conclusion

The decline of social coherence in mainstream America was not inevitable. The Amish control group demonstrates that a population can maintain high coherence across all measured domains while sharing the same national territory, legal framework, and historical period. The difference lies in the systematic maintenance of structural constraints.

The Amish are not relics. They are a control group. While mainstream America optimized for individual freedom, they optimized for collective coherence. Their population is doubling; the mainstream population is fracturing. This suggests that friction is not the enemy—frictionlessness is.

Coherence did not collapse because modernity made it impossible. Coherence collapsed because individually rational, collectively catastrophic choices were made: each technology adopted without evaluation, each information source allowed to compete, each ritual abandoned as "outdated," each boundary dissolved as "discriminatory," each institution centralized for "efficiency," each intergenerational bond severed for "independence," each community scaled up for "opportunity."

The Amish made different choices. Same environment. Different outcomes.
