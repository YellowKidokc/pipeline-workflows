## Abstract

This paper establishes the formal physical foundation—grounded in Landau theory, universality classes, and critical exponents—upon which subsequent analysis of social systems is constructed. We demonstrate that social systems possess a measurable order parameter (χ) that undergoes phase transitions identical in mathematical form to those documented in physical systems, specifically the power-law scaling χ ∝ |P − Pc|^β observed in ferromagnets, superconductors, and liquid-gas transitions. The falsification criterion is specified: if χ values are found to be uncorrelated across social domains under identical measurement conditions, the substrate-independence claim is disconfirmed.

**Assumed Priors:** (1) Phase transition mathematics exhibits substrate-independence, applying wherever a system possesses an order parameter, a control parameter, and a measurable degree of ordering (Landau, 1937; Wilson, 1971); (2) coherence in social systems is operationalizable through behavioral and institutional proxies; (3) the 1900–1950 period constitutes a defensible ordered-phase baseline.

**Thesis:** Any system satisfying the conditions of (i) a defined order parameter, (ii) a tunable control parameter, and (iii) measurable coherence in the ordered phase will exhibit phase-transition dynamics—including power-law scaling of χ and divergent correlation length—at the critical threshold Pc.

**Supporting Framework:** Landau mean-field theory (1937), BCS theory of superconductivity (Bardeen, Cooper, & Schrieffer, 1957), Ising model universality class (Onsager, 1944). Empirical cross-domain social data are presented in Page 4 (MDA-041).

## 1. Operational Definition of Coherence in Physical Systems

In physics, **coherence** denotes the degree of correlation and phase alignment among the components of a system. A coherent system is one in which components exhibit correlated behavior—acting in concert rather than as statistically independent entities. This is an operationally defined, quantitatively measurable property, not a metaphor.

In a laser, photons are coherent when they share identical phase and frequency. In a superconductor, electrons are coherent when they form Cooper pairs. In a ferromagnet, atomic spins are coherent when they align along a common axis. In each case, coherence is defined through a specific observable that vanishes in the disordered phase and becomes non-zero in the ordered phase.

---

## 2. The Order Parameter (χ)

Physicists operationalize coherence through an **order parameter** (Landau, 1937): a scalar or tensor quantity that is identically **zero** in the disordered (high-entropy, high-symmetry) phase, **non-zero** in the ordered (low-entropy, broken-symmetry) phase, and undergoes a **non-analytic change** at the phase transition.

### Table 1: Order Parameters Across Physical Systems

| System | Order Parameter (χ) | Physical Interpretation |
|--------|---------------------|------------------------|
| Ferromagnet | Net magnetization (M) | Alignment of atomic spins |
| Superconductor | Cooper pair density (ψ) | Macroscopic quantum coherence |
| Crystal | Crystallographic order parameter (Bragg peak intensity) | Periodic atomic arrangement |
| Liquid-gas | Density difference (ρ_liquid − ρ_gas) | Phase separation |

*Source: Landau (1937); Wilson (1971).*

## 3. Phase Transition Dynamics

When the control parameter (temperature T in thermal systems, or an analogous intensive variable in social systems) crosses the **critical threshold** Tc, the order parameter undergoes its characteristic transition:

### Phase Transition Equation

\[
\chi \propto |T - T_c|^\beta \quad \text{for } T < T_c \text{ (ordered phase)}
\]

\[
\chi \to 0 \quad \text{for } T > T_c \text{ (disordered phase)}
\]

**Variable Definitions:**
- \(T\): Control parameter (temperature in thermal systems; analogous intensive variable in social systems)
- \(T_c\): Critical threshold at which the phase transition occurs
- \(\beta\): Critical exponent, typically in the range 0.3–0.5, determined by the universality class

**Dimensional Analysis:** The order parameter χ is dimensionless when normalized to its maximum value; the control parameter T has dimensions of energy (in thermal systems) or is dimensionless when expressed as a reduced variable \((T - T_c)/T_c\).

The transition is discontinuous (first-order) or exhibits power-law divergence (second-order/continuous), not gradual. Critically, the mathematical form is **universal across substrates**—the same power-law scaling governs ferromagnetism, superconductivity, and (this paper argues) civilizational coherence. The transition is **predictable** once Pc is determined.

## 4. A Concrete Example: The Superconductor

### Below Tc — Ordered Phase
- Electrons form Cooper pairs via phonon-mediated attraction (Bardeen, Cooper, & Schrieffer, 1957)
- Electrical resistance equals zero
- The system exhibits quantum coherence at macroscopic scale
- Order parameter ψ ≠ 0 (Cooper pair density)

### Above Tc — Disordered Phase
- Cooper pairs break apart due to thermal fluctuations
- Resistance returns
- Coherence is lost
- Order parameter ψ = 0

The mathematics does not depend on the substrate; it only tracks the order parameter.

## 5. Substrate Independence and Universality

Phase transition mathematics applies to **any system** satisfying three structural conditions:

1. **Components capable of ordered or disordered configurations**—the system must possess degrees of freedom that can adopt either correlated or uncorrelated states
2. **A tunable control parameter governing the ordered-disordered boundary**—an intensive variable that can be adjusted to cross the critical threshold
3. **A measurable order parameter that is non-zero in the ordered phase**—an observable that distinguishes the two phases

This yields the operative question: Do social systems possess a measurable order parameter? If so, do they exhibit phase-transition dynamics at empirically identifiable critical thresholds?

---

## 6. Methodological Context

The isomorphism between physical and social phase transitions was identified through structural comparison of order parameter definitions, control parameter identification, and critical exponent scaling across domains. The Landau mean-field framework (1937) provides the theoretical foundation, while the Ising model universality class (Onsager, 1944) establishes the mathematical form of the critical exponents. The BCS theory (Bardeen, Cooper, & Schrieffer, 1957) demonstrates that microscopic interactions can produce macroscopic coherence—a principle that extends to social systems through analogous coupling mechanisms.
