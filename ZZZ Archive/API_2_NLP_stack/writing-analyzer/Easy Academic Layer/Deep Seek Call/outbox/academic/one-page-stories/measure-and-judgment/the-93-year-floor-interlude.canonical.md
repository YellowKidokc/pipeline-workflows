## 3. The Mortality Curve: Empirical Observations

### 3.1 Raw Data Characteristics

The empirical mortality data spanning 93 years reveal a floor that exhibits asymptotic behavior rather than linear decay. This observation is mathematically significant: a linear floor would indicate stochastic extinction, whereas an asymptotic floor suggests a maintained lower bound.

Let \( M(t) \) represent the mortality rate as a function of time \( t \). The observed behavior satisfies:

\[
\lim_{t \to \infty} M(t) = M_0 > 0
\]

where \( M_0 \) is the asymptotic floor value. The inequality \( M_0 > 0 \) distinguishes this case from models predicting eventual extinction (\( M_0 = 0 \)).

### 3.2 Exponential Versus Linear Dynamics

The critical diagnostic feature is the exponential character of the curve's approach to the floor. For a linear model, one would expect:

\[
M(t) \approx M_0 + \alpha t^{-1} \quad \text{(linear decay)}
\]

However, the observed data conform to an exponential approach:

\[
M(t) \approx M_0 + \beta e^{-\gamma t} \quad \text{(exponential decay)}
\]

where \( \beta \) is the initial deviation amplitude and \( \gamma \) is the decay constant (with dimensions of inverse time). The exponential form indicates a non-stochastic constraint mechanism, as stochastic processes typically produce power-law or linear asymptotic approaches.
