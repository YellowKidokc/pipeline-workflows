## 2. Proof 1: Entropy and Moral Alignment

### 2.1 Thermodynamic Formulation

The Second Law of Thermodynamics establishes that the entropy \( S \) of an isolated system satisfies:

\[
\frac{dS}{dt} \geq 0
\]

where \( S \) is the Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy defined as \( S = -k_B \sum_i p_i \ln p_i \), with \( k_B \) representing the Boltzmann constant (1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J·K⁻¹) and \( p_i \) the probability of the system occupying microstate \( i \).

### 2.2 Moral Interpretation

We define moral action \( M \) as any intervention that decreases local entropy through the expenditure of free energy \( \Delta F \). The thermodynamic cost of moral action is given by:

\[
\Delta S_{\text{local}} = -\frac{\Delta F}{T} < 0
\]

where \( T \) is the system temperature. Conversely, immoral action (or inaction) corresponds to the spontaneous increase of entropy:

\[
\Delta S_{\text{local}} \geq 0 \quad \text{(default decay)}

\]

### 2.3 Formal Proof

**Theorem 1.1:** Virtue requires thermodynamic work; vice is thermodynamically spontaneous.

*Proof:* Consider a system initially in equilibrium with entropy \( S_0 \). A moral intervention that reduces entropy to \( S_1 < S_0 \) requires work input \( W \) satisfying:

\[
W \geq T(S_0 - S_1) > 0
\]

No such work is required for entropy increase. Therefore, moral action is thermodynamically expensive, while immoral action is thermodynamically free. ∎

**Implication:** The Second Law of Thermodynamics establishes an objective asymmetry between moral and immoral action, rendering morality a physical law rather than a subjective preference.
