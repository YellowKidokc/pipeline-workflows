# Theophysics Isomorphism Registry: A Formal Analysis of Structural Correspondences Between Physical and Theological Domains

## Abstract

This article presents a systematic investigation of structural isomorphisms between formal physical theories and Christian theological constructs. Thirty-six candidate correspondences are examined through a rigorous four-test protocol assessing bidirectional mapping, topological conservation, metric correspondence, and falsifiability. The analysis yields twenty-seven tested correspondences with confidence ratings ranging from HIGH to LOW, eight candidate correspondences requiring further investigation, and one undetermined case. The framework operates under explicit epistemological constraints: no claim is made that theological constructs reduce to physical equations, that the existence of God is thereby proven, or that either domain is exhausted by the mapping. Rather, the analysis identifies shared topological structures—patterns of relation, operation, and constraint—that persist across domains while respecting ontological distinctions. Results indicate that the strongest correspondences cluster around information-theoretic, thermodynamic, and dynamical systems structures, with weaker correspondences in areas requiring domain-specific interpretive frameworks.

---

## 1. Introduction

The relationship between physical theory and theological doctrine has historically oscillated between conflict, independence, and dialogue (Barbour, 1997). The present work adopts a structural isomorphism approach: rather than asserting causal or reductive relationships, we identify formal patterns that appear in both domains independently, then test whether those patterns exhibit genuine structural correspondence or merely superficial analogy.

The methodology employed is the four-test protocol (HYP-ISO-001), which evaluates each candidate isomorphism against the following criteria:

1. **Swap Test**: Can the domain-specific roles be exchanged without producing incoherence? (Bidirectional mapping)
2. **Topological Test**: Does the structural relationship persist under transformation of domain-specific content? (Structural invariance)
3. **Metric Test**: Can quantitative measures be meaningfully compared across domains? (Quantitative correspondence)
4. **Falsification Test**: What empirical or logical conditions would break the claimed isomorphism? (Falsifiability)

Confidence ratings are assigned as follows: HIGH (all four tests passed with no significant strains), MEDIUM (three tests passed with acknowledged strains), LOW (two or fewer tests passed, or significant domain-specific assumptions required), and UNDETERMINED (insufficient data for assessment).

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Patterns in Confidence Distribution

The analysis reveals a clear pattern: the highest-confidence isomorphisms cluster around information-theoretic, thermodynamic, and dynamical systems structures. This is not accidental. These domains share formal features—conservation laws, irreversibility, threshold phenomena, and operator algebras—that admit precise mathematical formulation and cross-domain mapping.

The lowest-confidence isomorphisms share a different pattern: they involve domain-specific interpretive frameworks (theological anthropology, ecclesiology, soteriology) where the mapping depends on particular theological commitments (e.g., Reformed vs. Catholic anthropology for ISO-012). These isomorphisms are not necessarily false, but their truth is conditional on theological premises that are themselves contested.

### 6.2 The Circularity Problem

A significant methodological concern is the circularity problem identified in several isomorphisms (particularly ISO-013, ISO-019, ISO-024). When theological constructs are used to derive the physical framework that then "confirms" those same theological constructs, the epistemic value of the isomorphism is compromised.

The framework acknowledges this problem explicitly. The strongest defense against circularity is the independence of the impossibility theorems in ISO-002, which were derived without theological input and whose inversion produces a specification that matches Pauline grace point-for-point. This case provides a template for what a non-circular isomorphism looks like.

### 6.3 The Ontological Status of Isomorphisms

The framework does not claim that theological constructs reduce to physical equations, or that physical laws have theological content. Rather, the claim is that both domains exhibit shared topological structures—patterns of relation, operation, and constraint that persist across domains while respecting ontological distinctions.

This is consistent with what Whewell (1840) called "consilience of inductions": when independently derived conclusions converge, the convergence itself constitutes evidence for the truth-tracking capacity of both domains. The present work extends this principle to cross-domain reasoning, with the caveat that the independence of the domains must be carefully maintained.

### 6.4 Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged:

1. **Selection bias**: The isomorphisms were identified by a researcher with theological commitments, which may influence which correspondences are noticed and how they are evaluated.

2. **Domain asymmetry**: The physical side of each isomorphism is generally better formalized than the theological side, which may create an illusion of precision.

3. **Falsification asymmetry**: Physical predictions are generally more testable than theological predictions, which may affect confidence ratings.

4. **Denominational variance**: Several isomorphisms depend on particular theological traditions (e.g., Reformed soteriology for ISO-012), limiting their universal applicability.

Future work should include:
- Independent derivation of boundary conditions by researchers without theological commitments
- Quantitative testing of predictions where feasible (e.g., E6.2 for ISO-011)
- Engagement with scholars of other religious traditions for ISO-024
- Formalization of the Lowe Coherence Lagrangian (ISO-022)
